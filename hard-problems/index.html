Difficulty is often spoken of as though it belonged to a problem in the way mass belongs to an object. A theorem is hard. A landscape is rugged. A system is complex. Yet the same multiplication is routine for a calculator and difficult for a person without paper. The same maze is transparent with a map and baffling without one. The same dynamical system may be controllable through one set of effectors and unreachable through another.

The task has not changed in these comparisons. The relation to it has.

That observation begins the argument, but it does not complete it. It does not mean that difficulty is imaginary, that reality changes when it is described differently, or that every obstruction is merely ignorance in disguise. A locked door does not open under a more elegant description. A false theorem does not become true for a weak prover. A path through a valley does not become painless when its cost is known. The world supplies structure and resistance. What varies is the way an agent encounters them.

The account developed here is therefore relational and realist at once. It is relational because a complete statement of difficulty names a task, an agent, a representation, an interface, and a resource regime. It is realist because the success or failure of that relation is constrained by facts the agent does not choose. Representations can reveal structure, conceal it, compress it, or misstate it. They cannot make every description equally good.

Part I develops this position without assuming a single mathematical model of all problems. Part II will give several exact realizations drawn from information theory, control, automata, graph theory, logic, and complexity theory. The order matters. The mathematics will sharpen distinctions already made. It will not legislate what the word difficulty must mean in every domain.

Part IThe Philosophy

Chapter 1Difficulty belongs to a relation

A problem is not simply an object waiting to be inspected. It is something to be done. A theorem may be proved, a question answered, a target reached, a signal decoded, or a trajectory kept within a set. Each description introduces a success condition. It also introduces a space of admissible attempts, even when that space remains implicit.

An agent meets the task through a particular coupling. Some features of the task are available as observations. Some transformations are available as actions. The agent represents what is happening in some vocabulary, and it can spend only so much time, memory, communication, precision, or physical effort. Difficulty belongs to this whole encounter.

The claim is stronger than the harmless remark that different people find different things difficult. It says that an attribution of difficulty is incomplete until the relevant relation has been specified. Positional multiplication across growing input widths is not simply hard or easy. It may be easy for a device with growing writable storage, impossible for a one-pass finite-state family under a fixed state budget, and manageable for a person once paper supplies external memory. Any one fixed width, by contrast, can be handled by a sufficiently large finite machine. A maze may be easy as an offline shortest-path problem and hard for a searcher who learns one corridor at a time. The underlying arithmetic or graph remains the same. The available coupling does not.

This relational claim does not by itself entail subjectivism. Standpoint-relative accounts can be grounded in stable capacities, dispositions, and practices rather than momentary feeling. The present claim concerns constraints that are often objective and measurable. Whether two states produce the same observation can be a mathematical fact. Whether a target lies in the closure of a set of transformations can be a mathematical fact. Whether an algorithm exceeds a space bound can be a mathematical fact. These facts are relational because their statements mention an observation map, a move set, or a machine model. Their dependence on such parameters does not make them arbitrary.

Nor does the account deny intrinsic structure. A graph may contain a narrow cut. An objective may have many local maxima. A proof may require a long chain of dependencies. A language may be undecidable by a stated class of machines. Such properties belong to objects together with declared rules of representation. What does not follow without a further premise is that the property defeats every possible agent, or even every agent of interest.

There are consequently three levels that ordinary talk often collapses. First is the structure of an object under a specification: the graph, objective, language, dynamics, or proof system. Second is the encounter between that structure and a particular interface and resource budget. Third is the class of alternative descriptions or machines under which the hardness claim is meant to remain stable. The first level supports structural facts. The second supports claims about an agent. The third supports a qualified form of objectivity through invariance.

If a complexity bound survives every standard machine model up to polynomial simulation, then its dependence on a model does not make it a matter of taste. If a barrier survives every graph isomorphism preserving the objective and adjacency relation, then changing coordinates does not remove it. Relational properties can be robust across a large comparison class. Their objectivity consists in that robustness, not in the absence of all relations.

The useful unit is therefore neither the isolated problem nor the isolated agent. It is a task as encountered by an agent through a representation, an interface, and a resource envelope. This unit is broad enough to explain why a change of tools can transform difficulty, but strict enough to preserve the difference between altering an encounter and altering the world.

Chapter 2Four modes of difficulty

The word hard covers failures that have little in common. Some arise because the available evidence conflates possibilities. Some arise because the target cannot be reached by the available moves. Some arise because an answer exists but cannot be computed or selected within the available resources. Some arise because the current language cannot state the distinction on which a solution would depend.

These can be called epistemic, practical, computational, and expressive difficulty. They are not offered as an exhaustive taxonomy of every use of the word. Nor are they mutually exclusive compartments. They are four modes that can be stated separately and that place different burdens on an explanation.

Epistemic difficulty occurs when the information available to an agent does not separate possibilities relevant to success. Two states may produce the same entire observation history while requiring incompatible answers. In that case further calculation on the same history cannot recover the missing distinction. The limitation is not that the agent has failed to notice a fact already present in the signal. The signal, as supplied through the interface, does not contain it.

Epistemic difficulty is task-relative. Two states can be physically distinct yet equivalent for a decision when they have the same acceptable-answer set. More generally, exact reconstruction is unnecessary whenever all states left ambiguous by the interface share an acceptable answer. Conversely, a measurement may be extremely precise while remaining useless if it identifies states that require different responses. Resolution is not the number of decimal places. It is the ability to preserve distinctions that matter.

Practical difficulty occurs when success lies outside the closure of the available transformations, or when every route to it violates a constraint. Perfect information does not supply a missing action. A complete map of a locked room does not create an exit. A proof rule that cannot derive a formula does not become stronger when its derivations are catalogued. In dynamic settings there is also a difference between an unreachable target and a reachable target whose every path passes through a forbidden region. Both are practical limitations, but their structures differ.

Computational difficulty occurs when an acceptable answer or route exists and is represented, but computing, selecting, or describing it exceeds the available time, space, queries, communication, or precision. Physical execution cost belongs instead to the practical relation and to \(c_{\mathrm{world}}\) below. This mode appears only clearly when isolated examples are replaced by families of growing size. A single large search proves little. A complexity claim asks how the required resources scale, under a stated encoding and model of computation.

Computational difficulty prevents the theory from collapsing into a doctrine of missing representation. Sometimes the algorithm is known, every input is visible, every needed operation is available, and the computation still does not fit. The obstruction is not a conceptual void. It is a resource bound.

Expressive difficulty occurs when the current vocabulary cannot formulate a distinction, invariant, action, or target that a successor account would need. This is not ignorance about the value of a known variable. It is a limit on which variables or predicates the representation contains. A language may identify two structures because no formula in that language separates them, even though an expanded language can do so immediately.

Expressive difficulty must be handled cautiously. Adding the predicate that names a target obviously makes the target expressible, but that trivial fact does not explain how a useful predicate is discovered. The formal point is comparative: a distinction unavailable in one language can be available in an extension. The inventive production of the extension is a further problem, not a corollary of expressivity theory.

The four modes can occur together. A richer observation channel may reveal the state while leaving the target unreachable. A new action may make the target reachable while planning remains exponential. A new vocabulary may state the right invariant while the proof still requires resources beyond reach. A compressed representation may reduce computational cost while preserving an epistemic ambiguity present in the original data.

This coexistence explains why no single response dissolves difficulty in general. More observation does not create an action. More action does not guarantee an informative signal. A better language does not ensure a cheaper algorithm. More computation does not separate states already identified by the observation interface.

Chapter 3Observation, inference, and misspecification

Observation sometimes means direct access. A symbol is present on an input tape. A sensor reports a quantity. A proof checker receives a term. Nothing is gained by pretending that every such case requires an inner model of the world.

Often, however, the condition relevant to a task is latent. It must be inferred from a history of signals and interventions. In this second case observation is better understood as estimation. The agent maintains an internal state, predicts what signal should arrive, and revises its estimate when prediction and signal differ. Control theory calls such a dynamical estimator an observer. Bayesian filtering, hidden-state models, and many forms of scientific inference share the same broad shape without being identical constructions.

The distinction between direct access and estimation prevents a philosophical model from becoming universal by vocabulary alone. An observer is a precise formal object for a class of problems. It is not a proof that perception as such consists in running a duplicate world internally. What matters here is the narrower result: whenever action depends on a latent state, the quality of an estimate depends both on the information in the signals and on the model used to interpret them.

Two failures follow, and they should not be confused. The first is indistinguishability. Distinct states generate the same law over every observation history available to the agent. No estimator can reliably recover which state obtained because the observation relation does not carry that distinction. The second is misspecification. The real process lies outside the model class used by the estimator. The estimate may then diverge, or it may converge confidently within the wrong model.

Confidence cannot separate these failures. A posterior concentrated inside a misspecified class may be sharply wrong. A stable prediction error may signal unmodeled dynamics rather than random disturbance. Conversely, a broad posterior may reflect honest observational equivalence rather than a defective method. The shape of uncertainty inside a model class is not evidence that the class contains the world.

Derived quantities have a similarly double character. If a statistic is a deterministic function of an existing signal, it cannot distinguish two cases that the signal itself identifies. In that informational sense it adds nothing. Yet it may remove noise, expose regularity, compress a history, or place a calculation within a bounded agent's resources. A statistic can therefore be computationally transformative without being informationally finer than its source. Data processing preserves the information boundary while changing the cost of using what lies inside it.

Intervention changes the boundary. Two systems indistinguishable while left alone may respond differently to the same input. An action can therefore have both practical and epistemic roles: it changes the state and changes what can be learned about the process. Passive observation is only one experiment among the experiments an interface may permit. This is why identifiability must be defined relative to a class of admissible experiments rather than to a single stream of measurements.

Time also belongs to the observation relation. A signal may report a past state rather than the current one. Feedback based on stale information can destabilize a sufficiently aggressive correction rule, but delay alone does not imply oscillation. Stability depends on the dynamics, the gain, the filtering rule, model error, and disturbance. A known fixed delay can sometimes be bridged by prediction. An unknown or changing delay becomes another latent quantity. In either case, the age of evidence and the rate of revision are properties of the same loop.

Finally, uncertainty must be separated from underdetermination. Epistemic uncertainty presumes that one of several possibilities obtains even if the agent cannot tell which. Logical independence is different. If the assumptions do not determine a truth value, there is no hidden answer within those assumptions for a better observer to reveal. The continuum hypothesis is not a latent state obscured by a poor sensor. Treating an undetermined statement as an observation problem confuses extension of a theory with discovery inside it.

Observation is thus neither passive looking nor a universal act of inner simulation. It is a family of relations among states, experiments, signals, models, and time. Epistemic difficulty appears where those relations identify possibilities that the task requires an agent to keep apart.

Chapter 4Terrain, map, search, and traversal

The landscape metaphor gathers several different costs into one picture. A configuration space is imagined as terrain, an objective as height, and a sequence of admissible changes as a path. Local optima become peaks and costly transitions become valleys. The metaphor is useful only after its components are separated.

There is first the cost of determining what the configurations and transitions are. There is next the cost of learning or evaluating their relevant properties. There is the computational cost of finding a suitable route through the resulting structure. Finally, there is the cost of traversing that route in the system represented. A map can reduce uncertainty about the first two and can support a better search over the third. It does not pay the fourth.

Suppose a graph has vertices \(X\), admissible edges \(E\), and an evaluation \(J\). If every path from \(x\) to a better vertex \(y\) passes through a vertex valued below \(J(x)\), then the barrier is a fact about \(E\) and \(J\). Giving the whole graph to a searcher does not change that fact. Knowledge converts an unknown transition cost into a known one. It does not convert a descent into a level path.

Conversely, a searcher can become stuck without any deep barrier. A rule that moves only to a strictly better neighbor halts at a local maximum even when a route to a better region exists. Here the failure belongs to the search rule and its information, not to reachability. The same graph can therefore support three independent statements: a target is reachable, every route to it has a positive barrier, and a particular local search never finds such a route.

A complete map does not automatically make the search cheap. The represented space may be exponentially large, the relevant optimization problem may be intractable, and the map itself may require more storage than the agent has. Map possession removes one kind of ignorance. It does not remove computational difficulty. An offline algorithm with the entire input and an online algorithm that encounters it piece by piece solve different formal problems even when they move through the same terrain.

The edge set is equally load-bearing. A local optimum is local only relative to what counts as one move. Enlarging the move set can lower a barrier or reveal a direct path. Yet a larger move is not mere bookkeeping unless it already has an implementation in the original system. Treating a sequence of existing moves as one macro may accelerate planning without enlarging reachability. Adding a genuinely new transformation changes the practical setup. The first operation compresses a path. The second creates an edge.

The objective matters too. A path may descend under one evaluation and rise under another. This does not show that barriers are unreal. It shows that a barrier is a property of an evaluated move structure, not of an unlabeled world. Once the graph and evaluation are fixed, the barrier is no more subjective than a shortest-path distance. Before they are fixed, talk of ruggedness lacks an object.

For this reason, ruggedness is best treated as an informal nickname for barrier structure rather than as a primitive quantity. Its formal content lies in a declared graph, objective, and path comparison. This graph formulation also avoids a familiar defect of landscape pictures: it requires no continuous surface, natural unit of distance, or meaningful slope. Configurations can be discrete, and adjacency can be stated directly.

There is therefore terrain and there are maps. The terrain is already a representation, because its vertices, edges, and values have been selected. The map is an agent's access to that represented structure. Search is a process over the map, and traversal is a process in the represented system. Difficulty can arise at each layer, but no layer should borrow the name of another.

Chapter 5Coordinate change, abstraction, and extension

The phrase change the representation conceals several operations with different logical force. At least three must be separated: coordinate change, abstraction, and extension.

A coordinate change is invertible. It relabels the same states and transports the relevant structure along the relabeling. If transitions, observations, targets, and costs are carried across correctly, reachability and barrier height remain unchanged. A coordinate system may make a symmetry visible or a calculation shorter, but it does not add a state, signal, or action. Any apparent gain in possibility under a genuine coordinate change reveals that some structure was not transported.

An abstraction is generally many-to-one. It deliberately forgets distinctions in order to preserve answers to a specified class of questions more cheaply. No abstraction is simply faithful or unfaithful in the abstract. It may preserve reachability while losing path cost, preserve optimal value while losing the identity of optimal policies, or preserve external behavior while losing internal causal structure. Its adequacy is indexed by the question it was built to answer.

Forward preservation is only half of the required relation. If every concrete step has an abstract image, then every concrete path can be sent upward. This supports negative reasoning: absence of an abstract path can rule out a concrete one. It does not support the reverse inference. An abstract path may be an artifact of states or transitions merged by the abstraction. Bringing a solution down requires a lifting or concretization condition, together with reflection of the target and any relevant cost or constraint.

The logical asymmetry is easy to miss. Once a concrete witness and a forward map are given, mapping that witness upward requires only forward preservation. This says nothing about the cost of discovering, constructing, or computing the map. A map can merge thousands of streets into one regional connection. A proof can replace a long calculation with a lemma. A program analysis can replace concrete states with an abstract domain. In each case the compressed structure is useful only if the conclusion needed below can be recovered. The return path carries an additional soundness burden.

Abstraction can be exact for one question while silent about others. A route planning shortcut may store a witness path and its true cost. It then invents no route and understates no cost for the query it serves. A different abstraction may preserve reachability only approximately, with a quantified error. Exact transfer and graded transfer are distinct designs. Bisimulation, simulation, homomorphism, abstract interpretation, and state abstraction offer different formal versions of this choice. The word abstraction alone guarantees none of them.

An extension adds structure not present in the original setup. It may add a predicate, a latent variable, a sensor, an effector, an axiom, or an objective. Unlike a coordinate change, it need not preserve the old space. Unlike an abstraction, it need not forget. It can change what is expressible, observable, derivable, or reachable because it changes the language or the coupling between agent and task.

Extension is therefore not a free redescription. A new observation channel can separate states previously indistinguishable. A new action can enlarge the reachable set. A new predicate can define a target the old vocabulary could not state. These are real gains, but they occur in a new setup. Whether a result in the extended setup answers the earlier question requires an explicit transfer claim. New axioms, for example, may yield a proof of an old-language statement, but their relevance depends on which preservation or success criterion connects the stronger theory to the original concern.

An explicit transfer theorem names source objects, target objects, and the property to be carried between them. The compatibility condition may be formulated in either description or in a metalanguage relating them. The resulting transfer claim is therefore indexed by that comparison framework.

The computational cost of representation change belongs in the account as well. Building a hierarchy can be far more expensive than querying it. A field may spend decades constructing definitions, invariants, and lemmas that later make one proof short. This does not mean the final theorem was already cheaply available. Preprocessing and query time are different resources, and conceptual construction can occur during both.

Fermat's Last Theorem illustrates the point if read cautiously. Elliptic curves, modular forms, and Galois representations supplied languages in which a route from a hypothetical counterexample to a contradiction became expressible. Much of that structure was developed independently of Fermat's equation. Yet the final proof was not a trivial query against a completed hierarchy. The Frey curve, Ribet's theorem, and the Taylor and Wiles machinery included problem-directed construction. The history contains abstraction, extension, preprocessing, and new proof steps, not only a change of viewpoint.

Coordinate change, abstraction, and extension can each reduce difficulty. They do so for different reasons. Coordinate change can expose structure already present. Abstraction can reduce the cost of answering selected questions. Extension can alter what the setup permits or expresses. Confusing them makes every successful reformulation look magical and every failed transfer hard to diagnose.

Chapter 6Scale and computational objectivity

An isolated instance can be difficult for accidental reasons. It may be large, badly presented, or unfamiliar. Complexity theory asks a different question. It organizes instances into a family, assigns each an input size, fixes a model of computation, and studies how the resources required for a task grow with that size.

Every term in this description matters. An encoding determines which distinctions are explicit and how input length is measured. A machine model determines which operations count as elementary. An access convention determines whether the input can be reread or arrives once as a stream. An output convention determines whether results can be emitted incrementally or must be retained until the end. An approximation convention determines how much error is permitted. A resource measure determines whether the object of interest is time, space, queries, communication, randomness, precision, samples, or proof length.

Without these declarations, a large space is not a lower bound. An exponential number of candidate policies may have compact structure. A machine with exponentially many states stores only linearly many bits in the logarithm of its state count. A one-pass lower bound need not apply to a machine with random access to a read-only input and a scratch tape. A cardinality argument can prove that a representation must distinguish many cases without proving that no efficient algorithm exploits their regularity.

This dependence on formal choices does not reduce complexity to convention. Theoretical computer science obtains robust notions by comparing reasonable models and encodings. If two machine models simulate one another with at most polynomial overhead, polynomial-time tractability is invariant between them. If two encodings translate into one another efficiently, a result insensitive to that translation earns a wider claim than a result tied to one notation. Objectivity here is invariance over a specified class, not independence from every possible description.

The comparison class cannot be left mysterious. An encoding that hides the answer in each input can make a difficult language trivial, while an encoding that expands inputs astronomically can manufacture favorable asymptotics. Such examples do not refute complexity theory. They show why its robustness claims quantify over constrained transformations rather than arbitrary renamings. The choice of what counts as reasonable is substantive, but once chosen it supports real theorems.

The calculator example belongs in a deliberately narrow part of this picture. A finite-state machine that must absorb a positional multiplication instance into its final finite state and then report the result needs a growing number of states as operand width grows. A fixed collection of finite-range registers therefore fails at some width. This is a streaming memory result. It is not a claim that multiplication is hard in standard complexity classes, and it is not by itself a psychological theory of human arithmetic.

Paper changes the formal encounter by supplying writable external storage. The procedure can record partial products and carries instead of retaining every needed distinction in a fixed internal state. The arithmetic task remains the same while the machine model changes. This is the precise lesson of the example: known structure and a known algorithm can still exceed one resource envelope and fit inside another.

Resource bounds can also move across time. A representation may be costly to construct but cheap to reuse. Compilers, indexes, proof libraries, abstraction hierarchies, and learned models all exchange preprocessing for later query cost. Calling the eventual query easy without counting construction can be accurate for one encounter and misleading for another. Complexity is indexed not only by the machine but by which work has already been done and which costs the task is defined to include.

The relational view and complexity theory therefore reinforce rather than oppose one another. The relational view says that the machine, encoding, and resource envelope belong in the statement of difficulty. Complexity theory shows how to state those dependencies rigorously and how to identify conclusions that survive broad changes of machine and representation.

Chapter 7Reflexivity and the limits of the account

Some systems respond not only to an agent's actions but to what they infer about the agent. In such a system an intervention can have three roles. It can change the underlying state, reveal information to the agent, and reveal information about the agent to the system. The third role makes the act of identification part of the dynamics being identified.

Strategic games provide the clearest examples. A move in chess changes the board and also changes an opponent's beliefs about the available plan. A bid changes an allocation and conveys information about valuation. A test input presented to an adaptive program may alter the program's later responses. Reflexivity is not a property unique to persons, and it does not appear merely because a system has many internal states. It appears when representations of the agent enter the causal process.

One formal response is state augmentation. If the transition law depends on a counterpart's policy and that policy changes by a specified update rule, the policy can be included in an enlarged state. The resulting product may again be Markovian. The companion proves a narrower exact statement: conditional on the current product state and action, the first marginal of one augmented step is the supplied transition law on the focal state. If that law depends on an evolving policy coordinate, the projected focal process need not itself be autonomous or Markovian.

What it accomplishes is representation, not solution. The added policy may be unobserved. The enlarged space may be too large to search. The update law may be misspecified. A belief over the original physical state that once sufficed no longer suffices when another policy carries predictive information. Augmentation moves complexity and hiddenness into an extended product state rather than abolishing them. This is an extension of the state description, not an invertible change of coordinates on the original state space.

Repeating the construction can produce a hierarchy: a model of a counterpart, a model of the counterpart's model, and further levels. If every level explicitly enumerates all policies over the previous level, the represented state spaces grow rapidly by construction. That arithmetic does not show that strategic reasoning must explicitly traverse the whole hierarchy. A game may possess an equilibrium, a sufficient statistic, a symmetry reduction, or a fixed point that provides a compact closure. Growth of one naive representation is not a lower bound on every representation.

The same caution applies to tracking. If a finite machine exactly tracks an output-separated finite machine through an encoding that intertwines every transition and reproduces every output, then the encoding must be injective. The tracker needs at least as many states as its target. This is a precise capacity theorem under a strong notion of exact tracking. It says nothing by itself about approximate prediction, prediction for one decision, explanation, interpretation, or understanding. A smaller model can preserve a task-relevant quotient of a much larger system.

Reflexivity also complicates experiment. When a system updates in response to the experiment itself, the observation law is policy-dependent. Passive regularities may fail under probing, and a probe can teach the system what is being sought. Yet not every changing response is reflexive. Exogenous drift, a fixed but unknown parameter, random disturbance, and strategic adaptation are different mathematical objects. Folding all variation into the word adaptive would destroy the distinction the concept was introduced to make.

At this point the limits of the whole account become visible.

First, the framework assumes a task with a success condition. It can analyze whether an answer is hidden, unreachable, too expensive, or inexpressible for a given agent. It does not derive the success condition from the dynamics. An objective is an input to the formal problem, even when the problem concerns how that objective behaves under representation change.

Second, the framework presumes a sufficiently definite question or success relation, not the existence of a successful answer. Empty target sets and unsatisfiable constraints are representable outcomes. If a theory leaves a sentence independent, the issue is not epistemic access to a hidden truth inside that theory. If a specification is inconsistent, no improvement in search reveals a satisfying object. If a question is vague, sharpening its terms changes the task rather than merely observing it more accurately. Hiddenness, nonexistence, inconsistency, independence, and vagueness are not variants of one condition.

Third, not every intellectual activity is usefully reconstructed as control. Proof search can be represented as movement through a space of derivations. Interpretation can be represented as selection among models. Invention can be represented as extension of a language. These reconstructions earn their place only when they preserve something explanatory. A vocabulary broad enough to call every change an action and every result a target risks explaining nothing.

Fourth, formal verification has a limited role. A proof assistant can establish that a conclusion follows from definitions and hypotheses. It can expose a missing premise, a vacuous existential claim, or a counterexample to an overstated theorem. It does not establish that the formal objects capture the phenomenon named in the prose. A two-state witness proves possibility. A cardinality bound proves a necessary condition under a stated encoding. Neither licenses an unrestricted claim about perception, discovery, or strategic life.

These limits are not defects to be repaired by adding more notation. They mark the division of labor among philosophy, mathematics, and verification. Philosophy distinguishes the kinds of claim being made. Mathematics supplies exact models in which some of those distinctions can be proved. Verification checks derivations inside the models. Empirical and historical claims require evidence of their own.

The position can now be stated without metaphor. Difficulty is not a monadic property attached to an isolated problem. It is a relation among a task's structure, an agent's representation, the information and transformations available through an interface, and the resources permitted by a computational or physical regime. Epistemic, practical, computational, and expressive difficulty name different ways this relation can fail. Coordinate changes, abstractions, and extensions alter it in different ways. Reflexive systems add the further fact that the relation itself may enter the dynamics.

The mathematical reconstruction that follows will not prove this philosophy as a universal thesis. It will do something narrower and more useful. It will make selected distinctions exact enough that their hypotheses, consequences, and limits can no longer be exchanged without notice.

Part IIMathematical reconstruction

Part I treated difficulty as a relation between a task and an agent. This part chooses mathematical objects for selected parts of that relation. The choice is deliberately plural. A finite automaton is the right object for one memory question and the wrong object for a partially observed control problem. A graph can expose reachability and barriers without saying anything about the time needed to find a path. A logical vocabulary can expose indistinguishability without supplying a procedure that computes a classifier.

The formal companion checks consequences inside these chosen models. Whenever the prose moves from a theorem to an interpretation, the relevant hypotheses will be repeated. A proof about exact simulation will not be presented as a proof about approximate prediction. A cardinality identity will not be presented as a time lower bound. A forward simulation will not be presented as a license to execute an abstract plan.

Chapter 8From a task-agent relation to a dynamic system

The most general object needed here is not a dynamical system. It is an encounter between a task and a bounded agent.

Let a task be

\[ \mathsf Q=(I,W,\mathsf{ok},c_{\mathrm{world}},B_{\mathrm{world}}). \]

Here \(I\) is a set of instances, \(W\) is a set of proposed answers or witnesses, and

\[ \mathsf{ok}\subseteq I\times W \]

is the success relation. The term \(c_{\mathrm{world}}\) assigns a cost to an instance and execution record, and \(B_{\mathrm{world}}\) gives an admissible bound. The cost may be scalar, vector-valued, or path-dependent; its codomain carries the declared preorder used below, componentwise in the vector case. This separation matters. A shortest path algorithm can make route selection cheap while the journey remains long. A proof assistant can verify a proof quickly after a human has spent years finding it. Search cost and realization cost are different terms.

An agent meets the task through another tuple,

\[ \mathsf A=(\mathsf{enc},\mathsf{obs},\mathsf{act},\Pi,R). \]

The encoding \(\mathsf{enc}\) determines how instances are presented. The observation interface \(\mathsf{obs}\) determines what information becomes available. The action interface \(\mathsf{act}\) determines which proposed transformations can be realized. The class \(\Pi\) contains the programs or policies the agent is allowed to use. The resource envelope \(R\) specifies bounds on such quantities as time, memory, observations, communication, and precision.

This notation is schematic rather than a single universal datatype. Its job is to force the parameters of a hardness claim into view. For a decision problem, \(W\) may be Bool. For a search problem, a witness can be much larger than the input. For a control problem, the answer is a policy and \(c_{\mathrm{world}}\) depends on the trajectory generated by that policy. A resource statement becomes meaningful only after these types and costs are fixed.

The interfaces and a policy induce an execution law

\[ \mathsf{Exec}_{\mathsf Q,\mathsf A}(i,p) \in\Delta(\mathsf{Trace}\times W) \]

over records containing a trace \(\tau\) and a proposed answer \(w\). The program does not act directly on the bare instance. Its observations, available actions, resulting trace, and output are mediated by this execution semantics. For a declared risk allowance \(0\leq\alpha\leq1\), relative solvability can then be written schematically as

\[ \exists p\in\Pi_R\;\forall i\in I, \quad \Pr_{(\tau,w)\sim\mathsf{Exec}_{\mathsf Q,\mathsf A}(i,p)} \!\left[ \mathsf{ok}(i,w)\land c_{\mathrm{world}}(i,\tau)\leq B_{\mathrm{world}}(i) \right]\geq 1-\alpha, \]

where deterministic execution is the special case of a point mass and an approximate success relation can replace \(\mathsf{ok}\) when required. The subscript \(R\) restricts the programs under consideration; it does not change which answers are correct. If no such program exists, the failure may come from the interface, the program class, the resource bound, the world-cost bound, or the absence of any acceptable witness. The formula alone does not identify which.

The companion contains one deliberately narrow realization of a resource envelope. Envelope P selects a finite set of admitted programs. For any real-valued objective on a nonempty finite envelope, exists_bounded_optimum proves that an admitted maximizer exists. If one envelope is contained in another and both optima are supplied, bounded_optimum_mono proves that the larger envelope's optimum has at least the smaller envelope's value. exists_strict_envelope_gap gives a witness in which the best admitted program is strictly worse than an excluded program. These are finite-set existence and order results. They provide no search algorithm, no complexity bound for finding an optimum, and no global optimum for an infinite program space.

Complexity theory adds a size map

\[ |\cdot|:I\to\mathbb N \]

and asks how resource use scales over all instances of size \(n\). Without an instance family and a size convention, a large state count is not yet a complexity result. Without a machine model, the word "space" is ambiguous. Without an output convention, computing and recognizing may be different problems.

The dynamic specialization

For navigation problems, begin with the environmental core

\[ \mathcal P=(X,U,Y,T,Z,G,C). \]

The state space is \(X\), the input or action space is \(U\), and the observation space is \(Y\). The transition law \(T\) describes how actions change states. The observation law \(Z\) describes what signals states emit. The target is \(G\subseteq X\), and the admissible or safe region is \(C\subseteq X\).

In a deterministic system these laws may be functions

\[ T:X\times U\to X, \qquad Z:X\to Y. \]

In a stochastic system they are kernels

\[ T(\,\cdot\mid x,u)\in\Delta(X), \qquad Z(\,\cdot\mid x)\in\Delta(Y). \]

For general state spaces, \(X\) and \(Y\) carry sigma algebras and the kernels must be measurable. The PMF development in the Lean companion instead assumes countably supported distributions. The kernel development removes the countability restriction for the uncontrolled trajectory results, at the cost of explicit measurability hypotheses.

A partially observed policy has the form

\[ \pi_t:Y^{t+1}\to\Delta(U), \]

or, if past actions are not reconstructible from the policy, a function of the full action-observation history. A state-feedback rule

\[ \pi:X\to\Delta(U) \]

is a different object. It is justified when the state is available to the controller or when a sufficient statistic, such as a belief state, has replaced the latent state. The current Lean definition ConstrainedReachable uses the state-feedback form. Its theorems therefore concern a fully observed controller, or a supplied sufficient statistic, rather than the observation-history policies in POSystem.

For an initial state \(x_0\), horizon \(H\), and risk allowance \(0\leq\alpha\leq 1\), one possible success condition is

\[ \Pr_\pi\!\left[X_H\in G \text{ and }X_t\in C\text{ for }0\leq t\leq H\right] \geq 1-\alpha. \]

This is exact-horizon reachability. Reaching \(G\) at some time no later than \(H\) is a different event unless \(G\) has been made absorbing. The PMF formalization checks the exact-horizon event and requires the initial state to belong to \(C\). The kernel path theorem checks safety from times \(1\) through \(H\), with terminal membership in \(G\cap C\). These conventions agree when the initial state is assumed safe, but they are not definitionally identical.

The machine-checked monotonicity results are modest sanity checks. Increasing the risk allowance cannot destroy an existing certificate, and enlarging the target cannot reduce the success probability. They do not prove that a certificate exists, provide a planning algorithm, or convert a state-feedback policy into an observation-history policy. Also, if \(\alpha>1\) is permitted in the extended nonnegative reals, \(1-\alpha\) truncates to zero and the condition can become vacuous. Probability language therefore requires the explicit bound \(\alpha\leq 1\).

The tuple \(\mathcal P\) is the environmental core of a dynamic encounter, not the whole task-agent relation and not a definition of every hard problem. A complete dynamic specification also fixes the presentation of instances, an initial state or initial law, the admissible policy class, its resource envelope, a horizon, a risk bound, and any trajectory cost not already represented by the safe set. With those parameters supplied, observation, action, world cost, and computational search can be separated mathematically.

Chapter 9Experiment-indexed indistinguishability

Equality of two immediate readings is too weak to represent partial observability. An action may expose a difference that passive inspection does not, and a difference may appear only after several transitions. The relevant equivalence relation must therefore be indexed by a class of experiments.

Let \(\mathcal E\) be a declared set of admissible probing policies. For an initial state \(x\), experiment \(e\in\mathcal E\), and finite horizon \(n\), write

\[ \mathbb P^{e,n}_x \]

for the law of the resulting observation history. Define

\[ x\sim_{\mathcal E}x' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall e\in\mathcal E\;\forall n\in\mathbb N, \quad \mathbb P^{e,n}_x=\mathbb P^{e,n}_{x'}. \]

This is equality of laws, not equality of one sampled history. It allows stochastic observations and adaptive probes. It is relative to the experiment class: enlarging \(\mathcal E\) can split an equivalence class, while restricting available probes can merge states that a stronger interface would separate.

The Lean relation ObsEquiv uses this form with every policy List O -> PMF A admitted. The theorem obsEquiv_equivalence verifies reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity. The implementation quantifies over every policy of this function type, not merely physically or computationally feasible experiments. A feasible experiment class would be an additional parameter.

The concrete timing convention also matters. In obsProcess, each round first observes the current state, passes the extended observation history to the policy, and then applies the chosen action. An \(n\)-round run emits \(n\) observations from states \(x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}\); the terminal state \(x_n\) is not emitted. The Bayesian update below is a separate predict-then-observe construction. No theorem silently identifies the two conventions.

Decision-critical ambiguity

Let \(A^*(x)\subseteq U\) be the actions acceptable at state \(x\). Exact state identification is unnecessary when the ambiguity does not alter the available choice. The robust menu at \(x\) is

\[ \mathcal R(x)=\bigcap_{x'\sim_{\mathcal E}x} A^*(x'). \]

Robust choice is impossible at \(x\) exactly when \(\mathcal R(x)=\varnothing\). The companion theorem robustSet_eq_iInter checks the intersection reading against its predicate definition. A sufficient pair certificate, called DecisionCritical in the companion, is

\[ x\sim_{\mathcal E}x' \qquad\text{and}\qquad A^*(x)\cap A^*(x')=\varnothing. \]

From these hypotheses, DecisionCritical.no_robust_action proves that no single deterministic action belongs to the robust menu. The conclusion is exactly that. The pair certificate is not necessary: three acceptable sets can intersect pairwise while their total intersection is empty. The theorem does not show that a new experiment exists, that the model can be repaired, or that a randomized action satisfies some relaxed loss bound. Those are separate questions with separate definitions.

This formulation also makes the data-processing boundary elementary. If a statistic \(q\) identifies two histories or states, any rule factoring through that statistic must give them the same output. In symbols, if

\[ f=g\circ q \quad\text{and}\quad q(x)=q(x'), \]

then \(f(x)=f(x')\). FactorsThrough.eq_of_eq proves this general fact. The deterministic and randomized statistic-obstruction theorems then combine it with disjoint acceptable sets.

This is the precise sense in which deterministic post-processing cannot make an observation partition finer. It does not say that post-processing has no value. A statistic can suppress noise, compress a history, expose an invariant, or make information usable within a memory bound. It simply cannot distinguish two inputs that it maps to the same value.

Watching and probing

Two models can agree under a passive action and disagree under an intervention. The companion gives a finite Boolean witness. One model flips its state when the probe action is true; another remains fixed. Both reveal the current state exactly and behave identically under the passive action. At horizon two, the active probe produces different observation histories. The theorem exists_probe_only_distinguishable proves existence of this separation.

The witness establishes that intervention can add identifying information. It does not classify all systems into passive and active kinds, nor does it show that every probe is safe or useful. The probe changes the trajectory whose law is being compared. Identification and intervention are joined by construction.

Belief updates and zero evidence

For a prior \(b\in\Delta(X)\), a one-step predictive kernel \(K\), observation kernel \(Z\), and observed atom \(o\), the companion defines

\[ e(o)=\sum_{x'} Z(o\mid x')\,(bK)(x'). \]

When \(e(o)\neq0\), the posterior is

\[ b'(x')= \frac{Z(o\mid x')\,(bK)(x')}{e(o)}. \]

The theorem bayesEvidence_eq_zero_iff states exactly when the normalizer vanishes in this discrete model: for every next state, either its predicted mass is zero or its likelihood for \(o\) is zero. Thus the maintained predictive distribution assigned the observed atom probability zero.

Calling this event a refutation of an entire model class would be stronger than the formal object permits. The belief is over states, not over a declared set of competing transition and observation models. Sensor corruption and out-of-model events are not represented. The theorem localizes a zero in the current predictive law; interpretation beyond that requires additional assumptions.

The belief process itself forms a Markov process on \(\Delta(X)\). The theorem beliefKernel_bind_id verifies a barycentre identity: averaging the posterior over the predictive observation law returns the one-step predicted belief. Individual observations can move the posterior sharply and can change an optimal action. The theorem says only that Bayesian conditioning redistributes the predicted mass without changing its expectation.

Finally, equality of observation supports is weaker than equality of observation laws. supportEquiv_iff_no_concept_separates characterizes what a formal concept lattice can distinguish when incidence records only whether an observation has nonzero probability. It forgets probability values, temporal dependence, and experimental intervention. Support equality can coincide with full-law equivalence in special families where support determines the admitted laws, but no such hypothesis is present here. Without it, support equivalence can be strictly coarser than \(\sim_{\mathcal E}\).

Chapter 10Reachability, constraints, and barriers

At the level of a fixed graph or effector set, observation and reachability are separate structures. Perfect observation does not add an edge to a graph. At the policy level they can interact: better observations can permit state-dependent action choices and thereby enlarge the set of endpoints reached safely or with a stated probability, even though the underlying edge relation is unchanged.

Let \(G_0\) be a set of deterministic effectors \(g:X\to X\). Finite reachability is the least relation satisfying

\[ x\in\operatorname{Reach}_{G_0}(x) \]

and

\[ y\in\operatorname{Reach}_{G_0}(x),\;g\in G_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(y)\in\operatorname{Reach}_{G_0}(x). \]

The Lean inductive relation Reach implements this closure. traj_mem_reach proves that any trajectory whose successive steps select effectors from \(G_0\) remains in the orbit. The selection rule can vary with time; the theorem needs only the local fact that each realized step uses an allowed transformation.

The definition Programmed G g says

\[ \forall x,\qquad g(x)\in\operatorname{Reach}_{G}(x). \]

The theorem reach_insert_programmed proves that inserting such a map as a new primitive does not enlarge reachability. This is a closure theorem, but its name invites a stronger computational reading than its definition warrants. For each input state, the witnessing path may have a different length and may be selected noncomputably. A uniform program would instead provide one finite syntax, or one computable dispatcher with a stated observation interface. The current theorem needs neither.

The existential theorem exists_new_effector_enlarges_reach gives a two-state witness in which adding Boolean negation makes a formerly unreachable state reachable. It proves that a genuinely different transformation can enlarge an orbit. It does not say that every added primitive does so, or that transformations are available only through physical hardware.

Safe stochastic reachability

For a stochastic transition law and policy, reachability becomes a probability of a trajectory event. The PMF development defines a rollout and proves monotonicity under a larger risk allowance and a larger target. The kernel development defines \(n\)-step transition kernels on arbitrary measurable spaces. Its restricted iterate removes mass whenever a transition leaves the safe set. iterateSafe_le_iterateKernel proves that this restricted mass never exceeds the unrestricted mass.

The path-space bridge is important because a restricted kernel could otherwise be only a suggestive construction. trajMeasure_safety identifies it with the measure of the genuine event

\[ X_t\in C\text{ for }1\leq t\leq n, \qquad X_n\in G\cap C. \]

This is a finite-horizon, stationary, uncontrolled Markov result with measurable \(C\) and \(G\). It does not supply a controller, an infinite-horizon theorem, or a computational procedure for evaluating the measure.

Barriers are costs on paths

Reachability is Boolean. A target is either in the orbit or it is not. To represent a costly transition, add an objective \(J:X\to\mathbb R\) and an admissible adjacency relation \(\mathsf{Adj}\). A finite path from \(x\) to \(y\) is a list beginning at \(x\), ending at \(y\), and chained by \(\mathsf{Adj}\). Define its deepest loss below the starting value by

\[ D_x(\gamma)=\sup_{z\in\gamma} \max\{J(x)-J(z),0\}. \]

The barrier is

\[ B(x,y)=\inf_{\gamma:x\leadsto y}D_x(\gamma), \]

with the empty infimum set to \(+\infty\). The convention separates absence of a path from existence of paths with large finite losses.

Several exact consequences are machine checked. If \(B(x,y)>0\), every admissible path contains some state \(z\) with \(J(z)<J(x)\). If one path never falls below \(J(x)\), then the barrier is zero. For \(d>0\), the inequality

\[ B(x,y)<d \]

is equivalent to existence of an admissible path contained in the strict superlevel region

\[ \{z:J(x)-d<J(z)\}. \]

These results say nothing about how a path is found. They also need careful wording. A path that never falls below its starting value need not be stepwise monotone. Conversely, when infinitely many paths are available, an infimum of zero need not be attained by a zero-loss path. A positive barrier guarantees a dip somewhere on each path; to say that the dip occurs before arrival at a better endpoint, one must additionally assume \(J(y)>J(x)\).

On a finite Boolean hypercube with single-coordinate flips, an additively separable objective

\[ J(p)=\sum_v f_v(p_v) \]

has a path from any point to a coordinatewise maximizer that never drops below its starting value. barrier_eq_zero_of_separable proves this special result. Interaction terms are therefore necessary for a positive barrier to such a maximizer under that move relation. A separable objective can still have a positive barrier to a different target, and a different adjacency relation can change the result.

The same landscape can separate local action from global knowledge. The five-state example in BoundedMachines contains a strict-ascent rule that stalls at a lesser peak, a route to the global maximum, and an unavoidable dip on every such route. Having the complete map removes ignorance about the route. It does not remove the dip defined by the objective and the move relation.

Chapter 11Resource-bounded computation

A graph may contain a path while every available search procedure exceeds its budget. To state that claim in complexity-theoretic form, fix four items:

  1. an instance family \(I_n\),
  2. an encoding whose length defines \(n\),
  3. a machine and input-output convention,
  4. a resource measure.

For a program \(p\), a resource vector may be written

\[ R_p(n)= (T_p(n),S_p(n),Q_p(n),C_p(n)), \]

where the coordinates denote time, working space, query count, and communication. Nothing requires these resources to be interchangeable. A memory lower bound does not imply a time lower bound, and a large explicit policy space does not imply that every algorithm enumerates it.

The formal companion currently proves finite-state and storage-capacity results. It does not formalize general asymptotic time, query, or communication complexity; none of its theorems supplies those omitted bounds.

Fixed-width multiplication as a language family

For \(n\in\mathbb N\), let digits n a be the \(n\)-bit, little-endian encoding of \(a<2^n\) over symbols \(0\) and \(1\). Let the third symbol be a separator. The language binMul n contains words of the form

\[ \operatorname{digits}_n(a)\cdot\#\cdot \operatorname{digits}_n(b)\cdot\#\cdot \operatorname{digits}_{2n}(ab). \]

For each fixed width this is a finite language. The question is how the state count of a deterministic one-pass recognizer grows with \(n\).

The proof uses distinguishable prefixes. For each possible first operand \(a<2^n\), consider its \(n\)-digit prefix. If \(a\neq b\), append the encoding of the second operand \(1\) and the product \(a\). The completion is accepted after prefix \(a\) and rejected after prefix \(b\). Therefore the automaton must reach different states after all \(2^n\) first-operand prefixes.

The theorem binMul_needs_exp_states concludes

\[ 2^n\leq |S|. \]

This is a state-complexity lower bound for a DFA that consumes the whole input from left to right and decides membership from its final state. It is not a lower bound for a Turing machine that can reread a read-only input, use an unbounded work tape, or emit an answer incrementally.

State count is not the honest memory unit. A set of \(2^s\) states stores \(s\) bits. For automata whose state type is exactly \(\operatorname{Fin}(2^s)\), binMul_needs_n_bits derives

\[ n\leq s. \]

Thus the exponential count is a linear information bound. Describing it as "exponential memory" would be a rate error.

A generic Myhill-Nerode style theorem underlies the argument. card_le_of_pairwise_distinguishable proves that a finite set of pairwise suffix-distinguishable prefixes injects into the state set of every DFA recognizing the language. Aristotle produced the proof under Lean 4.28; it is now ported into Positional and checked under the repository toolchain. The concrete positional theorem supplies the distinguishable prefixes for the fixed-width multiplication language.

Recognition, production, and final-state storage

Recognizing a valid multiplication record is not the same task as producing a product. The companion separates two storage arguments.

An Emitter has a memory map and a later output map. If it must reproduce each of \(2^n\) possible operands, the memory states assigned to those operands must be distinct. emitter_needs_exp_states is exactly this injection argument. It models delayed output after the input-dependent information has been absorbed. It does not cover a transducer that emits digits while reading, or a machine that can reread the original input.

The more concrete RegisterMachine has \(k\) registers, each taking values in a finite range of size \(r\). It reads the fixed-width digits of two operands once and produces a natural-number output from its final register configuration. Its total state capacity is

\[ r^k. \]

If it computes all \(n\)-digit products for \(n>0\), fixing the second operand to one makes the final-state map on first operands injective. Hence

\[ b^n\leq r^k \]

for base \(b\geq2\). If the configurations themselves fit into at most \(2^s\) possibilities, linear_bit_lower_bound gives \(n\leq s\).

The theorem no_constant_register_budget then states that fixed \(r\) and \(k\) fail at some width. For a meaningful machine interpretation, the register range should be nonempty. As currently stated, the case \(r=0\) can be vacuous because there may be no machine to quantify over. The nonvacuous content is the finite positive-range case.

These theorems permit a narrow comparison. A fixed finite final-state device cannot handle arbitrary width under this one-pass interface, while storage that grows with width can retain the required distinctions. They do not establish a psychological number of human memory registers. They also do not make integer multiplication hard in a standard complexity class.

The older theorem mulLang_not_regular concerns a unary multiplication verification language. It correctly proves nonregularity, and mulDecide_correct gives an explicit decision function for that language. Unary input is not the positional representation used in ordinary arithmetic, and the decider calls ordinary natural-number multiplication internally. These results establish regularity and computability facts, not a useful upper and lower bound pair for long multiplication.

Exact tracking as a capacity bound

Another finite-state result concerns simulation rather than arithmetic. A tracking map intertwines every transition of one deterministic machine with another and preserves outputs. If the tracked machine is output-separated, meaning that distinct states can eventually be distinguished by some input word, Tracks.injective proves that the tracking map is injective. For finite state spaces,

\[ |S_{\mathrm{tracked}}|\leq |S_{\mathrm{tracker}}|. \]

This is a necessary capacity condition for exact homomorphic tracking. It says nothing about approximate prediction, a task-specific statistic, learned compression, or the time required to update the encoding. When each machine is output-separated, mutual tracking forces equality of finite cardinalities, not identity of machines or mutual interpretation.

The converse fails even at equal cardinality. In the explicit Boolean pair same_cardinality_but_no_tracking, one machine flips at every input and the other remains fixed, while each reports its state as output. Output preservation forces a proposed tracking map to be the identity, which is incompatible with the different transitions. Equal state count is therefore neither sufficient for exact tracking nor evidence of dynamical equivalence.

Chapter 12Solution transfer requires a return path

The word "representation" covers operations with different mathematical effects. A coordinate change is an invertible relabeling. An abstraction is usually many-to-one and deliberately forgetful. An extension adds a new predicate, observation, state component, or move. Their transfer obligations are different.

Let the concrete system be a graph \((X,\mathsf{Adj})\), the abstract system a graph \((X',\mathsf{Adj}')\), and

\[ \phi:X\to X' \]

the abstraction map.

Forward simulation

A forward simulation satisfies

\[ \mathsf{Adj}(x,y) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathsf{Adj}'(\phi x,\phi y). \]

By induction over a finite path, every concrete path maps to an abstract path. The companion implements this as PathBetween.map. Consequently, if no path exists between the corresponding abstract endpoints, no concrete path existed between the chosen concrete endpoints. isEmpty_of_map_isEmpty checks this contrapositive.

Forward simulation is useful for impossibility. It is insufficient for execution. An abstract graph may contain extra edges, and a many-to-one map may merge concrete states with different available moves. An abstract planner can then assemble a path from locally plausible steps that no single concrete trajectory realizes.

A one-state counterexample makes the gap explicit. Map the sole concrete state to false. Give the concrete graph no edges and the abstract graph an edge from false to true. Forward simulation holds vacuously because there are no concrete steps to check. The abstract target true is reachable, but there is no concrete state in its fibre and no concrete solution. Surjectivity onto all abstract states would remove this particular defect but would still not ensure that each abstract edge is available from the concrete representative actually reached.

Local path lifting

The appropriate descent condition is local. For every concrete state \(x\) and every abstract step offered from its image, require

\[ \mathsf{Adj}'(\phi x,y') \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \exists y\in X, \quad \mathsf{Adj}(x,y)\text{ and }\phi y=y'. \]

This condition is stronger than saying that some member of the fibre over \(\phi x\) can implement the step. The path must lift from the representative produced by the preceding lifted steps. Induction then gives a concrete lift of every finite abstract path beginning at an image point.

Targets need a second condition. For concrete and abstract goal sets \(G\) and \(G'\), sound descent requires goal reflection:

\[ \phi(x)\in G' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x\in G. \]

Without it, an abstract target cell can contain both concrete successes and concrete failures. With local path lifting and goal reflection, abstract reachability from \(\phi(x_0)\) implies concrete reachability from \(x_0\).

For equivalence rather than one-way soundness, combine four requirements:

  1. concrete steps map to abstract steps,
  2. abstract steps lift from every actual representative,
  3. concrete goals map to abstract goals,
  4. abstract goal membership reflects concrete success.

Under these exact hypotheses, concrete and abstract goal reachability agree. These conditions also compose: lifting through two abstraction layers can be performed one layer at a time, and exact goal correspondence is transitive.

The imported companion now proves this generic descent theorem. The dedicated Aristotle target returned unchanged proofs of forward mapping, finite path lifting, goal reflection, exact reachability equivalence, and composition. Its explicit finite counterexample shows why forward simulation without lifting is insufficient. The returned file was checked under Lean 4.28 before the proofs were ported and checked again in this repository.

Cost-preserving shortcuts

The companion does contain a strong same-space instance of solution transfer. Let \(S(x,y)\) be a shortcut relation added to the base adjacency relation. If every shortcut edge is backed by a concrete base path, then pathBetween_shortcut_nonempty_iff proves that adding shortcuts does not change which endpoints are connected.

Reachability preservation alone does not preserve barriers. A shortcut from one side of a valley to the other can hide the low intermediate states while still naming a real path. A sufficient certificate for preserving the barrier induced by \(J\) is a witness path \(\delta\) for each shortcut \(u\to w\) satisfying

\[ \forall z\in\delta, \qquad \min\{J(u),J(w)\}\leq J(z). \]

Under this depth condition, barrier_shortcut_eq proves equality of every barrier before and after shortcut insertion. The theorem exists_shortcut_hiding_valley proves that mere path backing is insufficient: it can preserve reachability while strictly lowering the represented barrier. It does not prove that the displayed depth certificate is necessary in every graph.

This is the cleanest formal example of an abstraction certificate in the companion. A shortcut stores a concrete witness and enough cost information to answer the specified path question honestly. It does not establish that the shortcut hierarchy is cheaper to construct, smaller to store, or faster to search. Those are algorithmic claims requiring a preprocessing model and a query-complexity analysis.

The theorem named exists_escape_hatch is an extension result rather than an abstraction result. Its witness leaves the Boolean state set and objective fixed but replaces an empty adjacency relation with a universal one. That is an extension of the move set. It correctly shows that reachability depends on the declared actions, but it does not show that a coordinate change or a forgetful map creates a path.

An invertible coordinate change is cleaner. The companion's Representation module considers deterministic input-indexed dynamics conjugated by an equivalence \(e:X\simeq X'\). run_conj proves that the equivalence commutes with every finite input word. With outputs transported along the same equivalence, indist_conj_iff proves that finite-word observational indistinguishability is preserved in both directions. The theorem image_reachableSet_conj proves that the image of the concrete reachable set is exactly the reachable set in the conjugate coordinates.

These are machine-checked coordinate invariance results for deterministic runs, observations, and reachability. The CoordinateBarrier development transports a graph, its paths, and its objective through an equivalence. pathDepth_relabel and pathDepth_unrelabel preserve the depth of each path, path_nonempty_relabel_iff preserves path existence in both directions, and barrier_relabel_eq preserves every barrier height, including infinity caused by unreachability. None of these results says that discovering or computing the equivalence is cheap.

Chapter 13Composition and semantics, narrowly stated

Composition becomes useful only after the objects, interfaces, and preserved properties have been named. Category theory supplies a language for that bookkeeping, but the existence of a categorical construction does not by itself establish behavioral correctness.

Let \(A\) be a category of interfaces, \(X\) a category of systems, and

\[ L:A\to X \]

a functor assigning a system object to each interface. An open system from interface \(a\) to interface \(b\) is a structured cospan

\[ L(a)\longrightarrow x\longleftarrow L(b). \]

The apex \(x\) is the internal system; the two arrows specify how its boundary is attached. If \(X\) has pushouts, two structured cospans sharing an interface can be composed by gluing their apices along the image of that interface. The companion definition StructuredCospan.hcomp implements exactly this pushout construction.

Pushouts are unique only up to canonical isomorphism. Accordingly, horizontal composition is not literal syntactic equality under reassociation. The definition hcompAssoc constructs the associating isomorphism using mathlib's pushout pasting lemmas. This is a structural statement about the chosen open systems. It does not say that a numerical objective, transition kernel, or reachable set has been preserved.

For fixed boundaries, a morphism between two structured cospans is an apex map making both boundary triangles commute. The companion proves that these morphisms form a category. It defines a behavior-preserving replacement to be such a morphism whose apex map is an isomorphism. Here "behavior-preserving" is a declared name for exact structural replacement at the apex. No external semantics or observation law is part of the definition.

Functorial semantics

A semantics functor

\[ \mathsf{Sem}:W\to B \]

obeys

\[ \mathsf{Sem}(g\circ f) =\mathsf{Sem}(g)\circ\mathsf{Sem}(f), \qquad \mathsf{Sem}(1_x)=1_{\mathsf{Sem}(x)}. \]

The companion records these as direct instances of the functor laws. Their content is exact: interpretation commutes with the composition already present in \(W\). They do not imply that \(\mathsf{Sem}\) is faithful, full, injective on objects, information-losing, or adequate for a task. A faithful embedding may forget no morphism-level distinctions at all. A coarse semantics may identify many systems. Those are additional properties, not consequences of being a functor.

Likewise, an oplax or lax comparison map records a directed relation between the semantics of a composite and the composite of the semantics. Without an order, norm, enrichment, or metric, the comparison has no numerical magnitude. It cannot be called a measurement of how much composition fails.

One elementary preservation law in the companion concerns invariant sets. If both \(f\) and \(g\) map a set \(C\) into itself, then \(g\circ f\) maps \(C\) into itself. invariance_comp proves this closure. A different local property need not compose: increment_bound_not_compositional exhibits two real functions that each move every point by at most one while their composite moves some point by two. The example does not show that safety in general fails to compose. It shows that a specific one-step margin is not closed under the chosen composition.

The abstraction results of the preceding chapter fit this narrow view. Forward simulations compose because step preservation composes. Exact solution transfer also needs the descent and goal conditions to compose. Structured cospans do not supply those conditions automatically. If reachability, probability, cost, or observational equivalence is the property of interest, it must appear among the interface hypotheses and receive its own preservation theorem.

Chapter 14Expressivity and revision

Some failures occur before search. The current representation may identify states that the target separates. To state this without pretending to have a complete syntax of thought, model a vocabulary as a set of predicates

\[ \mathcal L\subseteq\{P:X\to\mathsf{Prop}\}. \]

Define vocabulary equivalence by

\[ x\equiv_{\mathcal L}y \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall P\in\mathcal L, \quad(P(x)\leftrightarrow P(y)). \]

Adding predicates refines this partition: equivalence under a larger vocabulary implies equivalence under every smaller vocabulary contained in it. The companion proves reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and this monotonicity.

Saturation is not syntactic definability

A Boolean classifier \(d:X\to\mathsf{Bool}\) is called VocabInvariant when it is constant on the equivalence classes induced by \(\mathcal L\). The theorem exists_invariant_decider_iff states

\[ \begin{aligned} &\exists d, \quad d\text{ is constant on }\equiv_{\mathcal L} \quad\text{and}\quad d(x)=\mathsf{true}\leftrightarrow x\in F \\ &\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad F\text{ is a union of }\equiv_{\mathcal L}\text{-classes}. \end{aligned} \]

This is a semantic factorization theorem. It says that the characteristic function of \(F\) descends to the quotient \(X/{\equiv_{\mathcal L}}\) exactly when membership in \(F\) is constant on each fibre. The Lean proof constructs the Boolean classifier using classical decidability of set membership.

It does not prove that \(F\) is definable by a finite formula built from the predicates in \(\mathcal L\). It does not produce a computable classifier. Without syntax and closure operations on the vocabulary, "expressible" here means only "invariant under the induced partition," not logical definability.

The theorem vocab_blind_spot gives the negative direction directly. If \(x\equiv_{\mathcal L}y\) while exactly one of \(x,y\) belongs to \(F\), every fibre-constant Boolean classifier fails somewhere.

What extension proves

Suppose a predicate \(P\) separates an \(\mathcal L\)-equivalent pair. The theorem expansion_gain compares \(\mathcal L\) with

\[ \mathcal L'=\mathcal L\cup\{P\}. \]

The target chosen in the theorem is the extension of \(P\) itself. It is not classifiable by an \(\mathcal L\)-invariant rule, but it is classifiable after \(P\) is inserted. This is a valid existence witness that vocabulary extension can refine a relevant partition.

The construction does not explain where \(P\) comes from. It adds the target predicate itself, so it resembles supplying an oracle for the distinction of interest. It proves neither that arbitrary extensions help nor that a useful extension can be discovered within any resource bound. Those are constructive and computational questions beyond the theorem.

Persistent satisfiability

The model-theoretic compactness result concerns a different issue. Let \(T\) be a first-order background theory, \(o_n\) a sequence of observation sentences, and \(h\) a hypothesis sentence. If

\[ T\cup\{o_n:n\in F\}\cup\{h\} \]

is satisfiable for every finite \(F\subseteq\mathbb N\), compactness implies that

\[ T\cup\{o_n:n\in\mathbb N\}\cup\{h\} \]

is satisfiable. satisfiable_of_finite_observations checks this consequence of the first-order compactness theorem. rival_hypotheses_persist applies it separately to two hypotheses.

The result supplies one model for each expanded theory. It does not show that the two models have identical probabilities, are elementarily equivalent, or respond identically to every adaptive experiment. To draw an experimental indistinguishability conclusion, the observation sentences would have to encode the entire admissible experiment class and the resulting models would need an appropriate equivalence theorem.

Diagonal limits

For any enumeration

\[ e:\mathbb N\to(\mathbb N\to\mathsf{Bool}), \]

define

\[ f(n)=\neg e(n)(n). \]

Then \(f\neq e(k)\) for every \(k\). diagonal_escape machine checks this standard diagonal argument. no_final_stage applies the same argument to the enumerated rule stock at any selected stage of an explicitly countable hierarchy. halting_void separately restates mathlib's undecidability of the halting predicate.

These results have sharp boundaries. Diagonalization shows that a countable enumeration of all Boolean functions cannot be extensionally complete. It does not show that every practical representation is countable in the relevant sense, that the escaping function matters to a given task, or that every revision process must encounter it. The stage theorem proves a new diagonal function for each presented enumeration; it does not rule out a compact sufficient statistic for a restricted problem class.

Revision can therefore mean at least three formal operations: refining a partition by adding predicates, changing a theory while retaining its signature, or enlarging a computational class. The theorems above illuminate particular operations. None establishes a universal law that representation revision always helps or never terminates.

Chapter 15Reflexive augmentation

A transition law is reflexive in the narrow sense needed here when it depends on a variable representing another system's current policy, model, or response rule, and that variable itself changes during interaction.

Let \(x\in X\) be the physical or focal state, \(u\in\mathcal U\) an action, and \(p\in P\) the additional policy coordinate. Suppose

\[ T(\,\cdot\mid x,u,p)\in\Delta(X) \]

describes the next focal state and

\[ F:P\times\mathcal U\to P \]

updates the policy coordinate. Define a transition on the product by sampling \(x'\) from \(T\) and setting

\[ (x,p)\longmapsto(x',F(p,u)). \]

The companion definition extendStep implements this construction. The theorem extendStep_fst proves

\[ \operatorname{map}(\operatorname{fst}, \widetilde T(\,\cdot\mid(x,p),u)) =T(\,\cdot\mid x,u,p). \]

Thus the first marginal of the augmented one-step law is exactly the supplied state transition. extendStepRand_fst proves the same identity when the policy update is independently randomized conditional on the current \(p\) and action.

This is a representation theorem for one specified architecture. It does not make \(p\) observable or make planning on \(X\times P\) efficient. The randomized definition samples \(x'\) and \(p'\) independently given the current state and action. If the policy update depends on the realized next state, observation, or payoff, the kernel must be generalized to encode that dependence, for example by sampling \(p'\) conditionally on \(x'\) or by using a joint kernel.

State augmentation can restore a Markov description when the added coordinate contains all history relevant to the next transition. This is a conditional statement. If omitted history still affects the update, the product state is not sufficient. If the policy coordinate is latent, augmentation relocates the observability problem rather than removing it.

Self-tuning as product dynamics

The deterministic structure Tuned contains a state update and a parameter update. Its fixedStep combines them into one transition on \(S\times\Theta\). The theorems Tuned.stateRun_eq_fst and Tuned.paramRun_eq_snd prove by induction that the two separately defined runs are the projections of the ordinary product-state run. For finite types,

\[ |S\times\Theta|=|S|\,|\Theta|. \]

The result removes a false formal mystery: changing parameters during a run does not lie outside state-transition mathematics when the update rule is itself fixed and included in the state. It does not show that parameter learning is easy, that the correct parameter type is known, or that the product state is observable.

Policy-space cardinality

For finite state and action sets, the set of deterministic stationary policies has cardinality

\[ |U^X|=|U|^{|X|}. \]

card_policySpace checks this identity. The augmented state

\[ X\times(X\to U) \]

therefore has cardinality

\[ |X|\,|U|^{|X|}. \]

The requisite-variety theorem derives a related response-count lower bound under two strong hypotheses: the response-outcome map separates disturbances within each response fibre, and regulation holds the outcome exactly constant. Without the separation premise the counting inequality is false, as the companion's counterexample demonstrates.

Cardinality is not computational complexity. A policy may have a compact parametric representation, a symmetry, a sufficient statistic, or an equilibrium characterization. The identity does not prove that a planner must enumerate all policies or that its running time is exponential.

The counterexample exponential_policy_space_with_flat_objective makes the logical gap explicit. Boolean policies on an \(n\)-state domain number \(2^n\), but under a constant objective every policy is globally optimal. Search-space size can matter, but only together with the structure of the objective and the access model.

Explicit hierarchies and exact tracking

The recurrence

\[ N_0=s, \qquad N_{k+1}=N_k p^{N_k} \]

models an explicit construction that pairs the current state space with every policy from that space to a \(p\)-element action set. Under \(s\geq1\) and \(p\geq2\), towerCard_strictMono and towerCard_unbounded prove that this particular recurrence strictly grows and is unbounded.

The theorem is about the recurrence because the recurrence was defined to add the full policy space at every stage. It does not prove that strategic reasoning must use this representation, that higher-order beliefs have no finite closure, or that no equilibrium summarizes the hierarchy. It shows the cost of naive explicit augmentation.

The tracking results from the preceding complexity chapter provide a separate limit. Exact tracking of an output-separated deterministic machine requires an injective encoding of its states. This becomes relevant when the policy coordinate is itself a machine to be simulated. Yet exact simulation is only one possible objective. Prediction sufficient for one action can require far less information, while an equal state count does not guarantee any tracking map at all.

Finally, experiment and intervention can become coupled in an augmented model. An action may alter \(x\), reveal information about \(p\), and alter \(p\)'s future update. The Boolean probe witness from Chapter 9 proves that active and passive identification can differ in at least one model pair. The product-state theorems show how a declared adaptive coordinate can be represented. Neither result supplies a general solution to identification in reflexive systems. They isolate the objects on which such a solution would have to operate.

Chapter 16What the proofs establish

The formal companion does not contain one theorem called relational hardness. It contains smaller results about particular relations: which states an observation process identifies, which targets a move set reaches, which path costs survive compression, which computations exceed a finite memory model, and which conclusions remain invariant under an exact change of coordinates. Their value lies in this smaller scale. Each result fixes a vocabulary and makes the relevant quantifiers visible.

The theorem cards below separate a formal result from its intended reading. The distinction is load-bearing. A theorem may establish a necessary condition without supplying an algorithm. A counterexample may refute a universal claim without describing a typical case. An existence proof may rely on classical choice without naming a computable witness. Verification certifies the derivation from the hypotheses, not the adequacy of the hypotheses as a model of some further phenomenon.

Observational equivalence and robust action

Hypotheses. A partially observed stochastic system has a transition kernel, an observation kernel, and policies mapping finite observation histories to distributions over actions. Two initial states are observationally equivalent when every such policy, at every finite horizon, induces the same probability law over observation histories. Each state also has a set of acceptable actions.

Conclusion. Observational equivalence is an equivalence relation. The actions robustly acceptable at a state are exactly the intersection of the acceptable sets over its observational equivalence class. Robust choice is infeasible exactly when this total intersection is empty. If two equivalent states have disjoint acceptable sets, no robust action exists at either state. (machine-checked)

def ObsEquiv (M : POSystem S A O) (s s' : S) : Prop :=
  ∀ (π : List O → PMF A) (n : ℕ), obsProcess M π n s [] = obsProcess M π n s' []

  refl _ := fun _ _ => rfl
  symm h := fun π n => (h π n).symm
  trans h₁ h₂ := fun π n => (h₁ π n).trans (h₂ π n)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Decision-critical ambiguity: two observationally equivalent states whose

def DecisionCritical (M : POSystem S A O) (Astar : S → Set A) (s s' : S) : Prop :=
  ObsEquiv M s s' ∧ Disjoint (Astar s) (Astar s')

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- An action is robust at `s` when it is acceptable in every state
observationally equivalent to `s`. -/
def RobustAcceptable (M : POSystem S A O) (Astar : S → Set A) (s : S) (a : A) : Prop :=
  ∀ s', ObsEquiv M s s' → a ∈ Astar s'

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Robust actions are exactly the intersection of the acceptable sets over
the equivalence class of `s`. -/
theorem robustSet_eq_iInter (M : POSystem S A O) (Astar : S → Set A) (s : S) :
    {a | RobustAcceptable M Astar s a} = ⋂ s' ∈ {s' | ObsEquiv M s s'}, Astar s' := by
  ext a
  simp [RobustAcceptable]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Robust choice is infeasible when no action is acceptable throughout the
observational equivalence class. -/
def RobustlyInfeasible (M : POSystem S A O) (Astar : S → Set A) (s : S) : Prop :=
  ¬ ∃ a, RobustAcceptable M Astar s a

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- The exact criterion for robust infeasibility is emptiness of the total
intersection of acceptable-action sets over the observational class. Pairwise
disjointness is sufficient but not necessary for this intersection to be empty. -/
theorem robustlyInfeasible_iff_iInter_eq_empty
    (M : POSystem S A O) (Astar : S → Set A) (s : S) :
    RobustlyInfeasible M Astar s ↔
      ⋂ s' ∈ {s' | ObsEquiv M s s'}, Astar s' = ∅ := by
  rw [← robustSet_eq_iInter]
  change (¬ ({a | RobustAcceptable M Astar s a} : Set A).Nonempty) ↔ _
  exact Set.not_nonempty_iff_eq_empty

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- A disjoint observationally equivalent pair certifies that no robust action
exists at the first state. -/
theorem DecisionCritical.no_robust_action
    {M : POSystem S A O} {Astar : S → Set A} {s s' : S}
    (h : DecisionCritical M Astar s s') :
    ¬∃ a, RobustAcceptable M Astar s a := by
  rintro ⟨a, ha⟩
  have h₁ : a ∈ Astar s := ha s ((obsEquiv_equivalence M).refl s)
  have h₂ : a ∈ Astar s' := ha s' h.1
  exact Set.disjoint_left.mp h.2 h₁ h₂

Interpretation. The observation process partitions the state space at the resolution relevant to every admissible finite experiment. Exact state recovery is unnecessary when the acceptable sets over an equivalence class retain a common member. Robust choice is infeasible exactly when their total intersection is empty. A disjoint pair is a sufficient witness of emptiness, not a necessary one.

Does not imply. The result does not prove that the modeled policy class contains every physically possible experiment, that the maintained transition and observation kernels are correct, or that an informative new experiment exists. Pairwise intersections do not guarantee a robust action: three or more acceptable sets may intersect pairwise while having empty total intersection.

Reachability and pointwise derived transformations

Hypotheses. A set \(G\) contains deterministic state transformations. Reachability is generated by finite compositions of members of \(G\), including the empty composition. A transformation \(g\) is programmed over \(G\) when, from every state \(x\), the state \(g(x)\) is already reachable using members of \(G\).

Conclusion. Every trajectory whose successive steps apply transformations from \(G\) remains in the reachability closure of its initial state. Adding a programmed transformation \(g\) as a primitive leaves that closure unchanged. There also exists a two-state example in which adding a genuinely new transformation strictly enlarges reachability. (machine-checked)

Lean proof (BoundedMachines.lean): HardProblems.Reach, HardProblems.traj_mem_reach, HardProblems.Programmed, HardProblems.reach_insert_programmed, HardProblems.exists_new_effector_enlarges_reachrun
-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Finite-composition reachability: `y` is reachable from `x` by
applying effectors drawn from `G`. -/
inductive Reach (G : Set (X → X)) (x : X) : X → Prop
  | refl : Reach G x x
  | tail : ∀ {y z : X} {g : X → X}, Reach G x y → g ∈ G → z = g y → Reach G x z

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Any admissible trajectory stays inside the reachable set: at each
step some effector in `G` was applied, chosen by an arbitrary policy,
and no choice rule ever escapes the orbit. -/
theorem traj_mem_reach (G : Set (X → X)) (σ : ℕ → X)
    (hstep : ∀ n, ∃ g ∈ G, σ (n + 1) = g (σ n)) (n : ℕ) :
    Reach G (σ 0) (σ n) := by
  induction n with
  | zero => exact Reach.refl
  | succ n ih =>
      obtain ⟨g, hg, heq⟩ := hstep n
      exact Reach.tail ih hg heq

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Pointwise realizability over `G`: from every state, the image under `g` is
already reachable with `G`. The definition supplies neither a uniform generator
word nor a computable dispatcher for the witnessing paths. -/
def Programmed (G : Set (X → X)) (g : X → X) : Prop :=
  ∀ y : X, Reach G y (g y)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Inserting a pointwise realizable transformation as a primitive leaves every
reachability orbit unchanged. -/
theorem reach_insert_programmed (G : Set (X → X)) {g : X → X}
    (hg : Programmed G g) (x z : X) :
    Reach (insert g G) x z ↔ Reach G x z := by
  constructor
  · intro h
    induction h with
    | refl => exact Reach.refl
    | @tail y z f hy hmem heq ih =>
        rcases hmem with (rfl | hmem)
        · rw [heq]
          exact Reach.trans ih (hg y)
        · exact Reach.tail ih hmem heq
  · intro h
    induction h with
    | refl => exact Reach.refl
    | tail hy hmem heq ih =>
        exact Reach.tail ih (Set.mem_insert_of_mem g hmem) heq

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A transformation outside the prior reachability closure can strictly
enlarge reach in an explicit two-state example. -/
theorem exists_new_effector_enlarges_reach :
    ∃ (G : Set (Bool → Bool)) (g : Bool → Bool) (x z : Bool),
      Reach (insert g G) x z ∧ ¬ Reach G x z := by
  refine ⟨∅, Bool.not, false, true, ?_, ?_⟩
  · exact Reach.tail Reach.refl (Set.mem_insert _ _) rfl
  · intro h
    cases h with
    | tail hy hmem heq => exact hmem.elim

Interpretation. Under the formal definition, inserting \(g\) creates no new endpoint because each starting state already has some \(G\)-path to \(g(x)\). That witness may depend on \(x\), may have unbounded length across states, and need not be computable. A single finite composition implementing \(g\) uniformly is sufficient for this condition but strictly stronger. Policy complexity does not let a realized trajectory escape the orbit of the effectors it applies.

Does not imply. The result does not classify real hardware and software, or show that software can never expose an effector omitted from a previous model. It does not produce a uniform macro, a computable dispatcher, or a common bound on the witnessing paths. The strict enlargement theorem is existential, not a claim that every added transformation enlarges reach.

Path barriers

Hypotheses. A directed adjacency relation specifies finite paths between states, and a real-valued objective \(J\) assigns a score to each state. The depth of a path is the greatest drop below its starting score along that path. The barrier from \(x\) to \(y\) is the infimum of these depths over all paths, with the infimum over no paths equal to infinity.

Conclusion. A positive barrier forces every admissible path to visit a state whose score is strictly below \(J(x)\). If one path never falls below \(J(x)\), the barrier is zero. If no path exists, the barrier is infinite. More generally, the barrier is below a positive depth \(d\) exactly when some finite path keeps every dip below \(d\). (machine-checked)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- Barrier height from `x` to `y`: the infimum, over admissible paths, of the
deepest dip below `J x` along the path (dips measured in `ℝ≥0∞`, so paths
that never dip contribute `0`). Empty infimum is `⊤`: no path, infinite

  ⨅ γ : PathBetween Adj x y, ⨆ z ∈ γ.points, ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- A positive barrier means every admissible route to `y` contains a
configuration strictly worse than the start. No hypothesis here says that `y`
is better than the start or that the dip occurs before first reaching `y`. -/
theorem exists_dip_of_barrier_pos {Adj : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ} {x y : X}
    (h : 0 < barrier Adj J x y) (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) :
    ∃ z ∈ γ.points, J z < J x := by
  have hγ : 0 < ⨆ z ∈ γ.points, ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z) :=
    lt_of_lt_of_le h (iInf_le _ γ)
  obtain ⟨z, hz⟩ := lt_iSup_iff.mp hγ
  obtain ⟨hmem, hpos⟩ := lt_iSup_iff.mp hz
  refine ⟨z, hmem, ?_⟩
  have := ENNReal.ofReal_pos.mp hpos
  linarith

theorem barrier_eq_zero_of_monotone_path {Adj : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ}
    {x y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) (hγ : ∀ z ∈ γ.points, J x ≤ J z) :
    barrier Adj J x y = 0 := by
  refine le_antisymm ?_ zero_le
  refine le_trans (iInf_le _ γ) ?_
  refine iSup_le fun z => iSup_le fun hz => ?_

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- The barrier sits strictly below `d` exactly when some admissible path
keeps every dip strictly below `d`. The backward direction uses the
finiteness of a path's point list: finitely many quantities each below `d`
have supremum below `d`. -/
theorem barrier_lt_iff {Adj : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ} {x y : X}
    {d : ℝ≥0∞} :
    barrier Adj J x y < d ↔
      ∃ γ : PathBetween Adj x y, ∀ z ∈ γ.points,
        ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z) < d := by
  constructor
  · intro h
    obtain ⟨γ, hγ⟩ := iInf_lt_iff.1 h
    exact ⟨γ, fun z hz =>
      lt_of_le_of_lt (le_biSup (fun z => ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z)) hz) hγ⟩
  · rintro ⟨γ, hγ⟩
    have hx : x ∈ γ.points := List.mem_of_mem_head? γ.head_eq
    have hd : 0 < d := lt_of_le_of_lt zero_le (hγ x hx)
    exact lt_of_le_of_lt (iInf_le _ γ) (biSup_list_lt _ hd γ.points hγ)

Interpretation. Barrier height belongs to the objective and the declared move relation. It distinguishes a reachable target that requires a descent from an unreachable target, and it converts the valley metaphor into a quantified property of finite paths.

Does not imply. Knowledge of the barrier does not pay its traversal cost, find a minimizing path, or show that path search is computationally tractable. A different objective or edge relation defines a different barrier. The theorem does not make either choice intrinsic to an unrepresented world.

Backed shortcuts and separable objectives

Hypotheses. A shortcut relation augments an original adjacency relation. /-- Single-coordinate-flip adjacency on the hypercube. -/
def flipAdj {V : Type*} [DecidableEq V] (p q : V → Bool) : Prop :=
  ∃ v, q = Function.update p v (!(p v))

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- Helper: if every coordinate on which `p` disagrees with `y` occurs in a
duplicate-free list `l`, there is a flip path from `p` to `y` along which
the separable objective never drops below its value at `p`. -/
theorem exists_monotone_flipPath {V : Type*} [Fintype V] [DecidableEq V]
    (f : V → Bool → ℝ) (y : V → Bool) (hy : ∀ v b, f v b ≤ f v (y v)) :
    ∀ l : List V, l.Nodup → ∀ p : V → Bool, (∀ v, p v ≠ y v → v ∈ l) →
      ∃ γ : PathBetween flipAdj p y,
        ∀ z ∈ γ.points, ∑ w, f w (p w) ≤ ∑ w, f w (z w) := by
  intro l
  induction l with
  | nil =>
      intro _ p hp
      have hpy : p = y := funext fun v => by
        by_contra hv
        simpa using hp v hv
      refine ⟨⟨[p], rfl, by simp [hpy], List.isChain_singleton _⟩, ?_⟩
      intro z hz
      rw [List.mem_singleton] at hz
      subst hz
      exact le_rfl
  | cons v t ih =>
      intro hnd p hp
      have htnd : t.Nodup := (List.nodup_cons.1 hnd).2
      by_cases hv : p v = y v
      · refine ih htnd p fun w hw => ?_
        rcases List.mem_cons.1 (hp w hw) with rfl | hwt
        · exact absurd hv hw
        · exact hwt
      · obtain ⟨p', hp'⟩ : ∃ p', p' = Function.update p v (y v) := ⟨_, rfl⟩
        have hstep : p' = Function.update p v (!(p v)) := by
          have hnot : y v = !(p v) := by
            revert hv; cases p v <;> cases y v <;> simp
          rw [hp', hnot]
        have hmono : ∑ w, f w (p w) ≤ ∑ w, f w (p' w) := by
          refine Finset.sum_le_sum fun w _ => ?_
          rcases eq_or_ne w v with rfl | hw
          · rw [hp', Function.update_self]; exact hy w (p w)
          · rw [hp', Function.update_of_ne hw]
        have hp'mem : ∀ w, p' w ≠ y w → w ∈ t := by
          intro w hw
          rcases eq_or_ne w v with rfl | hne
          · rw [hp', Function.update_self] at hw; exact absurd rfl hw
          · rw [hp', Function.update_of_ne hne] at hw
            rcases List.mem_cons.1 (hp w hw) with rfl | hwt
            · exact absurd rfl hne
            · exact hwt
        obtain ⟨γ', hγ'⟩ := ih htnd p' hp'mem
        refine ⟨⟨p :: γ'.points, rfl, List.mem_getLast?_cons γ'.last_eq, ?_⟩, ?_⟩
        · refine List.isChain_cons.2 ⟨fun q hq => ?_, γ'.admissible⟩
          rw [γ'.head_eq, Option.mem_def, Option.some_inj] at hq
          exact ⟨v, hq ▸ hstep⟩
        · intro z hz
          rcases List.mem_cons.1 hz with rfl | hzt
          · exact le_rfl
          · exact hmono.trans (hγ' z hzt)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- An additively separable objective admits a nondecreasing flip path from any
configuration to the coordinatewise maximizer, so that particular barrier
vanishes. -/
theorem barrier_eq_zero_of_separable {V : Type*} [Fintype V] [DecidableEq V]
    (f : V → Bool → ℝ) (x y : V → Bool)
    (hy : ∀ v b, f v b ≤ f v (y v)) :
    barrier flipAdj (fun z => ∑ v, f v (z v)) x y = 0 := by
  obtain ⟨γ, hγ⟩ := exists_monotone_flipPath f y hy (Finset.univ : Finset V).toList
    (Finset.univ.nodup_toList) x fun v _ => Finset.mem_toList.2 (Finset.mem_univ v)
  refine le_antisymm ((iInf_le _ γ).trans ?_) zero_le
  refine iSup_le fun z => iSup_le fun hz => ?_
  refine le_of_eq (ENNReal.ofReal_eq_zero.2 ?_)
  have h := hγ z hz
  simp only [sub_nonpos]
  exact h

Interpretation. The displayed depth witness is a sufficient certificate not only for reachability but for the path property being claimed. The separability theorem eliminates the barrier to a target that maximizes every coordinate term under single-coordinate flips: independent contributions can then be improved one at a time.

Does not imply. An arbitrary option, macro, quotient, or abstract edge is not automatically backed. The theorem does not say that every coupled objective has a positive barrier, or that separable optimization is computationally cheap under every representation. Even a separable objective can have a positive barrier to a target that is not coordinatewise maximizing. The shortcut result preserves the stated path score, not every possible property of a path.

Positional multiplication and finite memory

Hypotheses. A streaming register machine reads two fixed-width base-\(b\) operands once, stores only \(k\) registers each ranging over a finite set of size \(r\), and produces the product from its final register configuration. It must be correct for every operand pair below \(b^n\), with \(b\) at least two, \(r\) positive, and \(n\) positive.

Conclusion. Correctness forces at least \(b^n\) distinct final register configurations, hence \(b^n \le r^k\). If all configurations fit into at most \(m\) bits, then \(n \le m\). For every fixed positive finite \(r\) and finite \(k\), some positive width cannot be handled. (machine-checked)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Positional.lean
/-- The machine computes `n`-digit multiplication when its final-state output

In particular, this quantifies over the actual transition-based execution; it
is not the mere existence of an unconstrained function on inputs. -/

    (M : RegisterMachine base range k) (n : ℕ) : Prop :=
  ∀ x y, x < base ^ n → y < base ^ n →
    M.output (M.run (multiplicationInput base n x y)) = x * y

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Positional.lean
/-- If a machine computes multiplication at width `n`, fixing the second
operand to one makes final configurations for distinct first operands distinct. -/
theorem finalState_injective {base range k n : ℕ} [NeZero base]
    (M : RegisterMachine base range k) (hbase : 2 ≤ base) (hn : 0 < n)
    (hM : M.ComputesMultiplicationAt n) :
    Function.Injective (fun x : Fin (base ^ n) =>
      M.run (multiplicationInput base n x 1)) := by
  intro x y hxy
  have h1 : (1 : ℕ) < base ^ n := one_lt_pow₀ (by omega) (by omega)
  have hx := hM x 1 x.is_lt h1
  have hy := hM y 1 y.is_lt h1
  simp only [mul_one] at hx hy
  simp only at hxy
  rw [hxy] at hx
  exact Fin.ext (hx.symm.trans hy)

/-- The central configuration-count lower bound: a correct width-`n` machine
needs at least `base^n` different register configurations. -/
theorem state_capacity_lower_bound {base range k n : ℕ} [NeZero base]

    (hM : M.ComputesMultiplicationAt n) :
    base ^ n ≤ range ^ k := by
  have hinj := finalState_injective M hbase hn hM
  simpa using Fintype.card_le_of_injective _ hinj

/-- Quantitative binary form.  If all register configurations can be encoded
in at most `bits` bits, correctness forces at least `n` bits. -/
theorem linear_bit_lower_bound {base range k n bits : ℕ} [NeZero base]
    (M : RegisterMachine base range k) (hbase : 2 ≤ base) (hn : 0 < n)
    (hM : M.ComputesMultiplicationAt n)
    (hbits : range ^ k ≤ 2 ^ bits) :
    n ≤ bits := by
  have h1 : base ^ n ≤ range ^ k := state_capacity_lower_bound M hbase hn hM
  have h2 : base ^ n ≤ 2 ^ bits := h1.trans hbits
  have h3 : 2 ^ n ≤ base ^ n := Nat.pow_le_pow_left hbase _
  have h4 : 2 ^ n ≤ 2 ^ bits := h3.trans h2
  exact (Nat.pow_le_pow_iff_right (by decide : 1 < 2)).mp h4

positive digit width cannot be handled under `ComputesMultiplicationAt`. Thus

    ∃ n > 0, ∀ M : RegisterMachine base range k,
      letI : NeZero base := ⟨by omega⟩
      ¬ M.ComputesMultiplicationAt n := by
  letI : NeZero base := ⟨by omega⟩
  have hbase1 : 1 < base := hbase
  by_cases hk : k = 0
  · exact ⟨1, by norm_num, fun M hM => by
      have hcap := state_capacity_lower_bound M hbase (by norm_num : 0 < 1) hM
      simp [hk] at hcap
      omega⟩
  · by_cases hr : range = 0
    · exact ⟨1, by norm_num, fun M hM => by
        have hcap := state_capacity_lower_bound M hbase (by norm_num : 0 < 1) hM
        simp [hr, hk] at hcap
        omega⟩
    · have hk0 : k ≠ 0 := hk
      have hpos : 0 < range ^ k := by positivity
      exact ⟨range ^ k, hpos, fun M hM => by

Interpretation. The lower bound is an information-retention argument. By fixing the second operand to one, distinct first operands must end in distinct configurations because the final output must reproduce them. The theorem is a lower bound for delayed production in this one-pass register model.

Does not imply. Multiplication is not thereby hard in standard complexity classes. The result does not apply unchanged to machines with rereadable input, unbounded registers, an auxiliary tape, or incremental output. The companion's decider for a unary multiplication-verification language is a separate computability result, not an upper bound for this positional production task. The result is not a theorem about human working memory, and state count must be converted to bits before being read as a space bound.

Coordinate invariance of behavior and reachability

Hypotheses. Two deterministic systems share an input alphabet. An equivalence between their state spaces conjugates every input-indexed transition, and their output maps agree through that equivalence. Runs are finite input words, indistinguishability means equal outputs after every such word, and reachability means arrival after some finite word.

Conclusion. Transporting a run through the equivalence gives exactly the run of the conjugate system. Observational indistinguishability is preserved in both directions. The image of the reachable set in the original coordinates is exactly the reachable set in the new coordinates. (machine-checked)

Exact tracking

Hypotheses. Two deterministic finite machines have the same input and output types. A tracking map sends target states into tracker states, intertwines every transition, and preserves outputs. The target machine is output-separated: every pair of distinct target states is distinguished by the output produced after some finite input word.

Conclusion. Every exact tracking map is injective. A finite tracker therefore has at least as many states as the output-separated machine it tracks. If two output-separated finite machines track one another exactly, their state counts are equal. (machine-checked)

/-- A deterministic machine with inputs `I`, outputs `O`, and state

structure Machine (I O S : Type*) where
  step : S → I → S
  out : S → O

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Run a machine from state `s` on an input word `w`. -/
def Machine.run (M : Machine I O S) (s : S) (w : List I) : S :=

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A machine is output-separated when any two states that agree on the
outputs along every input word are equal. -/
def Machine.Separated (M : Machine I O S) : Prop :=
  ∀ s t : S, (∀ w : List I, M.out (M.run s w) = M.out (M.run t w)) → s = t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- `f` tracks machine `B` inside machine `A`: the encoding intertwines
the dynamics and reproduces the outputs. This is what it means for `A`
to maintain a faithful copy of `B`. -/
structure Tracks (A : Machine I O S) (B : Machine I O T) (f : T → S) : Prop where
  step_eq : ∀ t i, f (B.step t i) = A.step (f t) i
  out_eq : ∀ t, A.out (f t) = B.out t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A tracking map intertwines whole runs, not just single steps. -/
theorem Tracks.run_eq {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (t : T) (w : List I) :
    f (B.run t w) = A.run (f t) w := by
  induction w generalizing t with
  | nil => rfl
  | cons i w ih =>
    simp [Machine.run]
    rw [← h.step_eq]
    exact ih (B.step t i)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Tracking an output-separated machine is injective: the tracker must
hold distinct internal states for distinct tracked states. -/
theorem Tracks.injective {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (hB : B.Separated) : Function.Injective f := by
  intro t₁ t₂ hft
  apply hB t₁ t₂
  intro w
  have eq1 : f (B.run t₁ w) = A.run (f t₁) w := h.run_eq t₁ w
  have eq2 : f (B.run t₂ w) = A.run (f t₂) w := h.run_eq t₂ w
  rw [← h.out_eq (B.run t₁ w), ← h.out_eq (B.run t₂ w), eq1, eq2, hft]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- The pigeonhole bound: a finite machine can exactly track an
output-separated machine only if it has at least as many states. This is a
capacity bound for the stated exact tracking relation. -/
theorem card_le_of_tracks [Fintype S] [Fintype T]
    {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (hB : B.Separated) :
    Fintype.card T ≤ Fintype.card S := by
  exact Fintype.card_le_of_injective _ (Tracks.injective h hB)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Mutual exact tracking forces cardinality parity for finite
output-separated machines. It does not by itself give an isomorphism between
their transition systems. -/
theorem card_eq_of_mutual_tracks [Fintype S] [Fintype T]
    {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S} {g : S → T}
    (hf : Tracks A B f) (hg : Tracks B A g)
    (hA : A.Separated) (hB : B.Separated) :
    Fintype.card S = Fintype.card T := by
  exact le_antisymm (card_le_of_tracks hg hA) (card_le_of_tracks hf hB)

Interpretation. Exact dynamical embedding must retain every state distinction that can eventually affect an output. The theorem is a representation-capacity lower bound obtained by converting any collision in the tracking map into a contradiction with output separation.

Does not imply. Equal state counts are not sufficient for tracking, and a smaller machine may still predict a quotient relevant to one task. The result does not formalize understanding, simulation within an error tolerance, or strategic competence. Cardinality alone is not a computational complexity lower bound.

Reflexive augmentation and the explicit tower

Hypotheses. A transition may depend on a policy coordinate that changes by a specified deterministic or stochastic update. Semantic vocabulary and saturation

Hypotheses. A vocabulary \(L\) is modeled as a set of predicates on a state space. Two states are \(L\)-equivalent when every predicate in \(L\) has the same truth value at both. Conclusion. An \(L\)-invariant Boolean rule classifies target membership exactly if and only if the target is saturated under \(L\)-equivalence, meaning it is a union of equivalence classes.     (J : X → ℝ) (x : X) {y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ z ∈ γ.points, ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- The least path depth; the empty infimum is `⊤`. -/
noncomputable def barrier {X : Type u} (Adj : X → X → Prop)
    (J : X → ℝ) (x y : X) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨅ γ : PathBetween Adj x y, pathDepth J x γ

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Relabeling a path and objective preserves that path's depth exactly. -/
theorem pathDepth_relabel {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {Adj : X → X → Prop} (e : X ≃ Y) (J : X → ℝ)
    {x y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) :
    pathDepth (fun z => J (e.symm z)) (e x) (γ.relabel e) =
      pathDepth J x γ := by
  simp only [pathDepth, PathBetween.relabel, PathBetween.map,
    Equiv.symm_apply_apply]
  apply le_antisymm
  · refine iSup_le fun z => iSup_le fun hz => ?_
    obtain ⟨a, ha, rfl⟩ := List.mem_map.mp hz
    simpa using
      (le_iSup_of_le a
        (le_iSup_of_le ha (le_refl (ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J a)))))
  · refine iSup_le fun a => iSup_le fun ha => ?_
    refine le_iSup_of_le (e a) (le_iSup_of_le ?_ ?_)
    · exact List.mem_map.mpr ⟨a, ha, rfl⟩
    · simp

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Returning a relabeled path to the source coordinates also preserves its
depth exactly. -/
theorem pathDepth_unrelabel {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {Adj : X → X → Prop} (e : X ≃ Y) (J : X → ℝ)
    {x y : X} (γ : PathBetween (Relabel e Adj) (e x) (e y)) :
    pathDepth J x (γ.unrelabel e) =
      pathDepth (fun z => J (e.symm z)) (e x) γ := by
  simp only [pathDepth, unrelabel_points, Equiv.symm_apply_apply]
  apply le_antisymm
  · refine iSup_le fun a => iSup_le fun ha => ?_
    obtain ⟨z, hz, rfl⟩ := List.mem_map.mp ha
    exact le_iSup_of_le z (le_iSup_of_le hz (le_refl _))
  · refine iSup_le fun z => iSup_le fun hz => ?_
    refine le_iSup_of_le (e.symm z) (le_iSup_of_le ?_ ?_)
    · exact List.mem_map.mpr ⟨z, hz, rfl⟩
    · rfl

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Exact coordinate changes preserve the existence of a path. -/
theorem path_nonempty_relabel_iff {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {Adj : X → X → Prop} (e : X ≃ Y) {x y : X} :
    Nonempty (PathBetween (Relabel e Adj) (e x) (e y)) ↔
      Nonempty (PathBetween Adj x y) := by
  constructor
  · rintro ⟨γ⟩
    exact ⟨γ.unrelabel e⟩
  · rintro ⟨γ⟩
    exact ⟨γ.relabel e⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Exact coordinate changes preserve every barrier height, including the
infinite value caused by unreachability. -/
theorem barrier_relabel_eq {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    (Adj : X → X → Prop) (e : X ≃ Y) (J : X → ℝ) (x y : X) :
    barrier (Relabel e Adj) (fun z => J (e.symm z)) (e x) (e y) =
      barrier Adj J x y := by
  apply le_antisymm
  · refine le_iInf fun γ => ?_
    exact iInf_le_of_le (γ.relabel e)
      (le_of_eq (pathDepth_relabel e J γ))
  · refine le_iInf fun γ => ?_
    exact iInf_le_of_le (γ.unrelabel e)
      (le_of_eq (pathDepth_unrelabel e J γ))

Interpretation. Coordinate invariance extends from finite-word behavior and reachable sets to the evaluated path structure. A genuine coordinate change preserves the valley rather than erasing it.

Does not imply. The theorem concerns an invertible equivalence, not a many-to-one abstraction, a new edge relation, or a changed objective. It does not say that every informal reframing preserves barriers, and it assigns no cost to finding or computing the coordinate map.

Cardinality caveats

Hypotheses. The first explicit finite example compares a Boolean machine that flips state on every input with a Boolean machine that remains fixed, both using their state as output. The second considers all Boolean policies on an \(n\)-state domain and a constant objective over that policy space.

Conclusion. Equal state cardinality does not supply an exact tracking map between the two machines. A policy space of cardinality \(2^n\) can have every policy globally optimal under a flat objective. (machine-checked)

Lean proof (BoundedMachines.lean, SystemsTheory.lean): HardProblems.Machine, HardProblems.Tracks, HardProblems.flipMachine, HardProblems.inertMachine, HardProblems.same_cardinality_but_no_tracking, HardProblems.IsOptimal, HardProblems.exponential_policy_space_with_flat_objectiverun run
-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A deterministic machine with inputs `I`, outputs `O`, and state
space `S`: a step function and an output map. -/
structure Machine (I O S : Type*) where
  step : S → I → S
  out : S → O

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- `f` tracks machine `B` inside machine `A`: the encoding intertwines
the dynamics and reproduces the outputs. This is what it means for `A`
to maintain a faithful copy of `B`. -/
structure Tracks (A : Machine I O S) (B : Machine I O T) (f : T → S) : Prop where
  step_eq : ∀ t i, f (B.step t i) = A.step (f t) i
  out_eq : ∀ t, A.out (f t) = B.out t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A Boolean machine that flips at every input. -/
def flipMachine : Machine Unit Bool Bool where
  step s _ := !s
  out s := s

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A Boolean machine that never changes state. -/
def inertMachine : Machine Unit Bool Bool where
  step s _ := s
  out s := s

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Equal cardinality supplies no dynamical conjugacy. Output preservation
forces a proposed tracking map here to fix `false`, while transition
preservation would require the incompatible flip. -/
theorem same_cardinality_but_no_tracking :
    Fintype.card Bool = Fintype.card Bool ∧
      ¬ ∃ f : Bool → Bool, Tracks flipMachine inertMachine f := by
  constructor
  · rfl
  · rintro ⟨f, hf⟩
    have hfalse := hf.out_eq false
    have hstep := hf.step_eq false ()
    simp [flipMachine, inertMachine] at hfalse hstep

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/SystemsTheory.lean
/-- A point is globally optimal for a natural-valued objective. -/
def IsOptimal {P : Type*} (J : P → ℕ) (p : P) : Prop :=
  ∀ q, J q ≤ J p

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/SystemsTheory.lean
/-- Exponential policy-space cardinality alone is not an optimization lower
bound: a constant objective makes every Boolean policy globally optimal. Ported
from the Aristotle target `CapacityCaveat`. -/
theorem exponential_policy_space_with_flat_objective (n : ℕ) :
    Fintype.card (Fin n → Bool) = 2 ^ n ∧
      ∀ p : Fin n → Bool, IsOptimal (fun _ => 0) p := by
  constructor
  · simp
  · simp [IsOptimal]

Interpretation. The examples place sharp counterweights beside the tracking and policy-count theorems. Injectivity makes state count a necessary capacity condition, not a sufficient dynamical condition. An exponentially large candidate set is compatible with trivial optimization.

Does not imply. The examples do not show that cardinality is irrelevant. It remains decisive in the lower bounds whose hypotheses force distinguishable states. They show only that cardinality without transition, output, or objective structure cannot establish exact tracking or computational hardness.

The cards use the same separation. In every case the formal statement is narrower than the philosophical use it may suggest. That narrowness makes clear where an interpretation adds a premise and where an analogy leaves the proved domain altogether.

The three layers of the project consequently have different tasks. Philosophy identifies distinctions worth preserving and explains why they matter to an account of difficulty. Mathematics selects formal objects and derives consequences from their structure. Verification checks that those derivations follow under the stated definitions and axioms. Philosophy does not certify a proof, mathematics does not choose its own interpretation, and verification does not establish that a model captures the world. The strength of the account comes from keeping those roles connected without allowing any one of them to silently perform the work of another.

NotesNotes and sources

The relational thesis is not a claim that computational models are arbitrary. Its complexity-theoretic form depends on robustness under efficient simulation and efficient translation between encodings. Turing's machine model provides the historical starting point for the bounded-machine discussion. Myhill and Nerode supply the distinguishable-prefix method behind the finite-state lower bounds. The positional multiplication theorems in the companion are narrower applications of that method, not standard lower bounds for unrestricted multiplication.

The treatment of observation draws on the control-theoretic distinction between state, output, and observer, especially Kalman's formulation and the nonlinear observability work of Hermann and Krener. Blackwell's comparison of experiments is the classical source for ordering information structures by their decision value. The present book uses only elementary consequences of data processing and experiment-indexed equality of finite observation laws. Its PMF models are discrete; its measure-kernel results use the additional measurability structure stated in the companion.

The dynamic specialization belongs to the literature on partially observable control and stochastic reachability. The book deliberately avoids assigning a single complexity classification to POMDP planning without specifying horizon, policy class, input encoding, approximation, and objective. Those distinctions are load-bearing in the results of Papadimitriou and Tsitsiklis, Kaelbling, Littman and Cassandra, and Madani, Hanks and Condon.

The distinction between forward simulation and solution descent is standard in state abstraction, homomorphism, and abstract interpretation. Cousot and Cousot provide the general preservation-oriented view of abstraction. Li, Walsh and Littman classify MDP abstractions by what they preserve, while Ravindran and Barto develop homomorphic transfer for semi-Markov decision processes. The backed-shortcut theorem in this book is one exact graph-level certificate within that older theory of solution transfer.

The compositionality chapter uses structured cospans in the sense of Baez and Courser and the broader categorical account of open systems developed by Fong and Spivak. The mathematical moral is deliberately restrained. Categorical composition supplies structural bookkeeping. Behavioral, probabilistic, cost, and reachability preservation still require property-specific theorems.

The Fermat discussion is historical rather than evidential. Wiles's proof and the surrounding modularity work demonstrate the use of remote mathematical structure, but they do not instantiate a clean separation between free preprocessing and cheap query. The proof also required new construction directed at the problem.

Appendix. Glossary

This glossary gives the meanings used in Hard Problems. The entries are intentionally narrower than ordinary usage. Each names the formal object or relation at issue and, where confusion is likely, states what the term does not establish.

Abstraction
A generally many-to-one map from a concrete domain to a simpler domain, designed to preserve answers to specified questions. In this book, forward preservation alone is not enough for solution transfer: abstract solutions also need a sound return path. Abstraction does not by itself add concrete observations, actions, or reachability. Its abstract graph may contain spurious reachable states unless a lifting condition rules them out. Nor does a smaller abstract state space establish that the abstraction is computable, cheap to construct, or easier to solve.
Adjacency relation
A relation specifying which state-to-state changes count as single admissible steps in a graph model. Local maxima, paths, and barriers are all relative to this relation. Changing adjacency can change the problem rather than merely relabel it.
Admissible action
An action permitted by the stated interface and constraints at the relevant point in a task. An action in the mathematical action type need not be admissible in every state, and a policy theorem is only as strong as the admissibility conditions it enforces.
Barrier
The infimum, over admissible paths between two states, of the deepest loss below the starting objective value along a path. No path gives an infinite barrier by convention. A zero infimum need not have a zero-loss witness when infinitely many paths are available. A barrier is not a property of the endpoints alone: it changes with the adjacency relation and objective. Its magnitude does not by itself give a search-time lower bound or a stochastic escape time.
Belief state
A probability distribution over latent states maintained by an agent under a specified transition and observation model. It can serve as a state for a belief process. Concentration of the belief does not show that the maintained model class contains the real process.
Compactness
The first-order logical principle that a theory is satisfiable when each of its finite subsets is satisfiable. Here it supports separate full models for hypotheses consistent with every finite observation batch. It does not by itself prove that the models agree under every experiment.
Composition
Formation of a larger process or system from compatible parts. The book treats composition only through declared operations, such as function composition or pushout gluing. A property of each component is not automatically a property of the composite. In particular, the existence of a structural composite does not establish that reachability, observability, probability, cost, or safety compose; each requires its own preservation theorem.
Computational difficulty
Failure to compute, select, or describe an otherwise represented solution within stated time, space, query, communication, precision, or related bounds. Physical execution is a separate practical cost. A meaningful claim requires an instance family, encoding, machine model, and resource measure. A large candidate set alone is not a complexity lower bound.
Concretization
A rule or witness that interprets an abstract result back in the concrete domain. In this book, concretization must recover an admissible concrete path and preserve the relevant target, constraint, or cost claim. It need not be an inverse of a many-to-one abstraction.
Constraint
A condition that acceptable answers or trajectories must satisfy in addition to reaching a target. In the dynamic model, a safe set constrains visited states. A reachable target can still be practically unavailable when every route violates a constraint.
Coordinate change
An invertible relabeling that transports transitions, observations, targets, and other relevant structure. Under a supplied conjugacy, deterministic runs, observational indistinguishability, and reachable sets are preserved. Transporting adjacency and the objective also preserves path depths and barriers. Discovering or computing the change need not be cheap.
Decision-critical ambiguity
Observational equivalence between states whose acceptable-action sets are disjoint. It rules out one deterministic action acceptable throughout the equivalence class. This pair condition is a sufficient certificate; the exact criterion for robust infeasibility is an empty total intersection over the class. It does not prove that a resolving experiment or a relaxed randomized solution exists.
Decision problem
A family of instances whose required output is a Boolean answer under a fixed correctness condition. Decision, search, production, and control problems can have different resource requirements even when they concern the same underlying objects.
Deterministic finite automaton (DFA)
A one-pass recognizer with a finite state set, deterministic symbol transitions, and acceptance determined by the final state. The positional multiplication theorem is a DFA state lower bound. It is not automatically a lower bound for a machine that can reread input or use a work tape.
Dynamic system
A state space together with a law governing change over time, possibly under actions and noise. The book's dynamic specialization names observations, a target, and constraints but remains only the environmental core. A complete encounter also fixes initial conditions, policy and resource classes, horizon, risk, and relevant costs.
Encoding
A representation of instances as inputs to a machine, together with the length convention used to define instance size. Complexity claims are relative to encodings but can be robust under efficiently intertranslatable encodings. An encoding that contains the answer changes the comparison.
Epistemic difficulty
Failure of the available information to separate possibilities that matter to success. It is relative to an observation interface and experiment class. More computation over exactly the same identified data cannot restore a missing distinction.
Equivalence class
The set of objects related to one object by an equivalence relation. For observational equivalence, it collects states no admitted experiment distinguishes. A task may need only a quotient of the full state space rather than exact identification.
Exact tracking
A map between deterministic machines that intertwines every transition and reproduces every output. Tracking an output-separated finite machine requires an injective state encoding. This is stronger than approximate prediction or preserving only one task-relevant statistic.
Experiment class
The declared collection of passive or active procedures allowed when comparing states or models. Observational equivalence is indexed by this class. Enlarging it may distinguish states that remain equivalent under passive observation alone.
Expressive difficulty
Failure of the current vocabulary to state a distinction, target, action, or invariant needed for success. It differs from uncertainty about the value of an already represented variable. Showing that an extension can express something does not explain how to discover the extension.
Extension
Addition of structure absent from the old setup, such as a predicate, sensor, state coordinate, action, or axiom. Extension can change what is expressible, observable, reachable, or derivable because it changes the setup. It is not a free coordinate change.
Finite-state machine
A machine whose entire internal configuration ranges over a finite set. Depending on its output structure, it may be a recognizer, transducer, or tracked dynamical machine. A state count of \(2^s\) corresponds to \(s\) bits of capacity, not \(2^s\) bits.
Forward simulation
A map that sends every concrete step to an abstract step and therefore sends finite concrete paths upward. It supports some impossibility arguments, since abstract unreachability can imply concrete unreachability. It does not ensure that an abstract path can be executed concretely.
Functor
A map between categories that preserves identities and composition. A semantics functor makes interpretation compositional in this exact sense. Functoriality alone does not imply faithfulness, information loss, behavioral adequacy, or a numerical measure of discrepancy.
Goal reflection
The condition that if the abstract image of a concrete state satisfies the abstract goal, then that concrete state satisfies the concrete goal. It prevents an abstract target cell from containing concrete failures. Goal preservation in the forward direction is a separate condition.
Indistinguishability
Inability of a declared observation or experiment interface to tell two possibilities apart under a stated comparison. It is always relative to what outputs, histories, policies, and horizons are included. Physical nonidentity does not imply decision-relevant distinguishability.
Instance family
A collection of problem instances organized by an input size parameter. Complexity theory studies how required resources scale over such families. One difficult instance, without a size convention and asymptotic comparison, does not establish computational hardness.
Kernel
A measurable assignment of a measure or probability law to each input state, used here for stochastic transitions and observations. Kernel iteration represents multi-step evolution under the stated stationarity and measurability assumptions. It does not supply an algorithm for computing the resulting probabilities.
Local path lifting
The condition that every abstract step offered from the image of the concrete state actually reached has a concrete successor in the required fibre. Iterating this condition lifts finite abstract paths. Global surjectivity of the abstraction map is not a substitute.
Machine model
A formal specification of available operations, input access, memory, randomness, and output behavior. Time and space claims are meaningful only relative to such a model. Results can gain wider objectivity when they survive efficient simulations between accepted models.
Markov description
A representation in which the next-state law depends only on the present represented state and current action, not on omitted history. State augmentation can restore this property when the added coordinate is sufficient. Merely adding some history does not prove sufficiency.
Observational equivalence
Also called observable equivalence in nearby literature. Two states are equivalent here when every admitted experiment gives the same law over every finite observation history. The Lean ObsEquiv definition currently quantifies over all policies of its type, not a separately encoded feasibility class.
Observation history
The finite sequence of observations available up to a decision point, often together with past actions when those are not reconstructible. A partially observed policy can act on such histories. Equality of one current reading is weaker than equality of all history laws.
Observation law
A function or stochastic kernel specifying which signal is produced from a state. It determines only one part of identifiability, since interventions and transitions also shape future observation histories. A derived statistic cannot make its exact fibres finer.
Observer
A dynamical estimator that maintains and updates an estimate of a latent state from signals and a model. It is appropriate when the decision-relevant condition is hidden. The term is not used as a universal theory of perception.
Partial observability
The condition that the controller receives observations rather than direct access to the full state, so distinct states may induce the same available evidence. It does not imply that every state distinction matters to the task. A sufficient belief or quotient state may be enough.
Path
A finite sequence of states beginning at a specified source, ending at a specified target, and chained by the adjacency relation. In this book paths include their endpoints. Existence, search cost, and traversal cost are separate questions.
Policy
A rule selecting actions, possibly at random, from the information available to an agent. Under partial observability this is normally a function of observation or action-observation history. A state-feedback policy is a stronger information model unless the state or a sufficient substitute is available.
Practical difficulty
Failure of the available transformations to reach success, or failure of every route to respect the constraints. Perfect information does not create a missing move. Practical difficulty is distinct from the cost of searching for a route that does exist.
Predicate
A property \(P:X\to\mathsf{Prop}\) used to distinguish states or define a set. In the vocabulary model, predicates induce an equivalence partition. Adding the target predicate itself proves a comparative expressivity fact but acts like supplying the desired distinction.
Probe
An intervention chosen partly for the information its possible outcomes reveal. A probe can separate models that agree under passive observation, while also changing the trajectory being studied. Existence of one useful probe does not imply that every ambiguity is experimentally resolvable.
Reachability
Membership of a target in the closure generated by admissible finite steps, or its stochastic analogue under a stated success probability. Reachability is relative to the move set, policy class, constraints, horizon, and risk convention. It does not measure search time.
Reflexivity
Dependence of a system's evolution on a representation of the acting agent, such as an inferred policy, together with possible updating of that representation during interaction. Reflexivity is not mere size, drift, or randomness. It can occur in machines as well as in strategic agents.
Resource envelope
The time, memory, query, communication, precision, or other bounds within which programs are admitted. The Lean Envelope formalization treats only a finite admitted program set and proves existence and value monotonicity, not an optimization algorithm or complexity bound.
Robust menu
The intersection of acceptable-action sets over an observational equivalence class. A nonempty robust menu makes exact state identification unnecessary for the immediate decision. An empty menu says only that no single action is acceptable throughout that class.
Saturation
The property that a target set is a union of complete vocabulary-equivalence classes. Saturation is exactly what the companion's fibre-constant Boolean classifier theorem characterizes. It is semantic factorization through a quotient, not syntactic definability or computability.
Semantics
A mapping from formal descriptions to interpreted objects or behavior. In the composition chapter, semantics is functorial when it preserves identities and composition. A semantics may be faithful or forgetful; forgetting is not automatic from the word.
Shortcut
An added edge standing for a path in a base graph. Path backing preserves reachability. The sufficient barrier-preservation certificate used here additionally requires the witness path not to dip below the lower objective value of the shortcut's endpoints; the theorem does not claim this condition is necessary.
State augmentation
Replacement of a state \(x\) by a product state such as \((x,p)\) that includes an additional policy, parameter, or history coordinate. Under the specified update architecture, the first marginal of one augmented step is the supplied transition conditional on the current product state. The projected process need not be autonomous or Markovian. Augmentation need not make the new state observable or tractable.
State-feedback policy
A policy of the form \(\pi:X\to\Delta(U)\) that acts from the current state. It differs from an observation-history policy in a partially observed system. The current finite PMF reachability definition uses this stronger form.
Statistic
A function of existing data used to summarize, denoise, or compress it. Any decision rule factoring through the statistic is constant on its fibres. A statistic can lower computational cost without being informationally finer than its source.
Structured cospan
An open system represented by two boundary maps into a common apex, \(L(a)\to x\leftarrow L(b)\). Compatible cospans compose by pushout when the required pushouts exist. This structural construction does not automatically preserve reachability, probability, cost, or observation.
Task-agent relation
The book's basic unit of difficulty: a task as encountered through an agent's representation, observation and action interfaces, policy class, and resource envelope. Calling difficulty relational does not make it subjective, because each component can impose objective constraints.
Trajectory
A time-ordered sequence of states generated by a transition law and, in controlled systems, a policy. Safe terminal reachability is an event about an entire trajectory, not only its final state. Exact-horizon and within-horizon events are different.
Transition law
A deterministic function or stochastic kernel specifying how a state changes under an action. Reachability and rollout results are relative to this law. A maintained transition law can be misspecified even when inference within it is internally correct.
Vocabulary equivalence
The relation \(x\equiv_{\mathcal L}y\) holding when every predicate in vocabulary \(\mathcal L\) has the same truth value at \(x\) and \(y\). A larger vocabulary can refine these classes. Equality under this relation concerns stated predicates, not all logically or physically possible distinctions.
