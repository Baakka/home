Chapter 0Scope, conventions, and the central claim

This book studies a family of decision problems in which an intervention cannot be selected, evaluated, or safely composed from the representation presently available to the decision-maker.

It does not claim that a company, a city, a software service, and an intimate relationship instantiate the same mathematical object. They do not. It claims something narrower: each may instantiate one or more failure modes familiar from partially observed control, constrained reachability, multi-objective optimization, compositional systems theory, and dynamic games. A mathematical model is useful only if it identifies a discriminating observation, an admissible intervention, a constraint, or a compositional invariant that informal reasoning concealed.

Reasoning by analogy across such systems is itself an evidence-bearing act: transfer succeeds when the structural mapping is made explicit and fails when the resemblance is ornamental. Gick and Holyoak showed that in the laboratory; this book takes it as its brief.

The governing principle is:

A hard problem is a decision problem whose current model omits decision-relevant structure.

The omitted structure may be in the objective, state, observation channel, action set, transition law, constraint set, or policies of other agents. The model is therefore an instrument of diagnosis, not a declaration that the world is simple enough to be optimized.

All state spaces may be finite, continuous, or hybrid. Equations are written in finite-dimensional notation for readability; the intended objects are measurable spaces and stochastic kernels when that distinction matters. Throughout, \(H\) denotes a planning or evaluation horizon; \(T\) is reserved for the transition kernel. Any application to people requires additional constraints that do not follow from the mathematics: consent, legitimacy, duty of care, rights, and the distribution of harm.

The companion formalization covers the load-bearing definitions of chapters 1 through 10, in Lean 4 over mathlib. It is not an ornament; every place where it forced a choice that the prose had left open is now stated explicitly in the text. Chapter 11 runs the entire apparatus on one ordinary case, so the reader can see what each instrument reads when it is actually applied.

Chapter 1The problem object

The chapters that follow all reason about one formal object, defined once here and reused without restatement.

Let the focal agent be indexed by \(0\), with other agents indexed by \(i \in N \setminus \{0\}\). Write \(a_{-0} = (a_i)_{i \neq 0}\) for a joint action of the non-focal agents and \(\Pi_{-0} = \prod_{i\neq 0}\Pi_i\) for their joint policy class; this notation is used throughout. A hard-problem instance is represented as

\[ \mathcal{P} = (S, \{A_i\}_{i\in N}, \{O_i,Z_{i,\theta}\}_{i\in N}, T_\theta, \{G_i\}_{i\in N}, C, \{\Pi_i\}_{i\in N}, \Theta). \]

The terms are:

  • \(S\): state space;
  • \(A_i(s)\): admissible actions for agent \(i\) at state \(s\);
  • \(O_i\): observation space for agent \(i\);
  • \(Z_{i,\theta}(o_i\mid s)\): observation kernel;
  • \(T_\theta(s'\mid s,(a_i)_{i\in N})\): transition kernel, parameterized by \(\theta\in\Theta\);
  • \(G_i\): agent \(i\)'s objective; \(G_0\), written \(G\) when no confusion arises, is the focal objective of chapter 2, and the non-focal \(G_i\) supply the realized payoffs that drive adaptation in chapter 8. Nothing requires the \(G_i\) to be known to the focal agent; chapter 8's counter-moves are hard to anticipate precisely because they are not;
  • \(C\subseteq S\): a safe or feasible set, possibly supplemented by path constraints;
  • \(\Pi_i\): the policy class available to agent \(i\);
  • \(\Theta\): the current model class, encoding uncertainty about the transition and observation kernels.

At time \(t\), the system evolves as

\[ s_{t+1}\sim T_{\theta}(\,\cdot\mid s_t,a_{0,t},a_{-0,t}), \qquad o_{i,t+1}\sim Z_{i,\theta}(\,\cdot\mid s_{t+1}), \]

where \(\theta\in\Theta\) is fixed but unknown. The focal agent acts on an information history

\[ h_t^0=(o_{0,0},a_{0,0},\ldots,o_{0,t}), \]

not on the unobserved state. A policy is a map \(\pi_0:h_t^0\mapsto \Delta(A_0)\).

Uncertainty is not drift. \(\Theta\) encodes epistemic uncertainty: a fixed \(\theta\) the agent does not know but can in principle learn. Non-stationarity is a different phenomenon: a parameter \(\theta_t\) that changes while you study it. Conflating the two corrupts both probing (chapter 9 assumes the target of identification holds still) and belief updating (chapter 3 conditions on a fixed law). When drift is real, model it explicitly, in one of two ways: as dynamics on the parameter, \(\theta_{t+1}=\varphi(\theta_t,\xi_t)\) with \(\varphi\) itself in the model class, or as reflexivity (chapter 8), where the apparent drift is other agents' adaptation and can be re-encoded as state. In this book \(\theta\) is fixed unless a chapter says otherwise.

Admissibility must be checkable. The admissible set \(A_0(s)\) depends on the state, which the focal agent cannot see; a history-typed policy cannot verify its own admissibility against it. The problem statement must therefore satisfy one of three conditions: (a) \(A_0\) is constant; (b) \(A_0\) is measurable with respect to the observation history, so the agent can always tell which actions are open; or (c) admissibility is defined on beliefs, \(A_0(b)\). Otherwise the policy class \(\Pi_0\) is ill-typed. The practical reading matters: "what am I permitted to try, given what I can see" is itself an observability question, and chapter 4 treats the gap between apparent and actual action sets as a first-class diagnosis.

This is deliberately broader than a POMDP. If \(a_{-0,t}\) follows a fixed exogenous law, the model can reduce to a POMDP. If other agents optimize, learn, bargain, or resist, it is closer to a partially observable stochastic game. If the objective is contested, the unresolved issue is prior to either formulation.

The problem object descends from Simon's ill-structured problem, and its partially observed skeleton follows the POMDP synthesis of Kaelbling, Littman, and Cassandra. Chapter 3 and chapter 10 return to each in turn.

1.1 Hardness is a profile, not a scalar

There is no universal scalar \(H(\mathcal{P})\) that measures how hard a human problem is. The relevant output is a hardness profile:

\[ \mathsf{H}(\mathcal{P}) = (\mathsf{F},\mathsf{O},\mathsf{A},\mathsf{L},\mathsf{Ru},\mathsf{Re},\mathsf{X}), \]

where the terms denote formulation, observability, agency, latency, ruggedness, reversibility, and reflexivity. Each term is a diagnostic claim, supported by evidence, not a personality label or a score out of ten.

DimensionFormal locusQuestion
Formulation \(\mathsf{F}\)\(G,C\)Is the objective coherent, feasible, and legitimately chosen?
Observability \(\mathsf{O}\)\(Z,\Theta\)Can observations distinguish states requiring different actions?
Agency \(\mathsf{A}\)\(A_0,T,C\)Is the desired set reachable by admissible action?
Latency \(\mathsf{L}\)\(T,Z\)When does an intervention become observable and evaluable?
Ruggedness \(\mathsf{Ru}\)\(E,J\)Do all admissible routes to better configurations pass through worse ones first?
Reversibility \(\mathsf{Re}\)\(A_0,T,C\)What is the cost of returning to an acceptable region after error?
Reflexivity \(\mathsf{X}\)\(T,\Pi_{-0},\Theta\)How do other agents alter the system in response?

The coordinates are developed in turn: formulation in chapter 2, observability in chapter 3, agency in chapter 4, latency in chapter 5, ruggedness in chapter 6, reflexivity in chapter 8. Reversibility is treated inside chapter 4 (recovery is reachability of the acceptable region, asked backwards) and carries its own weight in the worked case of chapter 11.

The tuple's arity is presentation, not commitment. The coordinates are not seven primitive things. Agency, reversibility, and ruggedness are one diagnostic, constrained reachability, evaluated at three re-descriptions of the same problem: the state system forward, the state system from the states an error would leave you in, and the configuration graph, where the barrier sits below a depth exactly when the target is reachable through the corresponding superlevel region (machine-checked). Latency is observability with a clock: an infinite observability lag against a comparison is precisely an observability failure for that pair (machine-checked). Reflexivity is observability and agency on the augmented system of chapter 8. Formulation alone resists absorption, correctly: it is governance, prior to the system. The profile is therefore an indexed family of diagnostic claims, and extending it means adding an index point, not renegotiating an arity. No higher-dimensional structure is needed for this bookkeeping; by chapter 7's own lowest-dimension rule, a family indexed by re-descriptions is one-dimensional. The genuinely higher question, whether the diagnosis of a composite restricts to the diagnoses of its parts, is chapter 7's, and its answer is no for exactly the properties one would most like it for.

Taxonomies of hardness embed into the profile rather than compete with it. Rittel and Webber's wicked problems concentrate on \(\mathsf{F}\) and \(\mathsf{X}\) with the stopping rule left open, which chapter 10 answers by engineering one. The "super wicked" refinement of Levin, Cashore, Bernstein, and Auld adds four traits, and each is a coordinate read under pressure: a hard deadline on a solution is \(\mathsf{L}\), the absence of a central authority is \(\mathsf{A}\), solvers who are also causing the problem are \(\mathsf{X}\), and present policies that impede future progress are \(\mathsf{Re}\) read forward. What the profile adds to such lists is that its readings come with diagnostics, and, where the companion reaches, theorems.

The first practical deliverable of the book is a model ledger. For every important term, record: the current assumption, evidence for it, competing models, the damage if wrong, and the next observation that would update it. Unwritten assumptions are not absent; they are merely unauditable.

Chapter 2Formulation: objectives, constraints, and governance

Optimization begins only after an objective has been specified. Hard problems often begin before that point.

Let

\[ G:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^k \]

be a vector-valued objective. For a housing system, its coordinates may include availability, affordability, location, emissions, fiscal sustainability, and displacement risk. For an organization, they may include revenue, retention, safety, reliability, learning, and equity.

For a fixed model \(\theta\), a policy \(\pi\) is evaluated by its expected return vector

\[ J^\pi_\theta(s_0) = \mathbb{E}_{\theta,\pi} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{H} \gamma^t G(s_t) \middle|s_0 \right]. \]

Aggregating over the model class. A bare set \(\Theta\) supports no expectation. Three readings of "the value of \(\pi\) under model uncertainty" carry this book, and each chapter names the one it uses:

  1. per-model: report the family \(\{J^\pi_\theta\}_{\theta\in\Theta}\) and reason about it directly;
  2. robust: order policies by their worst case over \(\Theta\) (or over a credal set of priors);
  3. Bayesian: fix a prior \(\mu\) on \(\Theta\) and integrate, \(J^\pi = \mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mu}\,J^\pi_\theta\).

The list is a working set, not a taxonomy, and it is not exhaustive. Ordering by the worst case over a credal set of priors is Gilboa and Schmeidler's maxmin expected utility; working per-model across a scenario ensemble is Robust Decision Making (Lempert, Popper, and Bankes), which then layers a regret criterion on top, itself a further way to aggregate and not one of the three; info-gap robustness (Ben-Haim), which reports the largest uncertainty horizon over which a satisficing outcome is still guaranteed, is yet another; and a survey of the field counts roughly a dozen robustness metrics that rank the same policies differently (McPhail et al.). The obligation this chapter imposes is declaring which reading is in force, not choosing from this particular menu.

The choice is consequential: chapter 4's chance constraint quantifies differently under each, and chapter 9's expected information gain is only defined under the Bayesian reading. The choice is also normative, because it decides whose scenarios count and how much pessimism is owed to the people bearing the risk; it belongs in the ledger.

The discount factor \(\gamma\) and horizon \(H\) are commitments of the same kind. A discount rate is a statement about intertemporal trade-offs: who bears costs, and when. It does not become innocent by being conventional. Record both in the formulation ledger next to the weights below.

Fix a reading, one of the three above or a declared alternative; the rest of this chapter applies to whichever is declared. Without a scalarization, return vectors are partially ordered by Pareto dominance, and policies are correspondingly preordered: two distinct policies with identical return vectors are equivalent under the order without being equal, so antisymmetry can fail at the level of policies (machine-checked by an explicit witness). A scalar objective \(w^\top J^\pi_\theta\), or of its robust or Bayesian aggregate, is valid only after weights \(w\) have been supplied by a legitimate decision process. The mathematics cannot derive those weights from the state of the world.

One structural fact sharpens the diagnostic below. Nonnegative weights respect Pareto dominance: a dominated policy cannot outscore its dominator under any legitimate weighting (machine-checked). Consequently, when two viable policies reverse their ranking under plausible weight vectors, they are necessarily Pareto-incomparable. Ranking reversal is not an arithmetic accident to be tuned away; it is the signature of genuine multi-objective conflict.

Diagnostic: formulation failure

Fix a set \(W\) of weight vectors defensible to the affected parties (the "plausible" weights; naming \(W\) explicitly is part of the formulation). A formulation is inadequate when any of the following holds:

  1. There exist viable policies \(\pi,\pi'\) that reverse their ranking under weights in \(W\), for the declared reading of the return.
  2. The target set is empty under the known constraints: \(G^{-1}(\text{acceptable})\cap C=\varnothing\).
  3. Different stakeholders have non-nested feasible sets or constraints.
  4. The act of intervention changes the goal itself, such as when a city's definition of "success" shifts after displacement becomes observable.

Checkland insisted that a problem situation be formulated only after its competing Weltanschauungen are on the table, and that formulation close on accommodation rather than consensus. (The word "mess," often attached to his method, is Ackoff's.) Operations research walked the same route under the name problem structuring methods: when no single uncontested formulation exists, model the situation with the stakeholders rather than for them, and let the model host a negotiation about what needs to change (Rosenhead and Mingers). Ostrom's design principles name what makes shared systems governable without central control: explicit boundaries, collective-choice arrangements, cheap conflict resolution, nested authority. Steps 2 and 3 below are their procedural shadow.

Procedure: move from optimization to governance

  1. State the objective vector and the hard constraints separately.
  2. Identify the decision rule: weighted aggregation, lexicographic priority, minimax regret, Pareto selection, or negotiated settlement.
  3. Name who has standing to choose the rule, the discount, and the horizon, and who bears the costs of their errors.
  4. Record unresolved conflict as conflict. Do not hide it inside a coefficient.

A careful reader will object that "governance" functions here as an exogenous oracle: a black box outside the state space that resolves incomparabilities on demand. The objection conflates two things. Governance as dynamics is endogenous: the weights and rule in force are state, they drift and are contested, and the augmentation of chapter 8 internalizes them exactly as it internalizes other agents' policies (machine-checked, the same marginal theorem). Chapter 11's case does this in miniature: the bonus rule is a state coordinate the analysis changed. Governance as justification is exogenous by design: no augmentation turns "legitimate" into a theorem, and a formalism that claimed to compute legitimacy would be an instrument of the very laundering this chapter warns against. The oracle is not a gap in the model; it is the model declining to overclaim. (Governance-as-dynamics has its own ancestor: Beer's viable system model treats the rule-choosing function as one more subsystem to be designed, monitored, and starved of none of its variety.)

An objective function can be internally consistent and still be politically or ethically illegitimate. Formal precision is not consent.

Chapter 3Observability: state estimation under model error

Two states may look identical to an agent while requiring different actions. This is the operational core of an observability problem.

That a decision-maker must maintain a model at all is the cybernetic inheritance: Conant and Ashby's good-regulator theorem, that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system, is the ancestor of this chapter's discipline of countermodels. The citation owes its reader a caveat: as proved in 1970 the theorem delivers only extensional equivalence to a model, under perfect state information, a reading weak enough to be nearly vacuous, and the repairs are recent and pull in opposite directions. Wentworth's information-bottleneck reformulation recovers conditions under which a good regulator must genuinely encode a posterior; Virgo, Biehl, Baltieri, and Capucci generalize the other way, making the model observer-relative, imposed on the regulator from outside rather than found inside it. Rosen's anticipatory systems carry the point further: a system that acts on a model of its future is steered by that model's errors. The belief-state formulation below follows Kaelbling, Littman, and Cassandra's synthesis; the sufficiency of the belief state for the history is Åström's result.

A maintained model is a pair \(M=(\theta,\pi_{-0})\). Standing assumption: the non-focal policies in \(M\) are Markov, functions of the current state. This is what licenses folding them into the kernels below. When other agents are adaptive, their policies are history-dependent and their internal states become latent variables; the belief machinery of this chapter is then unsound until those internal states are added to the state space. Chapter 8 gives the augmentation, and the two chapters should be read as a loop: reflexivity is exactly what breaks the naive belief update. That an agent modeling a system it sits inside faces exactly this regress is the embedded-agency problem (Demski and Garrabrant), and the loop is this book's finite-depth answer to it.

Define observational equivalence for the focal agent by

\[ s \equiv_{0,M} s' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathbb{P}_{\theta,\pi_0,\pi_{-0}} (o_{0,0:t}\mid s) = \mathbb{P}_{\theta,\pi_0,\pi_{-0}} (o_{0,0:t}\mid s') \]

for every admissible probing policy \(\pi_0\) and every finite horizon \(t\). This is an equivalence relation (machine-checked). Under model uncertainty, the analyst can require the relation to hold for every live \(M\), or reason with posterior-predictive distinguishability instead. Either way it is deliberately stronger than "the dashboard values match."

def ObsEquiv (M : POSystem S A O) (s s' : S) : Prop :=
  ∀ (π : List O → PMF A) (n : ℕ), obsProcess M π n s [] = obsProcess M π n s' []

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Observational equivalence is an equivalence relation, as the notation
`≡` silently promises. -/
theorem obsEquiv_equivalence (M : POSystem S A O) : Equivalence (ObsEquiv M) where
  refl _ := fun _ _ => rfl
  symm h := fun π n => (h π n).symm
  trans h₁ h₂ := fun π n => (h₁ π n).trans (h₂ π n)

The relation has a control-theoretic ancestor that deserves its name: it is Hermann and Krener's indistinguishability, two states that no admissible input separates through the outputs, with observability defined as that relation's failure. For smooth systems their rank condition makes this chapter's resolution principle concrete: the observation space, everything obtainable from the outputs and their repeated derivatives along the dynamics, either spans enough directions at a state to pin it down or it does not, and no quantity computed downstream of the outputs enlarges the span. Kalman posed the linear case and its duality with control. The stochastic setting used here trades the rank of a derivative span for distinguishability of observation laws, and inherits the same moral.

Now let \(A_M^*(s)\) be the set of actions that are optimal or acceptable in state \(s\) under \(M\). The problem has decision-critical ambiguity if

\[ s\equiv_{0,M}s' \quad\text{and}\quad A_M^*(s)\cap A_M^*(s')=\varnothing. \]

Disjointness is precisely the right threshold. Call an action robust at \(s\) if it is acceptable in every state observationally equivalent to \(s\); the robust actions are exactly the intersection \(\bigcap_{s'\equiv s} A^*_M(s')\). If the acceptable sets across the equivalence class merely differ but still intersect, that intersection is the robust menu and the agent can act. If they are disjoint, no robust action exists (machine-checked), and the agent cannot choose reliably until it acquires a more informative observation or narrows the model class. Probing and model revision are then forced, not optional.

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Decision-critical ambiguity: two observationally equivalent states whose

def DecisionCritical (M : POSystem S A O) (Astar : S → Set A) (s s' : S) : Prop :=
  ObsEquiv M s s' ∧ Disjoint (Astar s) (Astar s')

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Under decision-critical ambiguity no robust action exists: whatever is
acceptable at `s` is forbidden at `s'` and conversely. Probing or model
revision is then forced; no policy choice can dissolve the ambiguity. -/
theorem DecisionCritical.no_robust_action
    {M : POSystem S A O} {Astar : S → Set A} {s s' : S}
    (h : DecisionCritical M Astar s s') :
    ¬∃ a, RobustAcceptable M Astar s a := by
  rintro ⟨a, ha⟩
  have h₁ : a ∈ Astar s := ha s ((obsEquiv_equivalence M).refl s)
  have h₂ : a ∈ Astar s' := ha s' h.1
  exact Set.disjoint_left.mp h.2 h₁ h₂

The dashboard's discriminative power has an algebra. Over the context whose objects are states, whose attributes are observations, and whose incidence is possibility, the formal concepts of Ganter and Wille form a complete lattice, and two states share their observation support exactly when no concept separates them (machine-checked). Decision-critical ambiguity is then a structural mismatch stated in one line: the partition induced by acceptable actions cuts finer than the concept lattice can. The lattice also prices dashboard redesign: derived metrics refine nothing, being functions of the same context, and only new attributes, genuinely new observation channels, move the lattice. (The route from concept analysis to systems diagnosis is Iordache's.)

The lattice is one instance of a general resolution principle, and model theory, the study of the relation between formal descriptions and the structures that satisfy them (Tarski, Hodges), states it cleanly. Treat a representation as a vocabulary of expressible predicates; two states are indistinguishable to the vocabulary when no predicate in it separates them. A target set of states is then decided by some rule phrased in the vocabulary exactly when it is a union of indistinguishability classes (machine-checked); below that resolution, every expressible rule misclassifies some state, whatever its internal sophistication. This splits misspecification into two grades that demand different repairs. A theory that is false in an adequate vocabulary is repaired by updating within the language, which is what the belief update below does. A vocabulary that cannot express the decision-relevant distinction is repaired by no amount of updating: only a new predicate changes what is expressible (machine-checked), and that move is section 7.4's ontological revision, met here for the first time. And when the live hypotheses are axiomatizable theories, ambiguity has a third, harder face. If two rival hypotheses are each consistent with every finite batch of observations, the compactness theorem of first-order logic produces two complete world histories, one satisfying each rival, that agree with the entire observation stream (machine-checked). Some decision-critical ambiguity is therefore not a shortage of data to be probed away; it is a property of the description language, and no experiment schedule expressible in that language will resolve it.

Belief-state update

Given a maintained model \(M\) satisfying the standing assumption, let \(T_{\theta,\pi_{-0}}\) denote the transition kernel after marginalizing over the other agents' (Markov) policies. The canonical belief update is

\[ b_{t+1}(s') \propto Z_{0,\theta}(o_{0,t+1}\mid s') \int_S T_{\theta,\pi_{-0}}(s'\mid s,a_{0,t}) b_t(s)\,ds. \]

When Bayes' rule fails, it fails informatively. The proportionality hides a denominator: the predictive probability of the observation just made. The update is defined only when that probability is positive. If it is zero, every state the maintained model considered reachable assigned the observation probability zero; the model class itself has been refuted. This is not a numerical edge case to be patched. It is the single most informative event the update can produce, and the correct response is a ledger entry and model-class revision (chapter 9), not an exception handler. The companion formalization carries this as an explicit hypothesis on the update, and so should any implementation.

The update also cannot manufacture certainty. Averaged over the predictive law of the observation, the posterior returns exactly the prior prediction (machine-checked): only observations that actually discriminate can move the expected belief. No redesign of the dashboard substitutes for a discriminating signal.

Two theorems turn refutation into procedure, and one honest limit bounds them. The localization: evidence dies exactly when every state the prediction reaches makes the observation impossible (machine-checked), so a dead class points at the support it broke on, which is where any successor class must differ. The hygiene: a model class carrying an ε-contamination component with full support can never die; Bayes then registers surprise as posterior mass flowing to the contamination component, a quantitative misspecification alarm rather than an exception (machine-checked). This is the robust-Bayes tradition of Huber and of Berger and Berliner, and it converts "the model class might be wrong" from a philosophical worry into a monitored coordinate. The limit: no theorem generates the substantive successor class. What survives refutation is a necessary condition (any successor must give the refuting observation positive predictive mass) and a location; the generation of hypotheses is abduction, done by people, informed by the wreckage. Section 7.4 gives that abduction its usual shape: successor classes are minted by re-describing the objects the class ranges over, not by enlarging it pointwise.

In real applications, the difficulty is not writing Bayes' rule. It is that \(T_\theta\), \(Z_{0,\theta}\), and sometimes \(a_{-0,t}\) are uncertain. The book's standard is therefore weaker and more useful: every state estimate must specify its observation sources, its countermodels, and what observation would change the recommended action.

Proxy failure

A metric \(m:S\to\mathbb{R}\) is not a valid target merely because it correlates with a desired outcome in historical data. It must remain informative under intervention. If selecting policy on \(m\) changes the relationship between \(m\) and the outcome, the proxy is not intervention-stable.

The Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial illustrates the distinction. Suppressing ventricular premature depolarizations did not imply lower mortality; the trial was halted after excess mortality and cardiac arrests in treatment arms receiving encainide or flecainide (CAST Investigators 1989; Echt et al. 1991). The visible indicator was real. It was insufficient for the decision.

Procedure

For every primary metric, document:

  • latent condition purportedly measured;
  • distinct states capable of producing the same value;
  • interventions that may decouple it from the target;
  • a second signal that would catch high-cost proxy failure;
  • the observation delay and the population excluded from the measure.

Failure condition

Triangulation does not guarantee identifiability. If live models remain observationally equivalent at affordable scale, use robust policy or a bounded probe that makes them diverge. More reporting is not automatically more information.

Chapter 4Agency: constrained reachability and authority

Observability asked whether the state can be seen. Agency asks whether the state you want can be reached at all.

Let \(\mathcal{R}_{H}^{\pi_0}(s_0)\) be the set of states reachable from \(s_0\) by time \(H\) under focal policy \(\pi_0\). For a target set \(G_\star\), chance constraint \(\alpha\), and a maintained model \(M=(\theta,\pi_{-0})\) in the sense of chapter 3 (reachability is never independent of what the other agents do), define constrained reachability by the existence of a policy satisfying

\[ \mathbb{P}^{\pi_0,\pi_{-0}}_{\theta} \left( s_H\in G_\star \ \land\ s_t\in C\;\forall t\leq H \middle|s_0 \right) \geq 1-\alpha. \]

Under model uncertainty the probability inherits chapter 2's reading: per-model (a reachability verdict for each live \(\theta\)), robust (the constraint must hold for every live \(\theta\)), or Bayesian (the probability is taken under the prior as well). Say which one you are using; a plan that is reachable under the median model and unreachable under a live pessimistic one is not yet a plan.

This is the formal version of a blunt question: do we possess a path to the state we say we want, without violating what we say we will not sacrifice? Reversibility (the \(\mathsf{Re}\) coordinate of the profile) is the same question asked backwards: reachability of the acceptable region from the states an error would leave you in, with the path constraint relaxed.

Finite-horizon stochastic reach-avoid is the standard name for the object just defined: Abate, Prandini, Lygeros, and Sastry solve it by backward recursion, and Summers and Lygeros give the stochastic version its name and its value-iteration treatment. The robust reading descends from minimax reachability of a target tube, Bertsekas and Rhodes's 1971 problem, posed there in a set-membership setting with no chance level, so what carries over is the worst-case-over-a-class move rather than the constraint itself. And reversibility itself is a capture basin in the sense of viability theory (Aubin, Bayen, and Saint-Pierre), whose continuous-time, indefinite-horizon setting is why that literature does not settle the discrete, chance-constrained question asked here.

In a human system, focal actions may be insufficient because needed actions belong to another agent, require consent, or depend on institutional authority. The correct response is not "take ownership." It is to update the action set.

One cybernetic bound governs the whole chapter; Ashby proved it, and this book restates it in the chapter's coordinates. The law of requisite variety, in its counting form: if distinct disturbances produce distinct outcomes under any fixed response, then the number of disturbances a regulator can absorb is at most the number of responses it actually deploys times the number of distinct outcomes that actually occur; holding the outcome constant therefore requires at least as much response variety as there is disturbance variety (machine-checked). An actor whose authority map leaves it few deployable responses is not underperforming; it is under-equipped, and the remedy is authority, coalition, or a wider tolerance, not exhortation.

The authority map

Partition the candidate levers and system drivers that appear in the plan into:

\[ A_{\mathrm{direct}}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{induce}}, \qquad \xi_{\mathrm{exogenous}}. \]

The first set is under direct focal control. The second consists of other agents' actions, inducible only through bargaining, incentives, coalition formation, persuasion, or escalation. The third is not an action at all: exogenous environment parameters and disturbances that enter the transition law as inputs nobody at the table chooses. Keeping the third cell separate matters precisely because plans routinely smuggle drivers in as if they were levers; a driver misfiled as a lever is a step nobody can execute. The three cells partition the plan's candidate entries: every entry receives a classification, and the cells are mutually exclusive. (In the companion's typed form of the map, where classification is a single function into lever, inducible, or driver, both coverage and exclusivity are theorems rather than assumptions, and a driver misfiled as a lever is a type error; machine-checked.)

A manager may control meeting structure, induce a compensation-policy review, and have no control over another person's willingness to disclose harm. A city may control zoning, induce development under specified incentives, and not control interest rates. The distinction changes the problem.

The taxonomy of intervention points, from parameters up through rules, self-organization, and goals, is Meadows's, and this chapter inherits her ordering by leverage. What her taxonomy does not supply, and the authority map adds, is which of those points a given actor is permitted to touch: leverage and permission are different axes, and conflating them is how plans acquire steps nobody can execute.

Procedure

Identify the first unreachable prerequisite. If a culture change requires the ability to alter promotions, but the leadership team cannot do so, the immediate problem is governance or authority, not culture. If an individual needs safety before exploration is possible, the immediate task is protection, not self-optimization.

Failure condition

Reachability analysis becomes a moral failure when it treats people as controllable state variables. Consent and agency are constraints on admissible policy, not inconvenient noise.

Chapter 5Latency: delay, gain, and the cost of fast revision

A state can be correctly estimated and still arrive too late to act on.

A delayed observation model has the form

\[ o_t\sim Z_0(\,\cdot\mid s_{t-d}), \qquad d>0. \]

In practice \(d\) is rarely known and may be state-dependent; an unknown delay is itself a coordinate of \(\Theta\), and the lag forecasts below are then forecasts under each live delay model.

More generally, fix a comparison action \(a'\) (by default the status quo or null action; the choice is part of the analysis and must be stated). The observability lag of \(a\) against \(a'\) at resolution \(\delta\) is

\[ \tau_\delta(a,a') = \inf\left\{ \tau\geq0: D\!\left( P(o_{t+\tau}\mid \operatorname{do}(a)), P(o_{t+\tau}\mid \operatorname{do}(a')) \right) \geq\delta \right\}, \]

where \(D\) is an appropriate distributional distance. If the threshold is never reached, take \(\tau_\delta(a,a')=\infty\): the intervention is unobservable against that alternative at that resolution, which is itself a diagnosis, not a missing value. When a whole set of alternatives matters, the conservative planning quantity is the worst case \(\sup_{a'}\tau_\delta(a,a')\). The quantity is model-dependent; its purpose is to force a forecast of when action becomes distinguishable from non-action. Demanding a stronger signal never shortens the wait: \(\tau_\delta\) is monotone in \(\delta\) (machine-checked).

Procedure: specify a forecast before intervention

A valid intervention plan contains:

  1. the causal pathway from action to state;
  2. earliest leading indicator, expected direction, and expected lag;
  3. earliest terminal outcome and expected lag;
  4. a review cadence appropriate to those lags;
  5. a guardrail that triggers earlier action.

Do not call waiting "respecting the time constant" when the mechanism is unsupported or people are accumulating harm. Delay changes the evidence schedule; it does not suspend constraints. In particular, absence of evidence from an early review is not evidence that waiting is safe.

Chapter 6Ruggedness: barriers in policy space

The landscape metaphor is valid only after the policy space and evaluation relation have been declared.

Let \(X\) be a graph of feasible policy configurations, with edges \(E\subseteq X\times X\) representing one admissible local change. Let \(J:X\to\mathbb{R}\) be a scalar objective, to be maximized. A configuration \(x\) is a (weak) local maximum when

\[ J(x)\geq J(x') \qquad \forall x'\in N(x), \]

where \(N(x)=\{x':(x,x')\in E\}\). Weakness is deliberate: plateaus count, and much organizational life is spent on plateaus.

For a target \(y\) with \(J(y)>J(x)\), the barrier height from \(x\) to \(y\) is

\[ B(x,y) = \inf_{\gamma:x\leadsto y} \max_{z\in\gamma} \left[J(x)-J(z)\right], \]

with three conventions stated rather than assumed. Paths include their endpoints, so \(B(x,y)\geq 0\). If no admissible path from \(x\) to \(y\) exists, \(B(x,y)=\infty\): unreachability is an agency finding (chapter 4) and must stay distinguishable from "reachable but expensive," which is the ruggedness finding. And \(B\) is relative to the declared edge set; adding an admissible move can only lower it, which is why "what counts as one change" belongs in the ledger. One class of additions is exactly neutral: a shortcut backed by an admissible path that never dips below the lower of its two endpoints changes neither reachability nor any barrier height (machine-checked), and the depth clause is essential rather than decorative, since a shortcut dropped over a two-hop dip lowers the barrier the moment it is admitted (machine-checked, by that countermodel). This is the certificate that lets route planners compress a map without lying about it, and chapter 7 meets the same clause again under the name reflection.

Two propositions then hold. If \(B(x,y)>0\), every admissible route to the better state first passes through a configuration strictly worse than \(x\) under \(J\) (machine-checked). Conversely, if some admissible path never dips below \(J(x)\), then \(B(x,y)=0\) (machine-checked): a claimed valley with a painless route around it is not a valley. A third proposition says where valleys come from at all: an additively separable objective on the configuration hypercube has zero barrier to its coordinatewise maximizer, since disagreeing coordinates can be flipped one at a time without the objective ever dipping (machine-checked). Ruggedness is a property of coupling between decisions, Kauffman's K rather than his N: a landscape is only as rugged as its interactions, and a claimed valley in a system whose parts do not interact is a modeling error, not a terrain feature. (Kauffman supplies the landscape; reading K as coupling between decisions rather than between genes is Levinthal's import of it into organization science.)

Category theory belongs in this book only when it gives a stronger account of composition than a generic call to "zoom out."

7.1 Open systems and structured cospans

Let \(\mathsf{A}\) be a category of interfaces, \(\mathsf{X}\) a category of internal systems, and

\[ L:\mathsf{A}\rightarrow\mathsf{X} \]

a functor embedding interface structure into system structure. A structured cospan is a diagram

\[ L(a)\longrightarrow x\longleftarrow L(b). \]

It represents an open system \(x\) with input interface \(a\) and output interface \(b\). When \(\mathsf{X}\) has pushouts, two systems

\[ L(a)\rightarrow x\leftarrow L(b), \qquad L(b)\rightarrow y\leftarrow L(c) \]

compose by pushout along their shared boundary, yielding a system from \(a\) to \(c\). Under the standard hypotheses (\(\mathsf{X}\) with finite colimits, \(\mathsf{A}\) with finite coproducts, and \(L\) preserving finite coproducts; the original construction assumes \(L\) a left adjoint), Baez and Courser's structured cospans support a symmetric monoidal double category of open systems; the comparison with Fong's earlier decorated cospans is due to Baez, Courser, and Vasilakopoulou.

One caveat carries operational weight. Composition of open systems is associative and unital only up to coherent isomorphism, because a pushout is defined only up to isomorphism; that is exactly why the ambient structure is a double category rather than a category (the associator is constructed, not assumed; machine-checked). An architecture diagram that silently treats regrouped compositions as equal is assuming those isomorphisms are identities, and the assumption should be written down, because it fails precisely when boundary identifications carry information.

Composition also decides which safety properties survive it. Invariance-style properties compose: if each component maps the safe set into itself, so does the composite (machine-checked). Margin-style properties do not: two components each moving the state by at most one unit can jointly move it by two (machine-checked). Interfaces can carry guarantees of the first kind; guarantees of the second kind must be re-established at the level of the whole. That the hazard lives in the composition rather than in the components is Leveson's reading of accidents, and chapters 9 and 10 build their guardrails on it. The question of which properties survive composition has an ancestor in Simon's near-decomposability, which asked in 1962 when a system's parts can be analyzed as if independent; the machinery here inherits the question rather than discovers it.

Human organizations can sometimes be partially represented this way. Their handoffs, data schemas, authorization rules, and commitments have interfaces. Their motivations, dignity, and private interpretation do not reduce to ports. The formalism should therefore be used to expose a specific compositional failure, not to assert that the organization has been fully modeled.

7.2 Functorial semantics

Suppose a syntactic category or operad \(\mathsf{W}\) describes admissible wiring diagrams and \(\mathsf{B}\) is a category of behaviors. A semantics functor

\[ \llbracket-\rrbracket:\mathsf{W}\rightarrow\mathsf{B} \]

is useful because it satisfies

\[ \llbracket g\circ f\rrbracket = \llbracket g\rrbracket\circ\llbracket f\rrbracket, \qquad \llbracket \operatorname{id}_a\rrbracket = \operatorname{id}_{\llbracket a\rrbracket}. \]

The equation is not decoration. It says that composing models and then interpreting them agrees with interpreting models and then composing their behavior.

The idea that semantics should be a functor is Lawvere's; the applied tradition this chapter leans on, decorated and structured cospans and wiring-diagram operads, is Fong's, Spivak's, Baez's, and Courser's.

Strictness is the ideal, not the norm. In practice a semantics often fails the equation while retaining a canonical comparison cell \(\llbracket g\circ f\rrbracket \Rightarrow \llbracket g\rrbracket\circ\llbracket f\rrbracket\); such a semantics is lax (or oplax, with the arrow reversed). This is not a defect to be hidden: the comparison cell is a measurement of compositional failure, of the interference, emergence, or context-dependence that the parts' behaviors do not determine. The diagnostic question is therefore not only "is the semantics functorial" but "how far from functorial, in which direction, and does the intervention live inside the gap."

Human interfaces also drift. The wiring survives while the semantics moves: the cospan's legs, the reporting format, the meeting cadence, the declared metric all remain formally intact, and the meaning of what flows through them changes, because chapter 8's counter-moves act on the semantics family rather than the syntax. A contract check is therefore a time-indexed claim: verified at \(t\), it says nothing at \(t+1\) without an invariance argument (machine-checked witness). The gap between a symbol and what it measures is Pattee's epistemic cut, and interfaces sit on it; temporal concept analysis (Wolff) is the formal home for dashboards whose lattice itself moves. The remedies are chapter 8's, not this chapter's: anchors outside any one party's control, sanctioned recalibration windows, and re-verification on a cadence matched to the drift rate, which is what the worked case's contract tests and guild review implement.

7.3 Refinement and 2-cells

A category is inadequate when the application needs to distinguish composition of systems from transformation of their interfaces. A double category can represent:

  • objects: interface types;
  • horizontal 1-cells: open systems;
  • vertical 1-cells: interface maps, refinements, or rewiring maps;
  • 2-cells: squares witnessing compatibility between horizontal and vertical structure.

A 2-cell is not a loose "relationship between relationships." It is a specified commutative square or coherent cell showing that two transformation paths agree. In a systems-engineering application, it can witness that a subsystem refinement preserves the interface contract needed by the larger architecture.

The diagnostic is simple: if a redesign changes both components and their wiring, then it is unsafe to call the redesign local until the compatibility condition has been checked.

7.4 Ontological revision: when abstraction is not enough

When the navigation contract returns its fourth verdict, the target unreachable under the present action set and no probe able to gather separating evidence, the correct response is not to search deeper. It is to abandon the representation. Chapter 10's exit is an exit from a state space, not from a problem.

This is the operational purpose of this chapter's functors, and it is where they stop being bookkeeping. A re-description that respects admissible moves transfers reachability forward, so emptiness in the image refutes an entire technique class at the source (machine-checked); this is the logical schema of the barrier theorems in chapter 11's second profile. And re-description genuinely moves barriers: the same start, target, and evaluation can carry an infinite barrier in one representation and none in another (machine-checked), so unreachability is a property of the representation, not of the problem. The soundness condition is reflection: an escape hatch counts only if success in the new category implies success in the old, and a functor that does not reflect the goal proves nothing, however friendly its codomain. The condition is not new. It is the soundness relation of Cousot and Cousot's abstract interpretation, stated there between a concrete and an abstract domain and given its Galois-connection form in 1979; and the statement across a change of category has its own precedent, since Garrabrant, working with an induced functor between categories of Cartesian frames over a refined and a coarse world model, proves that ensurability and controllability transfer in both directions under exact correspondence while observability transfers in one direction only. What this section adds is the specialization of the condition to a fixed goal predicate and an admissible-move structure, which is what lets emptiness in the image refute a technique class at the source. It is recorded as a definition in the companion because practitioners outside program analysis, who inherit the maneuver without its proof obligation, routinely omit it.

The problem being solved has owners too. An agent whose objective is defined over an old ontology and whose world-model upgrades underneath it faces what de Blanc named an ontological crisis, and the fracturing of concepts across a model refinement is Armstrong's model splintering. de Blanc's target is preservation of the utility function, though the map he transports it along is selected by a structural test, bisimulation combined with divergence between predicted observation laws, which makes his construction a precedent for this section's move rather than a foil to it; Armstrong asks which refactoring of a reward function counts as natural once the features it was defined over splinter one to many; this section's demand is reachability-preservation, and the narrowness is deliberate. A category of models easily leaves its morphism structure underdetermined (Rogers's reading of Armstrong's construction is one worked case: the arrows are not pinned down by the objects, so the intended notion of update has no canonical carrier), so the demand here is stated relative to a fixed goal predicate and the admissible-move structure, not to transport of whole belief states.

Model theory gives the maneuver its established home. A representation is a signature, the stock of predicates a description is permitted to use; abstraction is passage to a reduct, and ontological revision is expansion by new symbols (Hodges). Expansion is exactly the move that changes expressive power: one new predicate separating two previously indistinguishable states makes a target expressible that provably no rule over the old vocabulary decides (machine-checked), which is chapter 3's resolution principle read as an instruction rather than a limit. Reflection, in turn, is the one-predicate shadow of what model theorists require of elementary embeddings, maps that preserve and reflect every formula of the language at once.

Two families of theorems now bracket the maneuver, one from below and one from above. From below, revision provably can pay, and can pay infinitely: a single separating predicate makes a previously inexpressible target expressible, and a re-description can erase an infinite barrier outright (both machine-checked). From above, revision provably never finishes. No countably presented stock of decision rules is complete, because the diagonal target, built by flipping each rule on its own index, escapes every rule in the stock (machine-checked), and the construction applies verbatim to every stage of every hierarchy of such stocks (machine-checked): each new map carries a void of its own. The level-zero void is not hypothetical: whether a program halts is not decidable by any program (machine-checked, resting on the mathlib formalization). The relativized fact is classical and sharper. Grant the machines an oracle for the halting problem and the halting problem for oracle machines reappears one level up, and so on transfinitely, through Turing's ordinal logics and Post's degrees of unsolvability; diagonalization, the same engine checked above, drives every level. The consequence is a boundary on this section's own optimism. Ontological revision is a move that can always be made and can always matter, and there is no representation from which it never needs to be made again: the ladder has no top rung. That is a theorem about the formal model, not a metaphor about the world; what carries into practice is chapter 10's discipline, which was engineered for exactly this shape of non-closure, a stopping rule for the episode precisely because the tower supplies none.

Modifying the state space is the highest-leverage action an agent possesses, and the most dangerous: every re-description forgets something (7.2), and what it forgets is unrecoverable downstream. The traverse contract applies to ontological revision more than to anything else in this book.

7.5 The lowest-dimensional rule

Use an \(n\)- or \(\infty\)-categorical model only when the system's intended equivalences and compositions require higher coherence data. For example, if transformations commute only up to an explicitly relevant equivalence and those equivalences must themselves compose coherently, higher categorical structure may be necessary.

Otherwise, adding dimensions is intellectual theater.

Choose the lowest-dimensional formalism that captures the interfaces, compositions, refinements, and equivalences the intervention must respect.

Categorical procedure

  1. State the applied category, operad, or double category.
  2. Give the interpretation of objects, arrows, composition, and equivalence.
  3. State the semantics or behavior map.
  4. Name the invariant required by the intervention.
  5. Prove, test, or explicitly assume that the map preserves it.
  6. List the information that has been discarded.
  7. Return to the original system with an intervention that depends on the preserved structure.

This is not a generic abstraction ladder. It is a contract of semantic accountability.

Chapter 8Reflexivity: when control becomes a game

The focal action may change the policies of other agents. Let

\[ \pi_{i,t+1} = U_i(\pi_{i,t},a_t,o_{i,t},r_{i,t},\xi_{i,t}), \]

where \(U_i\) is an adaptation or learning operator and \(r_{i,t}\) is agent \(i\)'s realized payoff, derived from its objective \(G_i\) in the problem tuple. Then the transition law is itself policy-dependent:

\[ s_{t+1} \sim T_{\theta}(s_t,a_{0,t},a_{-0,t}), \qquad a_{i,t}\sim\pi_{i,t}(h_t^i). \]

Reflexivity relocates the state; it does not break the formalism. Define the extended state \(\tilde{s}_t=(s_t,\pi_{-0,t})\). The pair evolves as an ordinary Markov process under an extended kernel, and its \(S\)-marginal recovers the original transition (machine-checked). The check covers adaptation operators driven by the focal action, deterministic or stochastic; dependence on observations and payoffs folds into the policy coordinate the same way. What augmentation costs is observability: the new coordinates, the other agents' policies, are unobserved, and the problem returns to chapter 3 with a larger hidden state. This is the loop announced there: adaptive counterparts are why beliefs over \(S\) alone stop being sufficient, and the drift discussion of chapter 1 lands here as well, because what looks like a drifting \(\theta_t\) is often an adapting \(\pi_{-0,t}\) that the model failed to represent. One reflexive channel stays outside even the augmented formalism: the extended state carries the other agents' policies, and the ladder below adds the focal agent's own model-revision rule, but no coordinate holds a focal policy that is itself an optimizer pursuing objectives diverging from the declared \(G_0\). That case is mesa-optimization, an inner optimizer emerging inside the learned policy (Hubinger, van Merwijk, Mikulik, Skalse, and Garrabrant), with the divergence from \(G_0\) its inner alignment failure rather than its definition, and it is flagged here rather than modeled. The flagged case is not formally empty: Turner, Smith, Shah, Critch, and Tadepalli prove that stated environmental symmetries suffice for optimal policies under most reward functions to favor states retaining more reachable options, which is chapter 4's reachability coordinate read as a prediction about the coordinate this formalism declines to carry.

A metric can remain statistically associated with a goal while becoming strategically unreliable once the metric is targeted. This is a mechanism of Goodhart-like failure, not a universal law. Some target measures improve the world; others are gamed, displaced, or made cosmetic. The refutable form of the claim "our metric is intervention-stable" is the absence of a witness: an intervened dynamics under which the metric does not fall while the goal strictly falls. The companion exhibits one such witness in miniature; exhibiting one ends the discussion.

The witness has formal relatives, and they locate what it adds. Manheim and Garrabrant taxonomize the mechanisms that produce the divergence, four ways a proxy comes apart from a goal under optimization pressure; Skalse, Howe, Krasheninnikov, and Krueger prove the static counterpart, that over the full policy space no pair of non-constant reward functions is immune to hacking against each other; and the economic ancestor is Holmström and Milgrom's multitask model, in which pricing the measurable task diverts effort from the unmeasurable one. The witness above is the dynamical member of the family: a single intervened dynamics rather than a classification or a policy-space bound, which is what makes the stability claim refutable case by case.

The warnings have owners. Goodhart's 1975 form: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes. Campbell's: the more a quantitative indicator is used for social decision-making, the more it corrupts the process it was meant to monitor. The familiar aphorism, that a measure which becomes a target ceases to be a good measure, is neither of theirs; it is Strathern's paraphrase. Reflexivity in the wider sense is Soros's, and it is worth stating correctly: not that observation disturbs the system, but that participants' fallible beliefs are part of the system's causal structure, so acting on a belief can help make it true or false.

One fragment of the warning is a theorem rather than a tendency. A decision rule that acts only through a summary statistic inherits the statistic's blindness: if the statistic fails to separate two states whose acceptable-action sets are disjoint, then every rule based on it, deterministic or randomized, acts unacceptably in at least one of them (machine-checked). Managing to the dashboard is exactly as safe as the dashboard is discriminating, which is chapter 3's condition. The reflexive twist this chapter adds is that agents under measurement do not wait for conflated states to occur; they manufacture them.

Procedure: adversarial and participatory pre-mortem

For each intervention, ask:

  1. Which agents gain, lose, or gain exposure?
  2. What is the cheapest way to satisfy the observable rule while defeating its purpose?
  3. To which unmeasured population, subsystem, or time horizon can the cost be displaced?
  4. Which people possess local knowledge about workarounds, harms, or dependencies that the model omits?
  5. What observation distinguishes legitimate resistance from strategic evasion?

Use multiple measures, protected reporting channels, and recurring model review. Involving affected people is not merely ethical; it improves the specification of \(\Pi_i\), \(C\), and the observation model. That monitors be accountable to the monitored is Ostrom's fourth design principle, and it is the institutional answer to metric corruption.

Failure condition

Do not call all disagreement adversarial. A counter-move can be evidence that the policy is unjust, impossible, or based on an invalid model.

Chapter 9Probes: constrained system identification

When \(T\), \(Z\), or \(\Pi\) is uncertain, a large intervention is often a bad first measurement. A probe should be selected for decision-relevant information gain under explicit risk constraints.

The prescription that the complex be met by probe-sense-respond rather than by analysis is Snowden's, as is the safe-to-fail probe itself. The boundary he draws between the complicated and the complex is not rhetoric; it can be stated in this book's terms. Call two live models passively distinguishable when watching alone eventually separates their observation laws, and probe distinguishable when some policy does. Watching is a special case of acting, and there exist model pairs that no amount of passive observation separates while a single intervention does (machine-checked, by an explicit two-state example). The complicated is what analysis can settle from the armchair; the complex is what only probes can settle, and the boundary between them is a property of the model class, not of the analyst's diligence.

Let \(\Theta\) be a live model class and let \(e\) denote a candidate experiment. One idealized criterion is

\[ \operatorname{EIG}(e) = I\!\left(\Theta;O_{t:t+H}\mid e,h_t^0\right), \]

the mutual information between the model class and future observations, conditional on the experiment and current history. Treating \(\Theta\) as a random variable presupposes the Bayesian reading of chapter 2: a prior over models must exist for the criterion to be defined. Where only a credal set is defensible, replace EIG by a robust criterion, such as choosing the probe that maximizes the worst-case distinguishability between the live models.

Raiffa and Schlaifer built the preposterior machinery behind experiment valuation; Lindley proposed mutual information as the value of an experiment; Howard put the practical rule below most sharply: information unable to change an action is worthless. That observation can never lower attainable expected utility is older than all three: it is Blackwell's comparison of experiments. Treating the model class as a random variable is one step short of Duff's Bayes-adaptive construction, which folds it into the planning state outright and was extended to partial observation by Ross, Chaib-draa, and Pineau; and the tension the probe manages, acting to learn against acting to perform, is Feldbaum's dual control.

In practice, use a simpler version: select an experiment only if its possible outcomes would cause different subsequent actions. This is a theorem, not a heuristic: observing before acting never lowers achievable expected utility, and the value of observation is zero exactly when a single action is optimal under every possible outcome (machine-checked, for finite outcome and action spaces). The practical rule is the contrapositive. So is the bookkeeping version: a probe is decision-irrelevant exactly when its recommendation is constant across possible outcomes (machine-checked). The same criterion runs physical searches: infotaxis (Vergassola, Villermaux, and Shraiman) steers by expected information gain when gradients are useless, which is chapter 9 with wind for a state space.

\[ \mathbb{P}^{e} \left( \exists t\leq H:s_t\notin C \right) \leq \alpha, \]

It sits alongside nonmathematical constraints of consent, fairness, and reversibility. Chapter 10The navigation contract and stopping rule

A framework earns its place only if it changes the next action in a falsifiable way.

The output of analysis is a navigation contract:

\[ \mathcal{N} = (\widehat{G},\widehat{b}_t,\widehat{A}, \widehat{\tau},\widehat{C}, \widehat{\Pi},e,\sigma). \]

The terms are:

  • \(\widehat{G}\): the current objective specification and unresolved trade-offs;
  • \(\widehat{b}_t\): the current state estimate and known blind spots;
  • \(\widehat{A}\): admissible actions and their owners;
  • \(\widehat{\tau}\): forecast feedback lags and review cadence;
  • \(\widehat{C}\): guardrails, irreversibilities, and risk distribution;
  • \(\widehat{\Pi}\): anticipated adaptations and counter-moves;
  • \(e\): the next decision-relevant intervention or probe;
  • \(\sigma\): a stopping, escalation, reformulation, or exit condition.

A contract is a finite description, and descriptions have a resolution. By the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem, a first-order specification with one infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality, all satisfying the same sentences and none isomorphic to another (Hodges); even a perfect contract pins the system down only up to what its vocabulary can express. That is not a defect to be engineered away. It is the reason the contract carries \(e\) and \(\sigma\), the probe and the exit, rather than a claim of identity between model and world.

Analysis stops when one of four conditions is established:

  1. Execute: a policy is feasible and robust enough under the stated model class.
  2. Probe: uncertainty is decision-critical but can be reduced within the constraints.
  3. Escalate or negotiate: the required action or objective choice belongs to a different authority.
  4. Exit or declare infeasibility: no acceptable path exists under the present action set and constraints.

The fourth verdict is narrower than it sounds: infeasibility is declared relative to the present representation, and section 7.4 names the move that remains after it, exit from the state space rather than from the problem. The four conditions are findings, and findings can co-occur: a policy can be feasible for the focal agent while the authority to choose the objective sits elsewhere. As a checklist the list above is complete; as a rule it becomes deterministic only under a declared priority order, and the order is itself a governance choice. A legitimacy-first ordering puts escalation above execution (do not execute what you lack standing to choose, even feasibly); an emergency ordering may invert that. Whichever order is chosen, it belongs in the navigation contract as part of \(\sigma\), and in the ledger next to chapter 2's reading and weights. Under any declared order, infeasibility is residual: it is declared only when all three positive findings fail, never while a live option remains (machine-checked under the encoded order). The companion formalization encodes one such order, execute before probe before escalate; a legitimacy-first deployment swaps the first and third tests.

The absence of an intrinsic stopping rule is Rittel and Webber's finding about wicked problems (the word itself is Churchman's, from the 1967 editorial that recorded Rittel's presentation), and this chapter does not overturn it so much as answer it: the rule above stops an analysis episode, not the problem, and it is engineered precisely because the problem supplies nothing to find. That rule's ancestor is Simon's satisficing: search halts when an aspiration level is met, not when an optimum has been demonstrated.

There is no final theorem that resolves human difficulty. Within the formal model the absence is itself a theorem rather than a resignation: every countably presented stock of rules misses a target of its own construction, at every stage of enrichment (machine-checked), so section 7.4's ladder has no top rung even inside the mathematics. There is, however, a discipline: do not promise a solution until you have specified the model, the preserved invariants, the missing information, and the harms your method permits.

Rigor matters because loose metaphor can authorize bad intervention. The formalism's job is not to turn people into plants or policies into proofs. Its job is to show exactly where the representation ends, what must be learned next, and when the responsible move is not optimization but governance, protection, or refusal.

Chapter 11A worked case: the reliability metric

Everything above is machinery. This chapter runs it, end to end, on one case, chosen because it is ordinary: a mid-sized product company whose on-call rotation is burning out while executives push a new reliability target. Names and numbers are illustrative; the discipline is the point.

The presenting problem. After two visible outages, the executive team mandates a company objective: 99.9% availability and a 40% reduction in monthly incident count, tied to engineering bonuses. Six months later the incident count is down 45%, availability is flat, mean time to recovery has doubled, and two senior on-call engineers have resigned. Leadership asks for "a root-cause analysis of engineering culture." The analyst's first move is not an answer; it is the ledger.

Declared conventions, before anything else. The analyst sets terms first. The reading of chapter 2 is robust while the model class below remains unresolved, switching to Bayesian once the probe yields a posterior. The plausible weight set is named: \(W = \{w_{\text{care}}, w_{\text{ship}}\}\), a recovery-and-wellbeing weighting and a velocity weighting, both defensible to the council. The horizon is six quarters, with no further discounting inside it. One admissible change, in the sense of chapter 6, is one service migrated per sprint. The stopping order of chapter 10 is legitimacy-first, stated in full: escalate over probe over execute, with infeasibility residual (the companion's default order is execute over probe over escalate; this case overrides it, declared rather than inherited). Each of these is a ledger row, not a footnote.

Formulation (chapter 2). The objective is a vector: availability, recovery time, delivery velocity, on-call wellbeing, cost. The mandated bonus scalar fails diagnostic 1's precondition before any reversal is tested: it weights raw incident count, which is not a coordinate of the declared vector but a proxy for two of them, so it is a formulation failure of the crudest kind, an objective built on an indicator. Within the declared vector, two viable roadmaps (incremental migration versus the feature roadmap) have Pareto-incomparable return vectors, and two defensible weightings in \(W\) rank them oppositely: a recovery-and-wellbeing weighting favors migration, a velocity weighting favors features. By chapter 2 that reversal certifies genuine multi-objective conflict, so the decision is returned to governance: the engineering council and the executive sponsor adopt a lexicographic rule (a floor on recovery time and paging load first, then velocity), record who chose it, and record the horizon and discount conventions in the ledger. The bonus coupling is severed; that is a chapter 8 decision made in chapter 2.

Observability (chapter 3). The dashboard state (count down, uptime flat) is consistent with three live models on two axes. The reporting axis: declared counts are honest (the system genuinely stabilized), or declared counts are suppressed (downgraded severities, off-ticket fixes). The mechanism axis, which concerns whatever incident load is real: instability is architectural (cascading retries), or it is fed by alert fatigue (a miscalibrated paging floor floods on-call, and exhausted responders let recoverable blips become real incidents). The dashboard summary takes the same value in all three worlds, so the statistic-blindness theorem applies to any rule driven by the dashboard alone, deterministic or randomized. The two axes then part company, and they illustrate chapter 9's boundary exactly. The reporting axis is passively distinguishable once the right second signals exist: a ticket-verified incident rate (support tickets reconciled against declared incidents) and a quarterly postmortem-quality audit separate honest from suppressed reporting by watching alone; that is the complicated cell, and chapter 8 records what the watching found. The mechanism axis is not: no signal in the case's channel set separates architecture from fatigue without intervening on the paging system itself, which is the complex cell, and the chapter 9 probe exists for it. One debt is recorded immediately: the suppression model is an adaptive counter-move, so chapter 3's Markov standing assumption fails for it, and once suppression is confirmed live the belief analysis must be rerun on the augmented state of chapter 8 (system state paired with reporting behavior). A zero-evidence event is pre-registered: a sev-1 outage with no preceding pages refutes the observation model itself and triggers ledger revision, not blame.

Agency (chapter 4). The migration needs two platform engineers who do not exist, and hiring is not something the team controls. The authority map makes the gap precise: direct control covers SLO policy, deploy gates, paging configuration, postmortem format; inducible via the sponsor: headcount for the platform team, roadmap trade-offs, bonus-structure changes; exogenous drivers, entries in the plan that are not actions at all: cloud-provider incidents, the acquisition-driven traffic spike, the market deadline. The first unreachable prerequisite is exactly the headcount. Requisite variety gives the diagnosis teeth, and its hypothesis is checked rather than assumed. The postmortem corpus distinguishes fourteen root-cause classes, audited as producing pairwise distinct outcomes under each fixed response; that audit is the theorem's injectivity premise, not a convenience. The rotation deploys three responses (restart, roll back, escalate) and tolerates four outcomes. Three times four is twelve, and twelve is less than fourteen: the bound is violated, and no rotation of this variety can keep outcomes tolerable across the disturbance inventory. The gap is equipment, not diligence. The verdict for this sub-problem, under the declared legitimacy-first order, is escalation, with the arithmetic attached.

Latency (chapter 5). With hiring escalated, the team still has to decide when to look. Lags are forecast per signal, against a declared comparison action: the rival feature roadmap, not a null action, since doing nothing is not on the table. The probe below shows its leading signal in about six weeks. For the migration, two signals carry different lags: the first slice's change-failure rate is a leading indicator with a one-quarter lag, and the fleet-wide verified incident rate is the terminal signal, invisible for about two quarters (backlog burn-down, then decay of legacy failure modes). Cadences are matched signal by signal: the probe is read at quarter end, the slice indicator at one quarter after funding, the fleet-wide rate at the two-quarter mark and not before. The theorem is quoted to the sponsor who wants weekly readouts of the fleet-wide rate: strictly before that signal's lag, a review of it cannot contain evidence discriminating the migration from the rival roadmap at the declared resolution (one fewer sev-2 per month). What an early review would do is a judgment the theorem does not make. One guardrail triggers earlier regardless of every lag: more than eight paging hours per person per week, measured weekly on every team including the probe teams, pauses feature work. Waiting is not licensed while people absorb harm, whatever the lag structure says.

Reversibility (the R coordinate). Recovery is analyzed before any traverse is authorized. Technical reversal is cheap: each migration slice rolls back in one sprint, and the rollback has been rehearsed. The irreversible axis is human: a departed senior on-call engineer does not roll back, and the two resignations already on the books are the case's only unrecoverable losses to date. The paging cap is therefore the R guardrail as much as the L one, and the hardness profile records R against attrition risk rather than system state. Which makes the choice of route, and not just its depth, the next question.

Ruggedness (chapter 6). Two routes are on the table: migrate everything at once, or migrate one service per sprint. The declared edge set does the first cut by itself: under one service per sprint, the big-bang route is not a path at all, since its single step, all services in flight at once, is not an admissible move, and the council declines to coarsen the edge set to admit it. Even offered arguendo under that coarser edge set, its forecast dip (velocity loss around 20% for two quarters, overlapping rollback windows) exceeds the tolerable depth the council sets at 10%. So the big-bang route is rejected twice over: inadmissible as declared, and too deep even if admitted. The strangler-pattern route is a genuine path in the declared edge set, dipping shallower and longer: about 10% for up to three quarters, one slice in flight at a time. Neither route is dip-free, so the decision rests on admissibility and the depth bound. (Had a dip-free route existed, chapter 6's converse theorem would have dissolved the valley talk entirely; here it merely bounds it.)

The bonus scalar was rejected twice over: it weighted an indicator that is not a coordinate of the declared objective, and within the declared vector a witnessed ranking reversal under defensible weights certified real conflict, sending the choice to governance instead of a spreadsheet. The three classical barriers are, in this vocabulary, framework diagnoses rather than curiosities. Relativization (Baker, Gill, Solovay): there are oracle worlds on either side of the question, and any technique whose verdict is insensitive to which world it inhabits takes the same value in both; since the correct conclusions in the two worlds are disjoint, no such technique decides the question. That is the statistic-blindness theorem verbatim, with the technique as the statistic and the oracle worlds as the conflated states (machine-checked in the general form). Natural proofs (Razborov, Rudich): the naturalness of a lower-bound property is a proxy for hardness that self-defeats; if strong pseudorandomness exists, a usable natural property would break it, so targeting the proxy collapses the proxy. This is chapter 3's proxy failure with a twist of self-reference, and honesty requires saying it is not chapter 8: nothing adapted. Algebrization (Aaronson, Wigderson): the algebraic extension of the admissible move set still lacks the variety to reach the target, an agency finding.

the same value in the two Baker-Gill-Solovay worlds, whose correct

    α (m s) ∉ Astar s ∨ α (m s') ∉ Astar s' := by
  by_contra hc
  push Not at hc
  obtain ⟨h1, h2⟩ := hc
  rw [hm] at h1

The community's actual strategy reads as chapter 9 executed over decades: probes on restricted models, each cheap relative to the terminal question, each reweighting the ledger. Monotone circuits (Razborov) established that non-monotonicity is decision-relevant structure. Bounded-depth circuits established the same for counting. The ACC⁰ lower bound (Williams) was a probe that deliberately routed around the natural proofs barrier, and its methodological payload (algorithm design as a route to lower bounds) mattered more than the specific class. The stopping discipline is the declared order in miniature: a candidate approach shown to relativize or naturalize is exited immediately for that branch, no sunk-cost appeal, because the barrier theorems are machine-grade refutations of the branch's model class.

The contrast is the payoff. The reliability case is moderate on every coordinate; this one is extreme on \(\mathsf{O}\), \(\mathsf{A}\), \(\mathsf{L}\), \(\mathsf{Ru}\) and nearly zero on \(\mathsf{F}\) and \(\mathsf{X}\). The profile does not just measure hardness, it classifies kinds of it, and the corner where only the epistemic coordinates are live is the corner where centuries of mathematical methodology already exist. Mathematics is what navigation looks like when the system does not fight back and nobody disputes the goal; organizations are what it looks like when both luxuries are revoked at once.

Chapter 12Agents as bounded machines

The agent side of the pairing has been carried as a list of capacities: a maintained model, a running estimate, a policy class, and limits on how much can be held in mind and how fast it can be revised. This chapter fixes one formal model for that list and reads the profile through it. The model is the obvious one. An agent is a machine with an observation tape it does not control, an action alphabet it writes to, a resource envelope, and a program. Optimizing over programs subject to the envelope, rather than over actions subject to nothing, is Russell and Subramanian's bounded optimality, and it is the formal content of the claim made in chapter 1 that difficulty is a predicate on a system together with whoever is attached to it.

The reading earns its place by collapsing most of the agent scale and refusing to collapse the rest.

The tuning rungs are one machine. Represent a machine as a step map \(\sigma:S\times I\to S\) with an output map \(S\to O\). A system that tunes the parameters of its own model looks like a different kind of thing: a parameterized dynamics \(\sigma_\theta\) together with a rule retuning \(\theta\) from experience. It is not a different kind of thing. It is one fixed machine on the product \(S\times\Theta\), and the tuned run is exactly the projection of the fixed machine's run, in the state coordinate and in the parameter coordinate alike (machine-checked). Learning a parameter is indistinguishable, from outside, from carrying it as state. The collapse is not free, and its price is the same currency chapter 4 charges: the fixed machine's state space has product cardinality, so every rung folded into the program is paid for in variety.

That settles the lower half of chapter 8's ladder. Maintaining an estimate against a fixed picture, tuning that picture's parameters, and adding variables to it are not three capacities but three programs, and a universal machine runs any of them. What the machine reading leaves standing is a partition into exactly three levels: the program, meaning anything computable from the tapes the agent already has; the interface, meaning the observation channel and the effectors, which are the physical coupling to the system; and the objective, meaning the acceptance condition. Chapter 2 is the third level and is untouched by everything here, which is the machine-theoretic restatement of its exemption in chapter 1.1.

The action wall is hardware. Let the effectors be a set \(G\) of state transformations and let reachability be the finite compositions drawn from \(G\). Three facts hold together. Any admissible trajectory stays inside the reachable set, whatever policy generated it, history-dependent or randomized or arbitrary; admitting a programmed macro, meaning an effector whose effect is already reachable from every state, leaves reachability unchanged; and a genuinely new effector can strictly enlarge it (machine-checked). The first says no decision rule escapes the orbit of its own effectors. The second says software cannot widen the orbit by composing what it already has, however cleverly it dispatches. The third says the wall is not a law of nature but a fact about equipment. Chapter 4's finding, that a target outside your reach yields to adding an action and to nothing else, is this triple.

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Finite-composition reachability: `y` is reachable from `x` by
applying effectors drawn from `G`. -/
inductive Reach (G : Set (X → X)) (x : X) : X → Prop
  | refl : Reach G x x
  | tail : ∀ {y z : X} {g : X → X}, Reach G x y → g ∈ G → z = g y → Reach G x z

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Any admissible trajectory stays inside the reachable set: at each
step some effector in `G` was applied, chosen by an arbitrary policy,
and no choice rule ever escapes the orbit. -/
theorem traj_mem_reach (G : Set (X → X)) (σ : ℕ → X)
    (hstep : ∀ n, ∃ g ∈ G, σ (n + 1) = g (σ n)) (n : ℕ) :
    Reach G (σ 0) (σ n) := by
  induction n with
  | zero => exact Reach.refl
  | succ n ih =>
      obtain ⟨g, hg, heq⟩ := hstep n
      exact Reach.tail ih hg heq

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A programmed macro over `G`: an effector whose effect is, from every
state, already reachable with `G`. This is exactly what software over
the same effectors can implement, including state-dependent dispatch. -/
def Programmed (G : Set (X → X)) (g : X → X) : Prop :=
  ∀ y : X, Reach G y (g y)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Admitting a programmed macro as a new primitive changes nothing: the
reachable set is closed under everything software can add. -/
theorem reach_insert_programmed (G : Set (X → X)) {g : X → X}
    (hg : Programmed G g) (x z : X) :
    Reach (insert g G) x z ↔ Reach G x z := by
  constructor
  · intro h
    induction h with
    | refl => exact Reach.refl
    | @tail y z f hy hmem heq ih =>
        rcases hmem with (rfl | hmem)
        · rw [heq]
          exact Reach.trans ih (hg y)
        · exact Reach.tail ih hmem heq
  · intro h
    induction h with
    | refl => exact Reach.refl
    | tail hy hmem heq ih =>
        exact Reach.tail ih (Set.mem_insert_of_mem g hmem) heq

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A genuinely new effector can strictly enlarge reach: the wall
yields to hardware and to nothing else. -/
theorem exists_new_effector_enlarges_reach :
    ∃ (G : Set (Bool → Bool)) (g : Bool → Bool) (x z : Bool),
      Reach (insert g G) x z ∧ ¬ Reach G x z := by
  refine ⟨∅, Bool.not, false, true, ?_, ?_⟩
  · exact Reach.tail Reach.refl (Set.mem_insert _ _) rfl
  · intro h
    cases h with
    | tail hy hmem heq => exact hmem.elim

The observation wall has a degenerate corner, and it is the calculator. Chapter 3's machinery presumes that observations fail to determine the state. Suppose they do not. When the observation map is injective, observational equivalence collapses to equality, the robust menu at every state is the whole acceptable set, and decision-critical ambiguity cannot occur at all (machine-checked). Every estimator rung of the ladder is then vacuous. A picture of the world is what a machine grows when its input tape stops being the state; when the tape is the state, there is nothing for the picture to do.

This answers the question chapter 1 opened with and did not close. A pocket calculator beats a person at long multiplication, and the reason is not that it sits higher on any scale. Its problem has trivial observability, since the operands are given; trivial reflexivity, since the operands do not care; and trivial reachability, since its output alphabet covers the answers. Every coordinate of the profile is switched off except the resource envelope. The scale was never a ranking of competence because the scale is indexed by what is hidden, and nothing is hidden from a calculator.

What separates the person from the calculator is then a space bound and not a class boundary. Both run the same schoolbook algorithm; the person has roughly four reliable registers, and n-digit multiplication does not fit in a constant number of them. The classical form of that claim is that unary multiplication verification is not accepted by any finite-state machine (machine-checked, by a state-collision argument). This is the formal shape of the fourth diagnostic in chapter 1.1's list, the one detected by varying the size of the problem and watching where performance falls off: degradation with scale, from an agent whose registers do not scale, with nothing structurally wrong anywhere. Give the person paper and the bound lifts, which is the pleasant fact that Turing's 1936 machine was introduced as a model of a human computer with pencil and paper. In this reading the calculator is not an anomaly the framework must explain. It is where the framework's model of an agent came from.

Maplessness is the online case. Chapter 6 insists that a barrier is a property of the objective and the declared moves and not of the search running over them, and the informal reading of ruggedness keeps sliding into a claim about the searcher. The machine reading separates the two cleanly, because the same landscape can be presented to an agent that sees only its neighbours and to one holding the whole map. On a five-vertex path with a two-peaked evaluation, every strict-ascent walk from the start stalls at the lesser peak, an admissible route to the global maximum exists in the same graph, and every such route dips below that peak (machine-checked). All three at once. Being stuck is a property of the local rule, reachability is a property of the graph, and the barrier survives both. The distinction dissolves nothing and licenses nothing: holding the map does not remove the valley, it converts the valley into a priced line item, which is what chapter 6 said and what the short edition of this book states as its thesis. Competitive analysis, in the sense of Sleator and Tarjan, is the mature theory of exactly this gap, and it is the natural home for the ruggedness coordinate when the question is what the shortage of information costs rather than what the terrain contains.

/-- The path graph on five vertices: adjacent iff the indices differ by

def pathAdj (i j : Fin 5) : Prop := i.val + 1 = j.val ∨ j.val + 1 = i.val

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A two-peaked evaluation: a local peak at vertex 1, the global
maximum at vertex 4, and a dip at vertex 2 between them. -/
def J : Fin 5 → ℤ := ![0, 2, 1, 3, 5]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A strict-ascent step: move to an adjacent, strictly better vertex.
Every greedy local searcher takes only such steps. -/
def Ascent (u v : Fin 5) : Prop := pathAdj u v ∧ J u < J v

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Every strict-ascent walk from vertex 0 stalls at vertex 1: the
reflexive-transitive closure of `Ascent` from 0 reaches only 0 and 1,
and no ascent step leaves 1. Being stuck is a fact about the local
rule. -/
theorem ascent_from_zero_stalls :
    (∀ v, Relation.ReflTransGen Ascent 0 v → v = 0 ∨ v = 1) ∧

    intro v hv
    simp [Ascent, pathAdj] at hv
    fin_cases v <;> simp [J] at hv

  · intro v hv
    induction hv with
    | refl => left; rfl
    | tail ih a ih' =>
      rename_i b c
      rcases ih' with ih0 | ih1
      · simp [Ascent, pathAdj] at a

      · simp [ih1] at a

Representation is counted, not judged. Chapter 8 claims that what decides difficulty in the reflexive case is the difference in representational capacity between the two sides. As machines the claim is a pigeonhole. Say a machine tracks another when some encoding of the tracked machine's states into its own intertwines the dynamics and reproduces the outputs. If the tracked machine is output-separated, meaning distinct states are told apart by outputs along some input word, then every tracking map is injective, a finite tracker needs at least as many states as its target, and mutual tracking between output-separated machines forces equal state counts (machine-checked). Steering something strictly smaller is ordinary work with a complicated object. Steering an equal is a different activity, and the difference is arithmetic rather than temperament.

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- `f` tracks machine `B` inside machine `A`: the encoding intertwines
the dynamics and reproduces the outputs. This is what it means for `A`
to maintain a faithful copy of `B`. -/
structure Tracks (A : Machine I O S) (B : Machine I O T) (f : T → S) : Prop where
  step_eq : ∀ t i, f (B.step t i) = A.step (f t) i
  out_eq : ∀ t, A.out (f t) = B.out t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Tracking an output-separated machine is injective: the tracker must
hold distinct internal states for distinct tracked states. -/
theorem Tracks.injective {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (hB : B.Separated) : Function.Injective f := by
  intro t₁ t₂ hft
  apply hB t₁ t₂
  intro w
  have eq1 : f (B.run t₁ w) = A.run (f t₁) w := h.run_eq t₁ w
  have eq2 : f (B.run t₂ w) = A.run (f t₂) w := h.run_eq t₂ w
  rw [← h.out_eq (B.run t₁ w), ← h.out_eq (B.run t₂ w), eq1, eq2, hft]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- The pigeonhole bound: a finite machine can faithfully track an
output-separated machine only if it has at least as many states.
"Something below you on the scale cannot represent you." -/
theorem card_le_of_tracks [Fintype S] [Fintype T]
    {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (hB : B.Separated) :
    Fintype.card T ≤ Fintype.card S := by
  exact Fintype.card_le_of_injective _ (Tracks.injective h hB)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Mutual faithful tracking forces representational parity: if each of
two output-separated finite machines tracks the other, their state
counts are equal. Steering an equal is a different activity from
steering something smaller. -/
theorem card_eq_of_mutual_tracks [Fintype S] [Fintype T]
    {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S} {g : S → T}

    (hA : A.Separated) (hB : B.Separated) :
    Fintype.card S = Fintype.card T := by
  exact le_antisymm (card_le_of_tracks hg hA) (card_le_of_tracks hf hB)

theorem towerCard_unbounded {s p : ℕ} (hs : 1 ≤ s) (hp : 2 ≤ p) :
    ∀ N : ℕ, ∃ k : ℕ, N < towerCard s p k := by
  intro N
  have hmono := towerCard_strictMono hs hp
  exact
    ⟨N + 1,
      lt_of_le_of_lt (hmono.id_le N) (hmono (Nat.lt_succ_self N))⟩

What the reading does not buy. It supplies no algorithm, and the intractability results of chapter 8 are unaffected: bounded optimality is a criterion, not a construction, and the honest belief space remains what it was. It says nothing about which envelope a given human or institution actually has, which is an empirical question this book does not answer and should not pretend to. And it leaves the third level exactly where chapter 2 put it, because no amount of machinery over the first two levels selects an acceptance condition. The machine reading sharpens the walls and prices the rungs. It does not choose the target, and a formalism that claimed to would be making the error this book has been warning about since its first page.
