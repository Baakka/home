The argument in brief.

Operational difficulty concerns what can be achieved in a specified encounter: with these observations, actions, methods, resources, and success conditions. Its mathematical representation is an attainable region, not a hidden scalar. Information, control, abstraction, and complexity explain different limits on that region. The fibred order records how regions can be compared when their performance coordinates differ.

The book's practical contribution is an audit: specify the encounter, calculate attainable performance, justify translations, and establish which conclusions survive them. Its worked examples expose a missing energy constraint, a deployment-induced error, and a proof method that fails without increasing optimal cost. Chapter 6 offers a relational-dispositional interpretation of these constraints. The operational argument does not require that metaphysical interpretation.

Difficulty is often spoken of as though it were stored inside a problem. A conjecture is hard, a landscape is rugged, a system is complex. Yet the same calculation is routine with writable memory and impossible for a fixed finite machine that must retain an unbounded input. The same state is recoverable through one sensor and indistinguishable through another. The same target is reachable with one actuator and absent from the closure of another move set.

A common problem-object can remain while the encounter with it changes. If the success condition changes, even the task may have changed. For that reason this book predicates operational difficulty primarily of an encounter, not of a bare object. Schematically, write

\[ E=(Q,\mathsf{Succ},\Omega,\mathsf{Info},\mathsf{Act}, \mathsf{Prog},\mathsf{Perf}). \]

Here \(Q\) is the problem-object or instance family; \(\mathsf{Succ}\) declares success or loss; \(\Omega\) is the admitted environment and uncertainty class; \(\mathsf{Info}\) and \(\mathsf{Act}\) are the observation and action interfaces; \(\mathsf{Prog}\) fixes the admitted programs and machine model; and \(\mathsf{Perf}\) evaluates executions in an ordered performance space. This tuple is a checklist, not a universal datatype: its internal mathematics changes across theorem proving, control, estimation, and computation.

The framework uses information, action, and resources as three operational boundaries. Information governs which differences can enter a representation. Action governs which transformations and states are available. Resources govern which attainable performance can be realized in time, memory, communication, precision, or energy. Success and environment are separate parts of the encounter against which those boundaries are evaluated. The decomposition is useful but neither unique nor an exhaustive metaphysics. Communication, for example, can constrain both information and resources; institutions can constrain information, action, and resource access at once.

This does not make difficulty imaginary. A locked door does not open under a better description. A false conjecture does not become true for a weak prover. Noise can erase distinctions whether or not an observer understands why. Once an encounter has been specified, its attainable performance is a matter of fact. The specification itself, however, still owes justification. Choosing average instead of worst-case loss, treating preprocessing as free, admitting one environment family rather than another, or valuing time while ignoring energy can express purposes, conventions, and institutional or ethical priorities. The framework therefore separates two layers:

\[ \text{selection and justification of the encounter} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{objective consequences within it}. \]

Its central thesis is deliberately limited:

Operational difficulty is objective and relational. Once an encounter is specified, its attainable-performance structure is a matter of fact, and difficulty comparisons are induced by that structure. Structural facts may explain or certify difficulty; normative and pragmatic considerations help select the encounter; justified transport or invariance licenses broader comparisons.

This operational thesis is not an exhaustive metaphysics of everything called hardness. A graph may have an intrinsic connectivity property, a theorem a proof-theoretic strength, and a space a topological obstruction. Such facts can cause or certify operational difficulty, sometimes uniformly across a large class of encounters. But the further claim that an object is hard remains elliptical until it says hard to accomplish what, with which access and actions, under which limits. The book rejects an unqualified hardness attributed to a bare problem, not intrinsic structure or context-independent truth. Chapter 6 develops a compatible metaphysical proposal: hardness as encounter-borne modal resistance with grounds distributed across the problem-object, environment, interfaces, admitted realizations, resource limits, and their relations. Attainable profiles represent this resistance without defining it into existence.

The practical contribution is an eight-step audit for difficulty claims:

  1. Identify and individuate the encounter. State the raw specification, structural signature and its reduct, claim language, and transformations being treated as identity, equivalence, directed transport, or extension.
  2. State success. Declare the target, loss, tolerance, horizon, and quantifiers.
  3. Declare the environment. Fix the instance family, uncertainty, and admissible worlds.
  4. Expose the boundaries. State the information, action, program, and resource interfaces.
  5. Determine attainable performance. Compute, estimate, or bound the outcomes realizable under those declarations.
  6. Justify cross-context alignment. Relate tasks, worlds, interfaces, and performance coordinates before transporting a claim.
  7. Search for stable content. Ask which equalities, orders, bounds, feasibility claims, or safety properties survive the admitted alignments.
  8. Separate specification from consequence. Do not let mathematical correctness stand in for empirical relevance or normative legitimacy.

The fibred order is the mathematical implementation of steps 5 to 7, not the philosophy by itself. Within one comparison context, encounters determine attainable regions, ordered by inclusion and generally only partially ordered. Across contexts, performance maps reindex ambient specifications by preimage. But such a map is admitted only after an encounter-alignment argument relates the tasks and worlds it is supposed to compare. Reindexing realized classes then requires a further closure or realization theorem.

This division gives the construction one job. Ordinary Pareto analysis compares alternatives already expressed in one performance space; ordinary model-relative complexity fixes one machine and one family. Neither alone justifies a claim transported from simulator to hardware, from abstraction to execution, or from one success criterion to another. The framework separates semantic comparability, performance transport, and realizability transport.

The argument proceeds from representation and feedback, through robustness and fault, to reachability, abstraction, complexity, cross-context comparison, environments that react to being modeled, and problem families whose nearby members stop being reliable guides. The mathematics is used locally. No universal scale or datatype is offered, and no machine-checked theorem is allowed to stand in for the philosophical, empirical, or normative argument that gives it a reading.

Chapter 1Representation is a state in a feedback loop

Description and operative state

A representation can be a sentence, diagram, coordinate system, probability distribution, learned feature vector, proof library, or physical mark. These objects differ too much for a single useful syntax. What matters here is a narrower role: an operative representation is whatever state carries selected consequences of past interaction into the next inference or action.

Let \(X\) be a task-relevant exogenous unknown and let \(Y_1,Y_2,\ldots\) be observations. The information theorem below does not cover a future target state that the actions themselves causally alter; such a problem needs a causal or directed-information formulation, in the sense introduced for feedback channels by Massey (1990). Put every random coin used by the estimator or sensing policy into a random tape \(W\), assumed independent of the pair \((X,Z_0)\), and write \(H_0=(Z_0,W)\). At round \(t\), write the maintained representation as

\[ Z_t=q_t(H_0,Y_1,\ldots,Y_t). \]

The map \(q_t\) is causal. It may describe an observer state, a posterior, a compressed transcript, or an external notebook included in the agent's extended state. An estimate is

\[ \widehat X_t=g_t(Z_t), \]

and an action can be chosen as \(A_t=\pi_t(H_0,Y_{<t})\). The random tape does not initially supply information about the unknown, but it must remain in the history. Once it selects sensing actions, discarding it from a conditional mutual-information expression can make the expression false.

This formulation separates representation quality from the amount of prose used to describe it. A large state can preserve irrelevant distinctions. A small sufficient statistic can preserve exactly those needed by one loss function. Compression is therefore task-relative: the same quotient can be exact for one decision and destructive for another.

There is already a preorder here before probability enters. A deterministic experiment \(o_f\) refines \(o_c\) when \(o_c=k\circ o_f\) for some post-processing map \(k\). Equality of fine reports then forces equality of coarse reports, so the fine compatibility class is smaller. Consequently, every action robust under the coarse report remains robust under the fine one. Mutual factorization preserves the compatibility classes and every robust action set. A two-state quotient can make the inclusion strict by merging states that require different actions. Distinct relabelings can occupy the same position, so antisymmetry requires identifying mutually factoring reports. This deterministic refinement preorder is the noiseless factorization case of Blackwell's comparison of statistical experiments: the coarse report is obtained by post-processing the fine report. Blackwell's theorem treats stochastic experiments and all decision problems; the elementary result here uses deterministic reports and declared robust-action sets. It is distinct from the Shannon data-processing inequality used below.

Feedback cannot invent information

Because \(Z_t\) is constructed from the initial state and the transcript, the data-processing inequality in Shannon's information framework gives

\[ I(X;Z_t) \le I(X;H_0,Y_{1:t}) =I(X;H_0)+\sum_{k=1}^{t} I(X;Y_k\mid H_0,Y_{<k}) =I(X;Z_0)+\sum_{k=1}^{t} I(X;Y_k\mid H_0,Y_{<k}). \]

The equality is the chain rule. In an adaptive experiment, the distribution of \(Y_k\) can depend on earlier actions. The conditional information terms must therefore be evaluated under the actual policy, with its random tape in the conditioning set. Calling a sensor noisy is not enough to determine them. Actions generated from the recorded history do not create information about \(X\) by themselves.

For a fixed policy, suppose each round contributes at most \(C\) units of task-relevant information. To obtain a lower bound for an entire experiment class, the same bound must hold uniformly over every admissible adaptive policy. Assume that \(I_0\) and \(C\) are finite, and measure all information with one logarithm base. Then

\[ I(X;Z_t)\le I_0+tC, \qquad I_0=I(X;Z_0). \]

This caps what can enter the representation; an update rule may retain less.

To connect information with accuracy, fix a distortion \(d(x,\hat x)\) and use Shannon's fidelity-criterion formulation to define the rate-distortion function

\[ R_X(D)= \inf_{P_{\widehat X\mid X}:\,\mathbb E d(X,\widehat X)\le D} I(X;\widehat X). \]

For the real-valued subtraction and division below, also assume that \(R_X(D)\) is finite.

If \(\widehat X_t\) achieves expected distortion at most \(D\), its information about \(X\) is at least \(R_X(D)\). Since \(\widehat X_t\) is computed from \(Z_t\), another application of data processing gives

\[ R_X(D)\le I(X;\widehat X_t)\le I(X;Z_t). \]

For \(C>0\), any estimator meeting this expected-distortion criterion under the declared source law therefore obeys

\[ t\ge \left\lceil \frac{[R_X(D)-I_0]_+}{C} \right\rceil . \]

If the initial representation already contains enough information, this lower bound is zero. If \(C=0\) while \(R_X(D)>I_0\), the target accuracy is unattainable through that declared policy and feedback channel. If the bound on \(C\) is uniform over the admitted policy class, the impossibility is class-wide. The ceiling is not philosophical decoration: observations arrive in whole rounds.

A defensible application must justify the source distribution, distortion, initial information, experiment class, and uniform per-round bound.

A scalar Gaussian reconstruction

The simplest exact example makes the dependence on noise visible. Let a fixed scalar state have prior

\[ X\sim\mathcal N(m_0,P_0), \]

and let independent observations be

\[ Y_k=X+V_k, \qquad V_k\sim\mathcal N(0,R), \qquad P_0>0, R>0. \]

Gaussian conjugacy adds precision. After \(t\) observations the posterior variance is

\[ P_t=\left(P_0^{-1}+tR^{-1}\right)^{-1}. \]

For a positive target variance \(D\), the condition \(P_t\le D\) requires

\[ t\ge R\left(D^{-1}-P_0^{-1}\right). \]

The integer lower bound is the ceiling of the positive part. Increasing the measurement-noise variance \(R\) increases the number of observations needed for the same reduction in uncertainty. This is the clean core of the Kalman analogy: reconstruction speed is limited by the precision entering through feedback.

Observability is local geometry; detectability is dynamics

The scalar formula is not a theorem about a dynamic Kalman filter. For an LTI system \(\dot x=Ax,\ y=Cx\), the rank test comes from Kalman's control-system formulation: observability is tested by the vertical stack

\[ \mathcal O_L= \begin{bmatrix} C\\ CA\\ \vdots\\ CA^{n-1} \end{bmatrix}. \]

A smooth nonlinear system has no universal matrix with that finite form. For \(\dot x=f(x,u),\ y=h(x)\), let \(f_{\bar u}(x)=f(x,\bar u)\) for each constant admissible input. Let \(\mathcal G\) be the smallest real vector space that contains the output components and is closed under Lie differentiation by every \(f_{\bar u}\). Its observability codistribution is

\[ \mathcal O(x)= \operatorname{span}\{d\phi(x):\phi\in\mathcal G\} \subseteq T_x^*M. \]

For an autonomous system, this is the span of \(d(L_f^k h_j)(x)\). A coordinate matrix made from finitely many such covectors can be useful computationally, but there is no universal \(n-1\) truncation. Controlled systems can require mixed iterated Lie derivatives along all admitted system vector fields, not repeated derivatives along the drift alone.

The Hermann-Krener observability rank condition

\[ \dim\mathcal O(x_0)=\dim M \]

is sufficient for local weak observability at \(x_0\) over the declared common input experiments. Its scope matters: \(\dot x=0,\ y=x^3\) is observable although \(dh(0)=0\), and pointwise local observability does not by itself give a global result.

If the full codistribution has constant rank \(r<n\) on a neighborhood, its annihilator

\[ \mathcal N_x=\mathcal O(x)^\circ =\{v\in T_xM:\alpha(v)=0\text{ for every }\alpha\in\mathcal O(x)\} \]

is a smooth \((n-r)\)-dimensional involutive distribution. Frobenius integrates it into local connected leaves on which every function in \(\mathcal G\) is constant. When the observation algebra represents the admitted common-input experiments, points connected within such a leaf cannot be separated by those observation functions. This conclusion is local, and the leaves are generally curved sets rather than linear unobservable subspaces; Frobenius alone does not make a local leaf a global indistinguishability class. The neighborhood and constant-rank assumptions do the work; rank deficiency at one point supplies only a kernel of the differential test.

Observability asks whether output separates states. Detectability asks what happens to distinctions that output does not separate. One incremental form requires two forward-complete trajectories with the same admitted input and the same output history to satisfy

\[ d(x_a(t),x_b(t))\longrightarrow0. \]

In a regular observability decomposition, this is asymptotic convergence of hidden trajectories along each indistinguishability leaf. Bounded hidden trajectories may still remain separated. A fixed residual radius gives practical detectability, while

\[ \limsup_{t\to\infty}d(x_a(t),x_b(t)) \le\rho(\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty), \qquad \rho(0)=0, \]

gives a robust disturbance-dependent version. Detectability removes one obstruction to state reconstruction; observer construction, stabilization, a nonlinear separation principle, and safety remain separate problems.

Indistinguishability is task-relative

Information bounds concern degree. A complementary boundary concerns exact ambiguity. Fix an admissible experiment class. Two states are observationally equivalent when every experiment in that class produces the same law of finite observation histories. No computation on such a history can distinguish the states.

Exact recovery may still be unnecessary. If each state \(x\) admits a set \(A^*(x)\) of acceptable actions, an observation class \(E\) permits a robust choice exactly when

\[ \bigcap_{x\in E} A^*(x)\ne\varnothing. \]

The relevant representation need only preserve distinctions on which acceptable action sets conflict. This is why information must be called task-relevant. Bits about an irrelevant coordinate can be plentiful while a single decision-critical distinction remains absent.

The experiment class is part of the claim. Equivalence under all mathematical history-dependent policies can be finer than equivalence under experiments that a bounded agent can implement. Forgetting that quantifier turns an exact theorem into a claim about a different interface.

Chapter 2Robust reconstruction has a margin

Nominal convergence is not robustness

An observer can converge inside its design model and fail after a small change in the plant or sensor. Noise perturbs observations under a maintained law; mismatch changes the law itself. A fault enters the disturbance model through a declared class and magnitude.

Consider a nonlinear plant and a Luenberger-type observer written schematically as

\[ x_{t+1}=f_t(x_t,u_t)+w_t, \qquad y_t=h_t(x_t)+v_t, \]
\[ \widehat x_{t+1} =\widehat f_t(\widehat x_t,u_t) +K_t(\widehat x_t) \bigl(y_t-\widehat h_t(\widehat x_t)\bigr). \]

The plant's output geometry and convergence of this particular observer are two analyses. Suppose the second yields a scalar comparison inequality for the estimation error \(e_t\ge0\):

\[ e_{t+1}\le(q_0+\mu)e_t+\bar d. \]

Here \(q_0\ge0\) is a certified nominal contraction factor, \(\mu\ge0\) bounds the part of model mismatch that scales with current error, and \(\bar d\ge0\) bounds additive effects such as disturbances, sensor noise after observer gain, bias, and bounded fault. There is no universal metric that makes every model difference into this \(\mu\); the norm and derivation of the comparison inequality are the system-specific work.

Write \(q=q_0+\mu\). If \(0\le q<1\), iteration gives

\[ e_t\le q^t e_0 +\bar d\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}q^k =q^t\left(e_0-\frac{\bar d}{1-q}\right) +\frac{\bar d}{1-q}. \]

The certified ultimate tube has radius

\[ r_\infty=\frac{\bar d}{1-q_0-\mu}. \]

The two effects of mismatch are now separate. Multiplicative mismatch consumes the nominal contraction margin. Additive disturbance enlarges the upper bound that this comparison argument can certify. The certified tolerance is

\[ \mu<1-q_0. \]

The certificate is valid while this margin is positive; beyond it the comparison proof is silent. The radius \(r_\infty\) is an upper guarantee, not an accuracy floor. Establishing a floor requires a worst-case or minimax lower bound.

The same inequality gives an accuracy condition. To certify \(\limsup_{t\to\infty}e_t\le D\) for \(D>0\), it is sufficient that

\[ \bar d\le D(1-q_0-\mu). \]

Thus faster nominal contraction, mismatch tolerance, and final accuracy share one margin. They cannot be read independently from \(q_0\), \(\mu\), and \(\bar d\). If \(r_\infty<D<e_0\) and \(0<q<1\), the transient bound reaches \(D\) once the natural-number time satisfies

\[ t\ge \left\lceil \frac{\log\bigl((D-r_\infty)/(e_0-r_\infty)\bigr)}{\log q} \right\rceil . \]

If \(D\ge e_0\), time zero already satisfies the bound. The formula makes a familiar tradeoff explicit. Increasing observer gain can reduce a nominal contraction factor while amplifying measurement noise and therefore increasing \(\bar d\). Speed in the noiseless model need not improve the certified robust accuracy guarantee. The finite \(\bar d\) premise is a deterministic bounded-disturbance assumption. Gaussian noise is unbounded and needs a moment or high-probability reformulation.

Changing models and switching regimes

A family of plants \(\{f_\theta,h_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}\) admits a uniform robust claim only when one common error metric or Lyapunov function gives the comparison inequality over the declared family. If the same bounds \(q<1\) and \(\bar d\) hold at every step, including every admitted switch, then the error tube survives arbitrary variation inside that family. Equal constants proved separately in mode-dependent metrics do not establish that claim. A switching theorem may instead need common Lyapunov data, dwell time, or reset bounds; these are standard alternatives in the switched-systems literature surveyed by Liberzon and Morse (1999).

Abrupt change can alter the observability codistribution or the stability of its hidden dynamics. Gain cannot recover a distinction absent from the output. An observer bank enlarges the hypothesis class while spending memory, computation, and identification effort. Any maximum divergence claim is therefore indexed by a chosen mismatch set and norm.

Accommodation, detection, and isolation

The separation of accommodation, detection, and isolation belongs to the model-based fault-diagnosis tradition reviewed by Venkatasubramanian et al. (2003). Accommodation means that the estimator or controller retains an accuracy or safety guarantee while the fault lies in an admissible class. The error tube above is an accommodation result when fault effects are included in \(\bar d\).

Detection asks whether a fault can be distinguished from ordinary uncertainty. Let a residual be

\[ r_t=y_t-\widehat h_t(\widehat x_t). \]

Suppose every healthy residual satisfies \(\lVert r_t^h\rVert\le\rho\), and a faulty residual has the additive form \(r_t^f=r_t^h+f\). Declare an alarm when \(\lVert r_t\rVert>\eta\). A threshold \(\eta\ge\rho\) avoids false alarms under that deterministic model. The reverse triangle inequality gives the worst-case sufficient condition

\[ \lVert f\rVert-\rho>\eta. \]

At the smallest threshold certified from this radius bound alone, \(\eta=\rho\), the familiar condition is

\[ \lVert f\rVert>2\rho. \]

This factor of two is the worst-case separation of the declared uncertainty tubes. Probabilistic false-alarm rates require a probability model.

Isolation asks which fault occurred. It requires the residual sets of candidate faults to be separated from one another, not merely from the healthy set. A fault may be detectable and still indistinguishable from another fault. If candidate \(i\) produces residuals in a closed ball of radius \(\rho\) around a signature \(f_i\), then \(\lVert f_i-f_j\rVert>2\rho\) makes the two candidate balls disjoint. The same geometry that governs task-relevant representation returns here: diagnosis is possible only when the observation interface preserves the distinctions the diagnosis is asked to make.

Robust reconstruction therefore has an informational boundary and a dynamical boundary. Observability determines which distinctions can enter the output, and information rate limits how quickly recoverable uncertainty can contract. Detectability asks whether the remaining ambiguity decays; stability margin limits how much mismatch and disturbance that contraction can tolerate. Neither boundary can be replaced by confidence reported from inside the maintained model.

Chapter 3Knowing does not create a path

Reachability is generated by actions

Reachability, invariance, and viability are distinct set-valued questions in control; Aubin's viability theory is a standard reference for their state-constrained forms. Here, let \(X\) be a state space and let each primitive action \(u\in U\) induce a transition \(F_u:X\to X\). The states reachable from \(x\) are

\[ \operatorname{Reach}_U(x) =\{F_{u_n}\circ\cdots\circ F_{u_1}(x):n\ge0,\ u_i\in U\}. \]

The empty composition includes \(x\). This set depends on the action boundary, not on how clearly the agent represents it. A complete map can settle whether a target is reachable and can shorten search, but it does not add an endpoint to the closure.

A derived action \(g\) also need not enlarge reachability. If

\[ g(x)\in\operatorname{Reach}_U(x) \quad\text{for every }x, \]

then treating \(g\) as one primitive step changes program length but not the reachable set. This condition is weaker than having one uniform finite macro for \(g\): the witnessing path may depend on \(x\), may have no common length bound, and may be noncomputable. A genuinely new actuator can enlarge reachability, but then the action boundary has changed.

Theorem proving supplies a sharp instance. Let the states be proof states over a fixed axiom and hypothesis context \(\Gamma\), let the primitive actions be the inference steps of the calculus, and let \(g\) be a lemma already provable from \(\Gamma\). Introducing \(g\) as one primitive step is a backed derived action: every use of \(g\) can be replaced by its derivation, so the set of provable goals does not change. What can change is cost. A goal whose direct derivation exceeds a search budget may fall inside the budget once \(g\) is a single step. Reachability preservation therefore does not imply preservation of finite-budget accessibility; the budgeted attainable regions of Chapter 5 are the coordinates that record what the lemma actually bought.

This separates epistemic and practical obstruction. A state can be perfectly known and unreachable. A state can be reachable while the agent lacks enough information to choose the path. The two failures can coexist, but neither explains the other.

Barrier depth is not search time

Reachability says that some path exists. It does not say what every path must cross. Let \(J:X\to\mathbb R\) be an objective, with larger values preferred, and let \(\gamma\) range over finite admissible paths from \(x\) to a goal set \(G\). Define the depth of a path relative to its starting value by

\[ \operatorname{depth}_J(\gamma) =\max_i [J(x)-J(\gamma_i)]_+, \]

and define the barrier

\[ B_J(x,G) =\inf_{\gamma:x\leadsto G}\operatorname{depth}_J(\gamma), \]

with value \(+\infty\) when there is no path.

A zero barrier means that paths can avoid a drop below the starting objective arbitrarily closely; under a witnessed monotone path it is exactly zero. A finite positive barrier means every path dips. An infinite barrier means no path exists. These are statements about the declared adjacency relation and objective.

Barrier depth records a bottleneck. Length, energy, escape probability, and search effort are separate coordinates: a zero-depth path may be extremely long, and a short path may have a deep dip.

The representation of a path can hide this distinction. An abstract edge may stand for a long concrete route. It preserves reachability if that route exists, but it preserves barrier depth only if the route satisfies an appropriate score bound. This is the point at which abstraction incurs a debt.

Chapter 4Behavior comes before its presentation

Systems as admitted trajectories

A differential equation, automaton, state-space realization, and input/output split are presentations of a system. In the behavioral approach of Willems (2007), the central object is instead a time domain \(T\), a declared signal space \(W\), and a set of admissible trajectories

\[ \Sigma=(T,W,\mathcal B), \qquad \mathcal B\subseteq W^T. \]

Equations matter insofar as they characterize \(\mathcal B\). Distinct internal models can present the same behavior at the declared signal boundary, even when one exposes a useful proof or algorithm that another hides. The equivalence is relative to the chosen variables, boundary, and trajectories.

When two components constrain the same signal universe, their behavioral interconnection is

\[ \mathcal B_1\parallel\mathcal B_2 =\mathcal B_1\cap\mathcal B_2. \]

If only some variables are shared, composition instead enforces compatibility over that interface, represented by a pullback or fibre product. Hiding a signal through \(\pi:W\to V\) takes the existential image

\[ \pi_*\mathcal B =\{\pi\circ w:w\in\mathcal B\}. \]

Interconnection removes trajectories; hiding identifies them. The operations do not generally commute:

\[ \pi_*(\mathcal B_1\cap\mathcal B_2) \subseteq \pi_*\mathcal B_1\cap\pi_*\mathcal B_2, \]

and the inclusion can be strict because the two projected memberships may use incompatible hidden witnesses. Two nonempty components can even have an empty interconnection. Composition therefore needs both compatibility and viability, not merely componentwise possibility.

An exact coordinate change transports a presentation while preserving the admitted behavior. An abstraction is many-to-one and can identify or introduce behavioral possibilities unless transfer conditions are proved. An extension adds a sensor, actuator, predicate, variable, oracle, or axiom and therefore changes the encounter.

The two directions of transfer

Simulation relations are a standard proof method for transferring behavior; forward and backward variants are treated systematically by Lynch and Vaandrager (1995), and their use for discrete abstractions of hybrid systems is surveyed by Alur, Henzinger, Lafferriere, and Pappas (2000). For the finite reachability setting here, let \(x\to_X x'\) and \(a\to_A a'\) be concrete and abstract step relations. Forward simulation requires

\[ x\to_X x' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \phi(x)\to_A\phi(x'). \]

It maps every concrete path to an abstract path. With preservation of goals, it proves that concrete success implies abstract success. Its contrapositive can certify concrete impossibility from abstract impossibility.

Executing an abstract plan requires the reverse direction, expressed by a local step-lifting condition:

\[ \phi(x)\to_A a' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \exists x',\ x\to_X x'\ \text{ and }\ \phi(x')=a'. \]

The quantifier starts from the current state \(x\), rather than a convenient representative of the same abstract cell. Induction then lifts a finite path one step at a time.

Goals need both directions as well. For concrete goal \(G_X\) and abstract goal \(G_A\), exact reachability transfer uses

\[ x\in G_X\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\phi(x)\in G_A. \]

Forward simulation, step lifting, and goal equivalence together give

\[ x\leadsto_X G_X \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \phi(x)\leadsto_A G_A. \]

Each premise has distinct work. Forward simulation gives sound abstract images of concrete behavior. Lifting prevents spurious abstract plans. Goal reflection prevents a non-goal concrete state from being declared successful only because its cell contains a goal.

These are strong reachability conditions. Approximate and probabilistic abstractions replace equality with error, value, kernel, or distributional bounds. Safety, cost, and barrier claims require corresponding bounds.

Local behavior and safety

Let \(\mathcal B(U)\) denote trajectories admitted on a region \(U\) of time. Restriction gives presheaf-like data. A sheaf of behaviors additionally requires compatible admitted local trajectories to have a unique admitted global glue. This use of sheaves for dynamical systems has a developed compositional treatment in Schultz, Spivak, and Vasilakopoulou (2020); the present manuscript uses only the elementary restriction-and-gluing distinction.

For a state predicate \(C\), define

\[ \operatorname{Safe}(C) =\{w:\forall t,\ C(w(t))\}. \]

Then the temporal safety claim is simply

\[ \mathcal B\models\Box C \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathcal B\subseteq\operatorname{Safe}(C). \]

For discrete deterministic dynamics, if \(\mathcal B_C\) consists exactly of the runs of \(F\) starting in \(C\), then

\[ \mathcal B_C\models\Box C \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F(C)\subseteq C. \]

Thus an all-time safety statement reduces to a one-step invariant for this declared behavior. A plant-controller interconnection is safe only when its closed behavior satisfies the inclusion. Nonblocking or viability remains a separate obligation: the empty behavior satisfies every universal safety claim vacuously.

Chapter 5Complexity and the fibred order

A bound needs a family and a machine

The dependence of a complexity measure on programs, encodings, and machines is classical; Blum's axiomatic treatment (1967) is an early general framework. An asymptotic resource bound concerns a family \(\{\mathcal I_n\}\), an encoding and size parameter \(n\), a machine or access model, a success or loss criterion, and a resource measure. Separately defined instance-level notions, including Kolmogorov and instance complexity, also exist. Time, workspace, queries, samples, communication, random bits, precision, and physical energy are not interchangeable until a conversion model is supplied.

The missing specifications matter. A one-pass stream and a rereadable array offer different information access. A random-access word machine and a Turing machine charge different primitive operations. Exact production and verification ask different questions. Worst-case, average-case, and smoothed analysis use different input quantifiers or distributions. A large search space by itself is not a lower bound under any of them.

Rather than force these resources into one number, fix a model and let each admissible program \(p\) have a resource vector \(\operatorname{Res}(p)\) and a loss vector \(\operatorname{Loss}(p)\), with the desired instance quantifiers already built into those functions. Take each coordinate in the extended nonnegative reals, order vectors componentwise, and treat smaller resource and loss values as better. Define

\[ \mathsf{Ach} =\{(r,\ell):\exists p, \operatorname{Res}(p)\preceq r, \ \operatorname{Loss}(p)\preceq\ell\}. \]

This attainable region is upward closed: a program meeting one pair of bounds also meets every looser pair. Its nondominated attainable points, when they exist, are Pareto-minimal. Implementation/resource spaces and Pareto antichains are already central to Censi's mathematical theory of co-design; the construction of \(\mathsf{Ach}\) is not claimed as new. Limiting tradeoffs need not be attained; they lie on the lower boundary of the closure rather than necessarily in \(\mathsf{Ach}\). More time may buy lower error. More measurements may reduce time. More memory may replace recomputation. Some tradeoffs are impossible because the information or action boundary excludes them before computation begins.

For one resource called time and one distortion target \(D\), define the extended-valued slice \(\tau(D)\in[0,+\infty]\) by

\[ \tau(D) =\inf\{t:\exists p, \operatorname{Time}(p)\le t, \ \operatorname{Loss}(p)\le D\}. \]

If the set is empty, \(\tau(D)=+\infty\). The information lower bound from Chapter 1 is a lower bound on such a slice when observations are the limiting resource. The robust recurrence from Chapter 2 certifies an ultimate tube but, without a matching adversarial lower bound, does not make smaller errors unattainable. Reachability can make the goal loss unattainable regardless of computation. These results constrain different slices of an attainable region when they are assembled in one context. They are not competing definitions of a hidden scalar.

Worked example: one robot, several encounters

Consider an autonomous warehouse robot that must leave a loading bay, choose one of two corridors, and reach a dock without collision. This is one task, but not yet one well-defined difficulty claim. We must say which layouts are admitted, what the robot senses, which motions it can execute, what uncertainty is allowed, and which costs count.

Start with two possible layouts, \(L\) and \(R\). In \(L\), the left corridor is blocked; in \(R\), the right corridor is blocked. A forward camera gives the same report at the fork in both layouts. Its observation class is therefore \(\{L,R\}\). If turning right is safe only in \(L\) and turning left is safe only in \(R\), the set of first turns safe for every compatible layout is

\[ \{\mathsf{right}\}\cap\{\mathsf{left}\}=\varnothing. \]

The obstacle has not made motion physically impossible. The current information boundary has made a certified first turn unavailable. A side looking lidar refines the report into the singleton classes \(\{L\}\) and \(\{R\}\), so each report has a robustly safe turn. This is the deterministic information preorder of Chapter 1 in operational form. If lidar is optional and all other interfaces stay fixed, every camera-only controller is still admissible; the attainable region can only expand. If a fire door closes both corridors, however, more sensing does not help. Unless the robot can open the door or take another route, the target lies outside its reachable set. That is an action boundary, not an information defect.

The sharp two-layout model is still too friendly. Let the estimated lateral position have error \(e_t\), let localization dynamics contract nominally by \(q_0<1\), let model mismatch contribute \(\mu\), and let bounded disturbance contribute \(d\). A certificate of the form

\[ |e_{t+1}|\le(q_0+\mu)|e_t|+d, \qquad q_0+\mu<1, \]

gives an ultimate localization tube. A path with smaller clearance lacks a robust certificate even when its nominal trajectory is collision-free. Performance must therefore record a bound under the declared uncertainty class. Better sensing may shrink the tube; the uncertainty quantifier remains.

The planner operates on a grid map rather than raw camera frames and motor currents. Let \(a:X_{\mathrm{hw}}\to X_{\mathrm{grid}}\) send hardware states to grid cells. Forward simulation,

\[ x\to_{\mathrm{hw}}x' \Longrightarrow a(x)\to_{\mathrm{grid}}a(x'), \]

shows that executed hardware paths have valid abstract images. Planning needs the other direction: each abstract step selected by the planner must lift from the current hardware state to an admissible hardware motion. A diagonal grid edge through a narrow aisle may fail this test because of turning radius, localization error, or acceleration limits. Goal reflection is separate again: an abstract cell labelled "dock" must contain only hardware states that meet the physical success condition, or the abstraction can report success too early. The useful abstraction theorem combines forward simulation, step lifting, and goal reflection for the declared planner interface.

Now put the alternatives in one hardware comparison context. Let lower values be better in

\[ P_{\mathrm{hw}} =\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\,4} \quad\text{with coordinates}\quad (\text{time},\text{energy},\text{memory},\text{clearance loss}). \]

For the following calculation, take these illustrative values as each package's exact worst-case performance point in the declared model:

Each point generates every looser bound above it. The lidar controller is faster than that camera controller but uses more energy and memory. If lidar is a mandatory hardware package, its power and memory costs can make its attainable region cross the camera region. If it is merely an optional observation available to the same program class at no charged interface cost, the camera encounter's region is included in the lidar encounter's region because lidar can be ignored. Finally compare simulation with hardware. Let the simulator's nonnegative performance bounds be

\[ P_{\mathrm{sim}}=\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{\,3}, \qquad (s,n,z) =(\text{motion steps},\text{peak live planner nodes}, \text{normalized clearance loss}). \]

Alignment must first relate layouts, observations, executable moves, and dock success. A simulator that omits an admitted fire mode or offers an unexecutable move cannot be repaired by converting units. For the following calculation, assume this alignment and an exact calibration: one motion step costs two seconds, each live planner node occupies two MB, and clearance loss is normalized by 0.1 metre. \[ u(t,e,m,c)=(t/2,m/2,10c). \]

\[ A_{\mathrm{sim}}=\uparrow(6,10,0). \]

Its inverse image is exactly

\[ u^{-1}(A_{\mathrm{sim}}) =\{(t,e,m,c):t\ge12,\ m\ge20\}. \]

The missing energy condition is visible: \(u\) forgets energy. The hardware lidar policy in the table instead generates

Now declare a comparison family consisting of hardware encounters in which every successful docking run uses at least ten Wh, and use their successful attainable regions. No encounter in this family realizes the displayed preimage: every nonempty successful region excludes its zero-energy witness. This is a concrete failure of preimage closure. The family restriction is essential; it concerns the stipulated docking package, not all possible robots or the camera packages above. Nor is the conclusion obtained merely by showing that this one lidar policy fails to realize the preimage.

There are three different achievements here. Alignment relates the tasks and admitted worlds. Calibration makes performance bounds comparable. Realizability would supply an admitted encounter with exactly the translated region. The first two have been assumed for this calculation; the third fails in the declared hardware family.

Ordinary Pareto order already compares the three hardware packages. The fibred formulation adds a place to record the different simulator coordinates and the precise question whether their inverse-image specification is realizable. It earns its use here by exposing the energy constraint that a coordinate translation silently dropped, not by replacing the underlying control or optimization theories.

The fibred order of difficulty

A comparison context \(c\) fixes a common encounter schema: task, instance and environment quantifiers, uncertainty class, admissible programs, information and action interfaces, and resource and loss coordinates. Its declared encounters instantiate that schema. Let \(P_c\) be the resulting ordered performance space. The ambient fibre is the powerset

\[ \mathcal R_c=\mathcal P(P_c), \]

ordered by inclusion. Let \(\operatorname{Enc}(c)\) be the declared encounters to be compared. Attainability is a map

\[ \mathsf{Ach}_c:\operatorname{Enc}(c)\longrightarrow\mathcal R_c \]

whose values are upward-closed regions. Within this one context, define

\[ E_1\mathrel{\unrhd_c}E_2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathsf{Ach}_c(E_1)\subseteq\mathsf{Ach}_c(E_2), \]

and read \(E_1\unrhd_c E_2\) as "\(E_1\) is at least as hard as \(E_2\)." The relation is reflexive and transitive, but it need not be antisymmetric on encounters. Two different plants, observation channels, or presentations can have exactly the same attainable region. Quotient by

\[ E_1\sim_c E_2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathsf{Ach}_c(E_1)=\mathsf{Ach}_c(E_2). \]

The quotient is order-isomorphic to the realized image:

\[ \operatorname{Enc}(c)/{\sim_c} \ \cong\ \operatorname{im}(\mathsf{Ach}_c) \ \subseteq\ \mathcal R_c. \]

This quotient is the realized subposet of the ambient fibre. The ambient powerset may contain unrealized regions, while the chosen performance coordinates intentionally forget other distinctions between systems.

The realized order is typically partial. Crossing Pareto frontiers witness incomparability, and an added scalarization can rank them differently.

To relate ambient fibres, choose a category \(\mathcal C\) of contexts. Its arrows are not arbitrary coordinate maps. An arrow \(f:c\to d\) is admitted only after an encounter alignment has justified the relevant relation between problem or task, success predicates, environment families, observation and action interfaces, and resource conventions. The strength and direction of these components may differ: an implication of success is weaker than an equivalence, and forward simulation is weaker than executable lifting.

The formal region layer retains from this certificate a performance-point map \(u_f:P_c\to P_d\), compatible with identities and composition. It assumes the semantic licensing argument; it does not manufacture one. Preimage gives

\[ f^*:\mathcal R_d\longrightarrow\mathcal R_c, \qquad f^*(A)=u_f^{-1}(A). \]

It is monotone and strictly contravariant:

\[ (\operatorname{id}_c)^*=\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal R_c}, \qquad (g\circ f)^*=f^*\circ g^*. \]

Thus the ambient data form a strict contravariant indexed poset

\[ \mathcal R:\mathcal C^{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow\mathbf{Pos}. \]

Preimage always preserves inclusion. It reflects inclusion whenever \(u_f\) is surjective:

\[ f^*(A)\subseteq f^*(B) \Longrightarrow A\subseteq B. \]

A bijection of performance coordinates is sufficient for reflection and gives an order isomorphism between ambient fibres, but bijectivity is stronger than reflection needs.

Once semantic alignment and performance calibration have licensed \(f\), even without closure of realized regions it permits a heterogeneous comparison of two given encounters:

\[ E_c\mathrel{\unrhd_f}E_d \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathsf{Ach}_c(E_c) \subseteq u_f^{-1}\bigl(\mathsf{Ach}_d(E_d)\bigr). \]

This compares the pair without constructing a source encounter. Restricting \(f^*\) to realized subposets requires the additional preimage-closure condition:

\[ A\in\operatorname{im}(\mathsf{Ach}_d) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_f^{-1}(A)\in\operatorname{im}(\mathsf{Ach}_c). \]

One sufficient way to prove this closure and choose source representatives is an encounter transport \(T_f:\operatorname{Enc}(d)\to\operatorname{Enc}(c)\) satisfying

\[ \mathsf{Ach}_c(T_fE) =u_f^{-1}\bigl(\mathsf{Ach}_d(E)\bigr). \]

The Lean companion packages this stronger witness. Identity and composition then follow at the region level. Without preimage closure, reindexing remains an operation on ambient powersets.

This contravariant preimage must be separated from a lossy direct image. For a many-to-one map \(q:P_c\to P_d\), the covariant operation

\[ q_!:\mathcal R_c\longrightarrow\mathcal R_d, \qquad q_!(A)=\{q(x):x\in A\} \]

is monotone but can send distinct subsets to the same image and therefore fail to reflect inclusion. Noninjectivity causes that collapse; reflection by inverse image instead depends on surjectivity.

The formal contexts, performance maps, and reindexing laws are declared data, but their use carries layered proof obligations. Encounter alignment licenses semantic comparison; the performance map transports ambient specifications; preimage closure transports realized classes; and chosen encounter transport is stronger still. Together they yield a partial, context-indexed order rather than a global ranking.

The ingredients above have substantial precedents. The proposed contribution is the encounter-audit protocol and its use of the fibred order to implement attainability, justified transport, and the search for stable claims. It is not a new theory of orders, fibrations, or Pareto frontiers.

Blackwell (1953) compares experiments through the decision risks they support. Medvedev degrees and Weihrauch reducibility compare problems by uniform procedures that transform solutions or realizers. Those orders can distinguish computational content even when two encounters have the same resource/loss region. Conversely, two problems of the same Weihrauch degree can have different time, memory, energy, or robustness envelopes under a chosen machine and environment. The present order is therefore not a substitute for a reducibility degree.

The nearest engineering precedent is monotone co-design. Censi's framework already has functionality, implementation, and resource posets, feasibility relations, Pareto antichains, composition, and feedback; a subsequent extension treats uncertainty. It is more developed than this book's attainable-region construction as a design calculus. The narrower question asked here is what licenses comparison when the performance spaces or encounter interfaces themselves change. The answer separates an ambient inverse-image comparison from closure of the realized family under that inverse image. The book has no general tensor or conversion calculus and does not replace them either.

The same honesty applies to the categorical language. Contravariant reindexing, indexed posets, and their associated fibrations are standard; see Lawvere (1969), Bénabou (1985), and Jacobs (1999). Restricting an ambient indexed family to selected subfibres requires those subfibres to be stable under reindexing. Calling that requirement preimage closure makes the modelling debt visible but does not make the categorical fact new.

The defensible originality claim is consequently modest. The book combines: an encounter audit separating specification from consequence; attainable regions within each context; encounter alignment before cross-context comparison; and a distinction between ambient performance transport and transport of realized classes. The warehouse example carries those levels through one system. Whether this synthesis is useful can be tested by whether it catches invalid simulator-to-hardware, abstraction-to-execution, or model-to-plant transfers that a less explicit account leaves tacit, and by whether it identifies nontrivial claims stable across a justified comparison class. No claim of historical priority is made.

Finite envelopes and missing algorithms

Let \(E\) be a nonempty finite set of admissible programs and let \(V(p)\) be their value. A maximizer exists because the envelope is finite. If \(E\subseteq F\), the optimum over \(F\) cannot be worse. A strict gap can occur when an excluded program is better than every member of \(E\).

The optimum is internal to the declared envelope. Enlarging the envelope may improve value while making search more expensive; an infinite envelope may attain no optimum at all. The same distinction separates existence of a sufficient statistic or coordinate equivalence from its computable and efficient construction.

State counts are information-retention bounds

Finite-state lower bounds become meaningful when the interface is exact. Say a target machine is output-separated when, for every distinct pair \(s\ne t\), there exists a finite input word \(w\) after which their outputs differ. Any machine tracking it exactly needs an injective encoding of those states. Thus

\[ |S_{\mathrm{target}}|\le |S_{\mathrm{tracker}}|. \]

This is a capacity condition, not a theory of understanding. Equal state counts do not guarantee a tracking map, and a smaller quotient may preserve everything needed for one task.

The state-separation argument is the elementary core of the Myhill-Nerode tradition. The positional multiplication example in the formal companion is even more specific. For \(b\ge2\) and \(n>0\), a one-pass register machine reads two \(n\)-digit base-\(b\) operands, retains \(k\) registers with \(r\) values each, and emits the product only from its final register state. Fixing the second operand to one turns the task into delayed reproduction of the first. Distinct first operands must therefore end in distinct configurations:

\[ b^n\le r^k. \]

If all configurations fit into \(m\) bits, then \(n\le m\). The lower bound is about retaining an operand under a one-pass, final-output interface. It is not a lower bound on multiplication in standard complexity classes. Rereadable input, auxiliary storage, or incremental output changes the model and can invalidate the argument.

The conversion from states to bits is essential. A machine with \(2^s\) states has \(s\) bits of state capacity, not \(2^s\) bits. Exponential state counts and linear bit bounds are the same statement in different units.

Where cost is paid

Representations can move work across time. An index, abstraction hierarchy, compiled circuit, or proof library may be expensive to construct and cheap to query. A fair resource vector separates preprocessing from online cost and states whether the artifact is amortized across instances. Treating a problem-specific artifact as free advice changes the computational model.

The relocation is measurable only after the acquisition protocol has been declared. Counting the cost of using a supplied lemma while omitting the search that found it measures conditional proof cost, not end-to-end discovery cost. The formal proof-cost infimum below deliberately begins after a proof language, admitted-proof predicate, and cost function have been fixed. It does not manufacture a metric for discovering those choices.

A comparison may grant the same offline artifact to both agents, charge its construction once, or study nonuniform families with size-specific advice. Each convention defines a different attainable region.

Complexity theory earns objectivity not by removing models but by declaring them and proving invariance or simulation relations across a comparison class. The model dependence is controlled, not denied.

Chapter 6Modal resistance and conditional objectivity

A grounded relational metaphysics

The operational framework asks: under conditions \(C\), what can be achieved? A metaphysics asks a different question: what kind of thing is hardness, and what makes a hardness fact true? The proposal here is grounded relational dispositionalism:

Hardness is the modal resistance to realizing a specified success condition within an encounter. It is borne by the encounter and may be grounded in the structure of its problem-object, environment, interfaces, admissible realization class, resource limits, or relations among them.

The language of real dispositions follows the tradition developed, among others, by Mumford (2003); Vetter (2015) develops the broader connection from dispositions and potentialities to modality. Applying that vocabulary to hardness is the proposal of this chapter, not a result quoted from those works.

Bearer and grounds must be kept apart. Operational hardness is borne by the encounter:

\[ \operatorname{Bearer}(H)=E. \]

Its grounds can be distributed across the encounter:

\[ \operatorname{Grounds}(H) \subseteq \{Q,\Omega,\mathsf{Info},\mathsf{Act},\mathsf{Prog}, \mathsf{Resource},\text{ relations among them}\}. \]

Structural properties such as graph width, topology, symmetry, dependency structure, spectral geometry, or proof-theoretic strength belong primarily to the problem-object \(Q\). A structural hardness ground is the special case in which a fact about \(Q\), together with encounter conditions, supports a modal constraint on success. Such a ground can explain or certify hardness without being identical to the hardness fact. Other encounters may instead be resistant because an environment removes a path, an observation interface hides a necessary distinction, an action interface forbids an operation, or a program class excludes a realization.

Here ground names constitutive or metaphysical explanation rather than an additional causal mechanism; Fine (2012) provides a standard orientation to that notion. Nothing in the attainable-region algebra proves that a proposed ground is the correct one.

High treewidth may ground a lower bound under a particular access and machine model; a topological obstruction may ground non-realizability for a specified class of maps; a narrow spectral gap may ground slow convergence for a specified dynamics. The problem-object may supply structural resistance; the environment and interfaces may supply additional resistance; the encounter determines their joint modal consequences for success.

The primary modality is encounter-relative admissibility. Write

\[ \mathcal M(E) = \{\text{realizations admitted by the laws, environments, interfaces, program class, and resource conventions of }E\}. \]

A conclusion quantified over this class initially has the force \(\Box_E\varphi\): it holds throughout the realizations admitted by \(E\). That is not automatically logical, physical, technological, or unrestricted metaphysical necessity. Those stronger readings require an independently justified modal base. In particular, one may need to distinguish

\[ \mathcal M_{\mathrm{formal}}(E),\qquad \mathcal M_{\mathrm{physical}}(E),\qquad \mathcal M_{\mathrm{technological}}(E). \]

Formal adequacy supports reasoning inside \(\mathcal M_{\mathrm{formal}}(E)\). Empirical validation is needed before interpreting that class as covering the relevant physical possibilities. Technological admissibility may be narrower again because it includes only available or permitted implementations.

Let \(\mathcal S(E)\subseteq\mathcal M(E)\) contain the realizations satisfying the encounter's success condition, and let

\[ \pi_E:\mathcal M(E)\longrightarrow P_E \]

record performance. Two profiles must be distinguished:

\[ \mathsf{Prof}_{\mathrm{all}}(E) = \pi_E[\mathcal M(E)], \qquad \mathsf{Prof}_{\mathrm{succ}}(E) = \pi_E[\mathcal S(E)]. \]

With the book's resource-and-loss convention, their upward closures are

\[ \mathsf{Ach}_{\mathrm{all}}(E) = \uparrow\mathsf{Prof}_{\mathrm{all}}(E), \qquad \mathsf{Ach}_{\mathrm{succ}}(E) = \uparrow\mathsf{Prof}_{\mathrm{succ}}(E). \]

The full profile records failure, accuracy, resource, and loss tradeoffs. The successful profile records the costs and tradeoffs under which success remains possible. Feasibility concerns whether \(\mathcal S(E)\) is empty; resource hardness concerns the geometry of the successful profile; analyses allowing graded failure or approximation may require the full profile.

If success is completely determined by performance, choose an acceptable set \(G_E\subseteq P_E\) and define

\[ \mathcal S(E)=\pi_E^{-1}(G_E). \]

Then

\[ \mathsf{Prof}_{\mathrm{succ}}(E) = \mathsf{Prof}_{\mathrm{all}}(E)\cap G_E. \]

This identity must not be assumed when the performance vector omits a semantic part of success. The unqualified attainable region used in Chapters 1 to 5 is the full resource and loss region unless success has explicitly been built into the admitted program or realization class.

This is modal rather than merely historical. A lower-bound claim says, for example,

\[ \forall m\in\mathcal S(E),\qquad \mathsf{Resource}(m)\ge r, \]

whether or not anybody has attempted the task. Feasibility says \(\mathcal S(E)\ne\varnothing\); impossibility says \(\mathcal S(E)=\varnothing\). Actual runtime is one manifestation of the disposition, not the disposition itself. One successful fast run can refute a universal lower bound; one person's struggle does not establish one.

The ontology consequently has three levels:

\[ \boxed{ \text{structure} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{disposition} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{manifestation}.} \]

The distributed structure of the encounter grounds counterfactual constraints; the disposition concerns how \(E\)-admitted interactions could or must proceed; manifestations are particular executions, proofs, failures, or observed costs. Neither attainable region creates hardness by definition. The full and successful regions represent different aspects of the modal profile in virtue of which the encounter has its operational hardness features. That claim is realist only to the extent that the declared modal base adequately captures the object, environment, interfaces, and laws rather than merely an analyst's preferred description.

Impossibility remains categorically distinct from extreme feasible difficulty. If \(\mathcal S(E)=\varnothing\), the successful profile is empty even though the full profile may contain many failed or inaccurate realizations. If \(\mathcal S(E)\ne\varnothing\) but every successful point lies beyond practical budgets, the encounter is severely resistant but feasible. Encoding both cases as one informal value, such as infinity, can be convenient for optimization while concealing the ontological distinction.

Dispositionalism is an optional interpretation of the grounded counterfactual constraints. The operational theory neither requires an additional entity called resistance nor distinguishes a dispositional realist from someone who accepts the same constraints without that ontology. A stronger metaphysical claim would need a further explanatory argument. Three projects remain distinct:

  • metaphysics: encounter-borne modal resistance with distributed grounds;
  • epistemology: knowledge of hardness through proofs, lower bounds, experiments, modal-base validation, and invariance arguments;
  • mathematics: representation and comparison through full and successful attainable regions, partial orders, translations, and stable claims.

Structural grounds across encounters

Attribution requires more than observing that many methods failed. A lower bound can identify a structural contribution when its proof derives a restriction from a property \(S(P)\) together with specified encounter conditions. Across an aligned comparison class \(\mathcal E\), its form is

\[ S(P)\ \land\ \text{the stated conditions on }\mathcal E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \forall E\in\mathcal E,\ \forall m\in\mathcal S(E), \quad C_E(m)\ge r_E. \]

The inference establishes the bound. Calling \(S(P)\) its metaphysical ground adds an explanatory interpretation; invariance alone does not establish that interpretation. Every encounter in a poorly chosen comparison class might share the same restrictive interface, even if their surface descriptions differ.

Conversely, resistance disappearing after a representation change does not show that the object made no contribution. An oracle, cached lemma, or new primitive may overcome a genuine structural obstruction for the former access model. If the change is truly a cost-preserving presentation equivalence, the corresponding attainable-performance claim should survive. An apparent gain then calls for checking the cost accounting, interface, or claimed equivalence.

Controlled perturbation is useful when competing explanations predict different outcomes. Specify the alternatives before choosing the transformations. Vary one restriction, account for the translation's cost, and check the predicted change in success or performance. This can discriminate an information limit from an action limit, or a defective proof recipe from an infeasible task. Chapter 8 supplies both kinds of algebraic example. Stability broadens the scope of a proved constraint; locating its structural source requires the argument connecting that structure to the constraint.

Identity, equivalence, and directed transport

Because the encounter bears operational hardness, its individuation conditions cannot be left to syntax. Yet there is no single context-free relation corresponding to the phrase “the same problem.” Relations between encounters must instead be classified by the structures and inferences they preserve.

Strict identity is literal equality \(E=E'\) of the specified encounter data. It supports formal substitution and machine bookkeeping, but is too fine-grained for every scientific use. Renaming an action symbol or changing coordinates can produce a different encoded tuple without producing a relevantly different encounter.

Structural signatures and reducts. A structural signature \(\Sigma\) names the encounter components, operations, relations, and laws relevant to a comparison. Its selection is part of the encounter-alignment certificate, not a conclusion extracted from the comparison. Write

\[ E|_\Sigma \]

for the \(\Sigma\)-reduct of \(E\): the structured encounter obtained by retaining exactly the data named by \(\Sigma\) and forgetting the rest. A reduct is typed forgetting, not a claim that omitted structure is unreal or causally idle.

Presentation equivalence is isomorphism of reducts:

\[ \boxed{ E\cong_\Sigma E' \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E|_\Sigma\cong E'|_\Sigma.} \]

A witness \(\alpha:E|_\Sigma\cong E'|_\Sigma\) consists of reversible component maps preserving every operation, relation, and law retained by the signature. The relevant environment, observation, action, transition, program, and performance diagrams commute only when those structures occur in \(\Sigma\).

If the realization and success structures belong to \(\Sigma\), the witness includes a bijection

\[ \widehat\alpha: \mathcal M(E|_\Sigma) \longrightarrow \mathcal M(E'|_\Sigma) \]

with

\[ m\in\mathcal S(E|_\Sigma) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \widehat\alpha(m)\in\mathcal S(E'|_\Sigma). \]

If performance also belongs to \(\Sigma\), then for its declared coordinate isomorphism \(u_\alpha\),

\[ u_\alpha\circ\pi_{E|_\Sigma} = \pi_{E'|_\Sigma}\circ\widehat\alpha. \]

If realizations or performance do not belong to the signature, their full preservation is not silently imposed. This is the point of taking the reduct before asking for an isomorphism.

For a fixed signature, witnesses contain identities, inverses, and composites:

\[ \mathrm{id}_{E|_\Sigma}, \qquad \alpha^{-1}, \qquad \beta\circ\alpha. \]

They obey the usual identity and associativity laws. Thus the witnesses form a groupoid, and \(\cong_\Sigma\) is an equivalence relation on encounters whose \(\Sigma\)-reducts have the same structural type. An isometry is one witness when metric structure belongs to \(\Sigma\), not the universal definition. Graph isomorphism, diffeomorphism, dynamical conjugacy, or another reversible change can witness other signatures.

Claim-relative equivalence is indexed by an independently declared family of claim languages. Encounters may use different vocabularies, so write \(\Phi_E\) and \(\Phi_{E'}\). A witness of

\[ E\equiv_\Phi E' \]

contains licensed translations in both directions,

\[ T_\alpha:\Phi_E\longrightarrow\Phi_{E'}, \qquad T_{\bar\alpha}:\Phi_{E'}\longrightarrow\Phi_E, \]

such that claims are preserved and reflected:

\[ E\models\varphi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E'\models T_\alpha(\varphi), \qquad \varphi\in\Phi_E, \]
\[ E'\models\psi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad E\models T_{\bar\alpha}(\psi), \qquad \psi\in\Phi_{E'}. \]

The translations are inverse up to semantic equivalence in the admitted claim semantics:

\[ T_{\bar\alpha}T_\alpha(\varphi)\simeq_E\varphi, \qquad T_\alpha T_{\bar\alpha}(\psi)\simeq_{E'}\psi. \]

Here \(\simeq_E\) means equality of truth conditions in the semantics declared for \(E\), not syntactic identity. Each translation must respect these semantic-equivalence relations, so it descends to the corresponding quotient claim language. When both encounters use literally the same claim language, the translations may be identities and the definition reduces to the simpler biconditional.

Claim-equivalence witnesses also support identity, reversal, and composition. Identity witnesses use identity translations. For composable witnesses \(\alpha:E\equiv_\Phi E'\) and \(\beta:E'\equiv_\Phi E''\), define

\[ T_{\beta\circ\alpha} = T_\beta\circ T_\alpha, \qquad T_{\overline{\beta\circ\alpha}} = T_{\bar\alpha}\circ T_{\bar\beta}. \]

The round-trip laws are preserved by composition. After translations are identified up to \(\simeq\), these witnesses form a groupoid; consequently the existence relation \(\equiv_\Phi\) is an equivalence relation at the chosen claim level. Coincidental agreement of the propositions currently tested is insufficient; the coherent translations belong to the witness.

Presentation equivalence yields claim-relative equivalence only when the chosen languages are invariant under reduct isomorphism and their translations are induced by the structural witness:

\[ E\cong_\Sigma E' \ \Longrightarrow\ E\equiv_\Phi E' \qquad \text{only for }\Phi\subseteq\operatorname{Inv}(\Sigma). \]

An extra memory bit may be irrelevant to reachability but visible to an exact memory-cost claim. Two sensors may be equivalent for robust action and inequivalent for state reconstruction. Extensionally equal success predicates may be presentationally interchangeable even when their syntax differs. Equal attainable regions provide performance equivalence, but can hide different causal mechanisms, realization spaces, or computational degrees.

Directed claim transport is asymmetric. A witness

\[ E\xrightarrow[\Phi]{\alpha}E' \]

contains a translation \(T_\alpha:\Phi_E\to\Phi_{E'}\) satisfying

\[ E\models\varphi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E'\models T_\alpha(\varphi), \qquad \varphi\in\Phi_E, \]

without requiring reflection or an inverse. When the witness also maps realizations, it may additionally satisfy

\[ m\in\mathcal S(E) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \widehat\alpha(m)\in\mathcal S(E'). \]

Directed witnesses have identity arrows and compose. If

\[ E\xrightarrow[\Phi]{\alpha}E' \xrightarrow[\Phi]{\beta}E'', \]

then

\[ E\xrightarrow[\Phi]{\beta\circ\alpha}E'', \qquad T_{\beta\circ\alpha} = T_\beta\circ T_\alpha, \]

with \(T_{\mathrm{id}_E}=\mathrm{id}_{\Phi_E}\) and associativity. They therefore form a category of encounters and licensed transports; only after discarding distinctions between parallel witnesses need this collapse to a preorder. Simulation, sound abstraction, embedding, refinement, reduction, and relaxation naturally live here.

Arrows in both directions do not by themselves give claim-relative or presentation equivalence. Claim equivalence additionally requires preservation, reflection, and coherent round trips in the chosen languages. Presentation equivalence additionally requires an isomorphism of the declared reducts.

Extension changes the admitted realization structure by adding, for example, an oracle, sensor, actuator, axiom, primitive, or resource. The old encounter may embed into the new one, but that embedding does not make the extension a presentation change.

Continuous relaxation makes the direction visible. If \(X_{\mathrm{disc}}\subseteq X_{\mathrm{relax}}\) and the objective is compatible, then

\[ \inf_{x\in X_{\mathrm{relax}}}f(x) \le \inf_{x\in X_{\mathrm{disc}}}f(x). \]

The relaxation supplies a lower bound for minimization. It need not return an integral optimizer, executable path, or valid resource certificate. A minimum should not replace the infimum unless attainment has been proved.

Realizations must also remain distinct from their performance images. When the realizations are trajectories, write

\[ \Gamma_{\mathrm{succ}}(E) = \{\gamma:\gamma\text{ is admitted and successful}\}, \]

and only then define

\[ \mathsf{Prof}_{\mathrm{succ}}(E) = \pi_E[\Gamma_{\mathrm{succ}}(E)]. \]

The ontological order is

\[ \text{realizations} \longrightarrow \text{successful realizations} \longrightarrow \text{performance profiles} \longrightarrow \text{attainable regions}. \]

The individuation thesis is consequently:

Encounter individuation is preservation-relative: the relevant notion of sameness is determined by which independently specified structures or inferences must survive transformation. Strict identity is equality of specified data; presentation equivalence is isomorphism of a declared structural reduct; claim-relative equivalence is coherent bidirectional translation of a declared claim language; directed transport preserves selected inferences in one direction.

The formal implications should be read in two stages. Equality always induces the identity reduct isomorphism:

\[ E=E' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E\cong_\Sigma E'. \]

When \(\Phi\subseteq\operatorname{Inv}(\Sigma)\) and the claim translations are induced by the reduct isomorphism,

\[ E\cong_\Sigma E' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E\equiv_\Phi E' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left( \exists\alpha,\ E\xrightarrow[\Phi]{\alpha}E' \right) \ \text{and}\ \left( \exists\bar\alpha,\ E'\xrightarrow[\Phi]{\bar\alpha}E \right). \]

None of the converses holds in general. In particular, two directed arrows do not supply the coherent round trips required by \(\equiv_\Phi\). Nor may a difference be declared irrelevant after inspecting the desired conclusion: \(\Sigma\), \(\Phi\), their semantics, and the admitted transformations must be fixed and justified independently.

The architecture is therefore

\[ \boxed{ \text{raw encounter} \to E|_\Sigma \to \text{presentation equivalence} \to \Phi \to \text{claim equivalence or directed transport} \to \text{stability class} \to \text{scope of authority}.} \]

Specification before calculation

The framework separates the justification of an encounter from calculation inside it. Selecting a task, environment family, uncertainty model, success tolerance, program class, and charged resources can depend on scientific purposes, engineering priorities, conventions, institutions, and values. A declaration does not validate itself merely by being explicit.

Three questions should therefore remain visible:

  • formal adequacy: do the conclusions follow from the declared model?
  • empirical adequacy: do the admitted worlds, interfaces, and calibrations represent the system of concern?
  • normative relevance: do the success criteria and charged consequences answer the question that ought to be asked?

Mathematics addresses the first directly and can support the second through validated models and measurements. It cannot infer the third from a type signature. Conditional objectivity begins after the specification has been made; responsible application also defends why that specification is relevant.

Within a fixed encounter, the resulting facts are not matters of opinion. The camera report at the fork merges \(L\) and \(R\); the closed fire door removes the dock from the reachable set; a controller either satisfies its certified time, energy, memory, and clearance bounds or it does not. Changing a sensor, actuator, uncertainty set, or charged resource creates a different encounter whose attainable region may change.

Why the fibred order is needed

Inside the hardware context, ordinary Pareto order already performs the local comparison. It records the camera/lidar tradeoffs and leaves crossing frontiers incomparable. The fibred layer becomes useful when claims move between different performance spaces or encounter interfaces.

Its authority is deliberately staged:

\[ \text{encounter alignment} \Longrightarrow \text{licensed performance translation} \Longrightarrow \text{ambient region comparison} \Longrightarrow \text{realized-region transport, if proved}. \]

An alignment relates the tasks and admissible worlds. A performance map then translates specifications. Inverse image supports comparison of a given pair of aligned encounters, but does not produce a hardware encounter realizing every translated simulator region. Preimage closure proves transport of realized regions; an encounter map additionally supplies representatives.

Information theory, control, abstraction, and complexity determine particular constraints and attainable regions. The fibred order organizes how those results may be compared after semantic alignment has been justified. It does not decide whether the simulator represents the plant, whether a safety threshold is legitimate, or whether two tasks deserve comparison.

No unqualified master scale

The result is a partial order indexed by declared contexts, not an automatically privileged scalar. Crossing Pareto frontiers may remain incomparable, and scalarizations can rank them differently. This does not imply that context-independent facts are impossible. It says that global authority must be earned by stability across an appropriate comparison class rather than asserted by omitting the index.

The comparison class is itself declared. Requiring stability across coordinate changes is weaker than requiring stability across machine simulations, sensor substitutions, task translations, and environment enlargement. A claim is only as broad as the arrows along which it has been shown to survive.

What can survive context change

The individuation hierarchy supplies the transformation theory beneath stability. Reduct isomorphisms specify which changes count as presentation; coherent bidirectional translations specify which preserve and reflect a claim language; composable directed witnesses specify one-way inference. Their identity and composition laws allow stability to be tested along chains rather than only for isolated pairs. Exact equality is therefore only one form of invariant content. What survives an encounter alignment may instead be:

  • an ordering of attainable regions;
  • a lower or upper resource bound;
  • feasibility or impossibility;
  • a safety or reachability statement;
  • a robust interval or error margin.

Let \(\mathcal A\) be a declared class of licensed encounter transformations. A claim is stable over \(\mathcal A\) when every relevant transformation transports it in the stated direction. Exact invariance may follow from presentation or claim-relative equivalence; directional stability may preserve only implications or bounds. Neither follows from a performance-coordinate map alone. The transformation class and claim language must be independently motivated: a trivial \(\Phi\) or a post hoc \(\mathcal A\) can manufacture uninformative invariance.

This yields a hierarchy of authority:

\[ \boxed{ \text{local} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{transportable} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{stable over a declared comparison class}.} \]

The endpoint of the framework is therefore not that everything is contextual. It is a disciplined account of what individuates an encounter, what licenses transport, and what remains true when the encounter changes. Operational difficulty is the comparison induced by attainable performance. A relational hardness claim acquires broader objective authority insofar as it remains invariant, or directionally stable, under an independently justified and relevant class of encounter transformations, not by having its assumptions erased.

Chapter 7When the system reacts to the model

Reaction is not necessarily strategy

A model can fail because the world was unknown, because it changed, or because using the model helped change it. These explanations call for different tests. An unknown fixed parameter calls for identification. Time-dependent drift calls for tracking. Deployment feedback calls for a model of the response to the deployed policy.

Reaction need not involve an opponent. A controller can heat a sensor; a prediction can determine which observations will be collected; a scheduling rule can shift arrivals. Strategic response is the narrower case in which an agent selects behavior according to an objective. Adversarial response further specifies opposition to the solver's guarantee. Calling all three strategic would obscure the mechanism rather than explain it.

For a sequential encounter, let \(h_t\) be the history visible at time \(t\). A solver policy and an environment response jointly generate a trajectory:

\[ a_t=p(h_t), \qquad x_{t+1}=\alpha(h_t,a_t). \]

This timing lets the environment respond to the current action but not to an unobserved future action. Other timing and information conventions define different encounters. Randomized policies also require a declaration of which random choices the response observes.

If actions can change the transition rule itself, include the rule \(R_t\) in the state and write an augmented transition for \((x_t,R_t)\). Such an endogenous law still evolves under a specified higher-level rule. It does not license arbitrary changes to the encounter during an argument.

The uniform guarantee

Let \(\mathcal A\) be the admitted response class, and let \(L(p,\alpha)\) and \(C(p,\alpha)\) be the loss and resource cost of their joint execution. The robust attainable region is

\[ \mathsf{Ach}_{\mathcal A} =\{(r,\ell):\exists p\in\mathsf{Prog},\ \forall\alpha\in\mathcal A,\ C(p,\alpha)\le r\ \land\ L(p,\alpha)\le\ell\}. \]

One policy must meet both bounds against every admitted response. The weaker statement \(\forall\alpha\,\exists p\) permits a different policy after the response is known. Changing those quantifiers can turn a failed robust task into a collection of easy tasks without improving the original solver.

Holding programs, cost, and loss fixed, enlarging \(\mathcal A\) can only shrink this region. Merely replacing one known response law by another need not do so: it may change the best policy while leaving optimal performance unchanged. The following calculation separates these effects.

Worked example: a forecast changes its target

A deployed system announces a number \(\theta\in[0,1]\) and incurs absolute prediction loss \(\lvert\theta-y\rvert\). Each announcement costs one unit. There are two possible deterministic environments:

\[ F(\theta)=1, \qquad R(\theta)=1-\theta. \]

The first is frozen. The second mechanically reacts to the forecast. At the initial announcement \(\theta_0=0\), both produce the same observation \(y=1\). Fitting that observation gives \(\theta_1=1\). Its historical fitting error is zero. Deployed against \(R\), however, it produces \(y=0\) and error one. No agent chose to deceive it. The evidence failed to distinguish two response laws that agree at the data-collection policy.

Repeatedly replacing the forecast by the latest reactive observation gives

\[ \theta_{n+1}=1-\theta_n. \]

Starting at zero, this rule oscillates between zero and one. The fixed point \(\theta=1/2\) instead predicts its own reactive outcome perfectly. Thus the reactive encounter alone has the same optimal loss, zero, as the frozen encounter alone, although their optimal announcements differ.

Suppose the solver must commit to one announcement before learning which law applies. No further experiment is admitted in this one-round encounter. Its worst-case loss is

\[ W(\theta)=\max\{1-\theta,\lvert2\theta-1\rvert\}. \]

If \(W(\theta)\le\ell\), then \(1-\theta\le\ell\) and \(2\theta-1\le\ell\). Twice the first inequality plus the second yields \(1\le3\ell\). At \(\theta=2/3\), both losses equal \(1/3\), so

\[ \min_{0\le\theta\le1}W(\theta)=1/3. \]

The exact attainable regions are therefore

\[ \mathsf{Ach}_{\{F\}} =\{(r,\ell):r\ge1,\ \ell\ge0\}, \]
\[ \mathsf{Ach}_{\{F,R\}} =\{(r,\ell):r\ge1,\ \ell\ge1/3\}. \]

The second is strictly smaller. Reaction changed the required policy; uncertainty over the response class imposed the positive robust loss. Additional diagnostic announcements could identify the law here, but would change both the information interface and the resource accounting. The arithmetic, optimal bound, and both regions are machine checked.

This belongs to the setting studied as performative prediction: deployment can alter the data distribution. Perdomo, Zrnic, Mendler-Dünner, and Hardt (2020) distinguish performative optimality from stability under retraining. The displayed \(1/3\) bound is our elementary robust calculation over two response laws; it is not their convergence theorem. The oscillation also shows why a deployment fixed point and convergence of a chosen retraining procedure are different questions.

Counterfactual comparison against an adaptive environment

When the response depends on the solver's history, evaluating an alternative policy on the actual policy's frozen observations can misstate its performance. The alternative would have generated another history and therefore another response. A counterfactual policy comparison must rerun that interaction.

For cumulative loss \(L_T\), one possible comparison is

\[ L_T(p,\alpha) -\inf_{q\in\mathcal Q}L_T(q,\alpha), \]

where each term uses the trajectory jointly generated by its own policy and the same response rule \(\alpha\). The comparator class \(\mathcal Q\), information timing, and any randomization remain fixed. This is the concern addressed by policy regret in Arora, Dekel, and Tewari (2012). Their distinction from ordinary regret against an adaptive adversary is directly relevant; their guarantees also depend on restrictions on the adversary, such as bounded memory.

Learning itself can be limited by strategic opacity: two strategic response rules may agree on every interaction the learning protocol can perform but require different deployment policies. As in Chapter 1, the first question is whether the experiment class distinguishes the cases that matter to action. The forecast example exhibits the same information problem without strategy.

For deployment, the useful audit is short: which response laws were admitted, what did they observe, which policy generated the evidence, and does one implementable policy meet the promised bounds after deployment? A guarantee over a frozen dataset answers only part of that question. Once these declarations are supplied, the attainable-region comparison is the same one used elsewhere in the book.

Chapter 8When nearby problems stop being nearby

A failed recipe is not a lower bound

Nearby examples often suggest how to solve a target. The suggestion becomes a mathematical transfer claim only when a proof transformation is supplied and its side conditions and costs are checked. Division by a parameter, removal of an exceptional case, or relaxation of integrality can invalidate that transfer even when the resulting statements look nearly identical.

Three costs should be kept separate. Search cost is the work of locating a certificate. Certificate cost is a declared cost of constructing, executing, or checking a supplied certificate. Budget infeasibility means that no admitted certificate meets the bound. A search may fail while a cheap certificate exists. A changed search order can then help without extending the proof language. Conversely, adding an oracle or a stronger primitive changes which realizations are admitted or how they are charged. “Change the model” does not identify which of these happened.

The mathematical premises also remain distinct from a solver's expectations. A proof cannot violate the theorem's hypotheses to establish its conclusion. It can reject an expectation that the proof should use a particular coordinate system, resemble neighboring proofs, or proceed by a familiar decomposition. The formal question is whether that favored method was necessary, affordable, or merely the first one tried.

Cost profiles and available mechanisms

Fix a proof or certificate type, a predicate \(\operatorname{Proves}(p,P)\), and a cost \(C(p)\in\overline{\mathbb R}_+=[0,\infty]\). Write

\[ \kappa(P)=\inf_{p:\operatorname{Proves}(p,P)} C(p). \]

An empty admitted-proof set gives \(\kappa(P)=\infty\). Finite cost implies that a proof exists; the converse requires a finite-cost proof. An infimum need not be attained, so a budget at the infimum is not automatically feasible.

To compare costs even at infinity, use the extended cost gap

\[ g(a,b)=(a\mathbin{\dotminus}b)\vee(b\mathbin{\dotminus}a). \]

It is symmetric, vanishes exactly when \(a=b\), gives \(g(\infty,\infty)=0\), and gives \(g(a,\infty)=\infty\) for finite \(a\). For a declared proximity map \(d\), define the local variation

\[ \Delta_\varepsilon(P) =\sup_{Q:\,d(P,Q)\le\varepsilon} g\bigl(\kappa(Q),\kappa(P)\bigr). \]

This is monotone in \(\varepsilon\) and is zero exactly when every neighbor in the displayed set has the target's cost. The order laws need no metric axioms. Interpreting \(d\) as geometric distance requires those extra laws; interpreting it as useful mathematical similarity requires a modeling argument.

A selected proof label \(m(P)\) records what someone used, not what was available. To study availability, independently classify certificates by \(\operatorname{kind}(p)\) and define

\[ K_B(P)=\{k:\exists p,\ \operatorname{Proves}(p,P)\ \land\ C(p)\le B \ \land\ \operatorname{kind}(p)=k\}. \]

The set is empty exactly when no admitted certificate fits budget \(B\). Increasing the budget can only enlarge it. A selected label can change while the entire set stays fixed; therefore a change of label alone does not locate a boundary of possible methods.

For directed transport, a proof transformation \(\tau\) with kind map \(v\) must send every admitted source certificate within \(B\) to a target certificate within \(B'\), with \(\operatorname{kind}(\tau p)=v(\operatorname{kind}(p))\). Then

\[ v[K_B(P)]\subseteq K_{B'}(Q). \]

This is the proof-level counterpart of the performance transports in Chapter 5. Its soundness and cost assumptions expose what an analogy needs to supply.

The stability radius includes its supremum

Let \(m(P)\) be a declared mechanism summary and \(\sim\) a declared binary relation on summaries. One substantive choice is \(m(P)=K_B(P)\) with equality of sets. A selected-label summary instead studies stability of the selection. Define

\[ \operatorname{Stable}_P(r) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \forall Q,\ d(P,Q)<r\Rightarrow m(Q)\sim m(P), \]
\[ R(P)=\sup\{r:\operatorname{Stable}_P(r)\}. \]

With extended nonnegative distances and this strict ball condition,

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{Stable}_P(r)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r\le R(P).} \]

Here is why the endpoint is included. If \(d(P,Q)<R(P)\), the defining supremum contains some stable radius \(s>d(P,Q)\); otherwise \(d(P,Q)\) would be an upper bound. Stability at \(s\) supplies the required relation. Thus \(R(P)\) is itself stable, and downward closure gives the equivalence. This argument needs neither metric axioms nor equivalence laws on \(\sim\). Those laws are needed only for the interpretations that invoke them.

The stable radius includes its supremum, but its open ball excludes points whose distance equals that radius. At \(R(P)=\infty\), it also excludes points at infinite distance. Confusing these two endpoints led to the erroneous nonattainment warning in the previous edition; the corrected theorem is machine checked.

Worked example: a pivot fails while the problem stays easy

Consider the rational-parameter family

\[ t x+y=1,\qquad x=1,\qquad t\in\mathbb Q. \]

Its coefficient matrix has determinant \(-1\) for every \(t\). There is no singular system at zero: the solution is always \(x=1,\ y=1-t\). Now declare a deliberately small certificate language with two recipes. Recipe \(S\), substitution, uses that solution directly and charges one subtraction. Recipe \(E\), elimination with the first coefficient as pivot, requires \(t\ne0\) and calculates

\[ m=1/t,\quad u=-m,\quad v=1-m,\quad y=v/u,\quad x=(1-y)/t. \]

It charges six unit-cost arithmetic operations: three divisions, one negation, and two subtractions. This is a symbolic arithmetic model with exact rational input and checked side conditions; bit lengths and side-condition checking costs are outside the displayed construction-cost coordinate. Both recipes produce the required solution wherever admitted. Their algebraic soundness is proved in Lean over the reals and therefore applies to rational inputs too.

In this certificate language,

\[ \operatorname{Proves}(S,P_t)\text{ always},\qquad \operatorname{Proves}(E,P_t)\Longleftrightarrow t\ne0, \qquad C(S)=1,\ C(E)=6. \]

Consequently \(\kappa(P_t)=1\) for every \(t\), and \(\Delta_\varepsilon(P_t)=0\) for every neighborhood radius. No claim about the optimal bit complexity of unrestricted equation solving is being made. The certificate class and charged operations are what make this exact minimum a well-defined calculation.

At budget one the available set is \(K_1(P_t)=\{S\}\) everywhere. At budget six it is

\[ K_6(P_t)= \begin{cases} \{S,E\},&t\ne0,\\ \{S\},&t=0. \end{cases} \]

Use ordinary parameter distance \(d(P_t,P_s)=\lvert t-s\rvert\) and equality of available sets. At budget one, the stability radius is infinite. At budget six, the radius at a nonzero \(t\) is \(\lvert t\rvert\): every smaller distance avoids zero, while any larger-radius ball contains it. The radius at zero is zero, since nonzero rationals lie arbitrarily close. In particular, the ball of radius \(\lvert t\rvert\) is stable even though the point on its boundary has a different available set.

The chosen elimination recipe fails at zero, and even the availability set changes there. Yet the cheap substitution method survives and optimal cost never changes. A zero mechanism-stability radius is therefore compatible with constant attainable certificate cost. It can reveal the loss of an optional, inefficient method rather than any increase in operational difficulty.

Worked example: a genuine feasibility boundary

Contrast the scalar task

\[ Q_t:\quad\text{find }x\text{ such that }tx=1. \]

Here a certificate is a supplied exact value \(x\), admitted when \(tx=1\). Charge one multiplication for the arithmetic part of checking it; supplying the witness and checking exact equality are outside this coordinate. This is a verification-cost encounter, distinct from the construction-cost encounter above. For \(t\ne0\), the witness \(x=1/t\) is admitted. For \(t=0\), no value is admitted. Hence

\[ \kappa(Q_t)= \begin{cases} 1,&t\ne0,\\ \infty,&t=0. \end{cases} \]

Under ordinary parameter distance, \(\Delta_0(Q_0)=0\), while \(\Delta_\varepsilon(Q_0)=\infty\) for every positive \(\varepsilon\): the neighborhood contains a nonzero parameter with finite cost. This is an actual change of feasibility, not merely the failure of a pivot choice. The costs and the infinite gap between zero and each nonzero parameter are machine checked. The density argument giving the stated neighborhood supremum is the elementary argument in this paragraph.

Both examples contain a division that fails at zero. In the first, the determinant remains nonzero and another admitted recipe works. In the second, the equation itself loses every solution. Looking only at the failed division would miss precisely the distinction the framework is meant to expose.

What a perturbation experiment can establish

A useful experiment records the admitted certificate class and budgets, tests which methods survive, and computes or bounds the attainable cost. It then asks whether the change follows from the problem's structure, the interface, or the chosen method's side conditions. Failed attempts are observations to explain, not universal quantifiers over all admitted proofs.

An anti-model construction starts from a specified solver or model and constructs an instance that defeats it. The form \(\forall M\,\exists P\,\operatorname{Fails}(M,P)\) does not supply a fixed \(P\) defeating every model; swapping those quantifiers would invent a much stronger obstruction. Nor does locating a short certificate measure the cost of discovering its representation. That requires a search protocol and an additional resource coordinate.

The chapter's diagnostic can therefore be stated without a new hierarchy of hardness: declare proximity, distinguish selected from available methods, measure attainable cost, and prove the transport or failure being claimed. These are concrete uses of the encounter audit, not additional definitions of what makes all mathematics hard.

Decision-bearing benchmark support

For benchmark comparisons, one useful diagnostic is decision-bearing support. Let \(B\) be a nonempty finite benchmark, let \(A\) be its finite candidate type, and let \(Y_i(a)\) be the measured outcome of candidate \(a\). Define

\[ D=\{i\in B:\exists a,b\in A,\ Y_i(a)\ne Y_i(b)\}, \qquad \rho_{\mathrm{dec}}=\frac{|D|}{|B|}. \]

For any two selectors \(s,t\), every instance on which \(Y_i(s(i))\ne Y_i(t(i))\) lies in \(D\). Consequently the fraction of benchmark instances on which their measured outcomes differ is at most \(\rho_{\mathrm{dec}}\). This cardinality theorem does not bound an arbitrary weighted or unbounded evaluation metric. It says exactly that non-decision-bearing instances cannot distinguish two selectors by their measured candidate outcomes. A benchmark can therefore contain many labeled examples while containing few actual selection decisions. Any empirical estimate of \(\rho_{\mathrm{dec}}\) still owes its dataset, sampling rule, candidate construction, outcome protocol, and analysis code; kernel verification supplies none of those things.

This edition uses claim-level links where a named theorem or established framework enters the argument. The bibliography is selective rather than exhaustive, but it now distinguishes foundations, neighboring frameworks, and the manuscript's own elementary deductions.

The information chapter draws on Shannon's mutual information and rate-distortion theory, Blackwell's comparison of experiments, and Massey's directed information. The displayed feedback inequality is a chain-rule and data-processing consequence under the manuscript's explicit random-tape and policy assumptions; it is not quoted as a separate theorem from those papers.

The control chapter uses Kalman's linear rank test and the Hermann-Krener local weak-observability condition. The robust scalar recurrence and the \(2\rho\) residual-separation bounds are proved directly in the text. Their interpretation is situated against switched-system and model-based fault-diagnosis literature.

The behavioral and abstraction chapters use Willems's trajectory-based system view, simulation proof methods, viability theory, abstract interpretation in the sense of Cousot and Cousot (1977), state abstraction in the sense of Li, Walsh, and Littman (2006), and a minimal sheaf-gluing observation. Exact finite-path lifting in the manuscript is an elementary theorem under stronger hypotheses than most approximate, probabilistic, or temporal-logic abstraction results.

Chapter 6 proposes a relational-dispositional metaphysics: the encounter bears hardness, its grounds may be distributed across the object, environment, interfaces, program class, resources, and their relations, and particular runtimes or failures are manifestations. The primary modality is encounter-relative admissibility; stronger physical or metaphysical readings require validation of the modal base. Full and successful attainable profiles represent different aspects of that modal structure rather than defining hardness into existence. Dispositionalism remains optional: the operational results establish counterfactual constraints without settling their metaphysical interpretation. The perturbation methodology of Chapter 8 discriminates specified explanations; invariance alone does not locate a structural ground. Encounter individuation is stratified into strict identity, reduct isomorphism, coherent claim-relative equivalence, composable directed transport, and extension. This interpretation is philosophical rather than machine-checked.

Chapter 7 connects deployment feedback to performative prediction and adaptive comparison to policy regret. Its two-law calculation is an original elementary illustration, not an empirical model or a claim of priority for those frameworks. Chapter 8's algebraic families similarly test distinctions in the certificate model; they do not establish a new complexity lower bound.

Throughout, the book separates established ingredients from the proposal. Pareto regions, co-design, resource preorders, reducibility degrees, indexed posets, and fibrations are established theories. The manuscript proposes an encounter audit in which the fibred order implements attainable-region comparison, licensed transport, and the search for stable claims. It does not claim mathematical priority for the component constructions. Nor does it infer semantic encounter alignment from a performance map.

Appendix. Glossary

These definitions are local to Why Problems Are Hard.

Abstraction
A generally many-to-one map from a concrete domain to a simpler domain, chosen to preserve specified answers. Executable return requires step lifting and goal reflection.
Action boundary
The primitive transformations, constraints, and policy permissions through which states can change. Adding an actuator changes this boundary.
Anti-model construction
A problem generated from a declared solver or model class so as to defeat it. The quantifier order, every model admitting some defeating problem, distinguishes it from a fixed exceptional target.
Attainable region
An upward-closed set of resource and loss bounds achieved under a specified encounter. The full region includes every admitted realization; the successful region includes only realizations satisfying the success condition. Neither is a hidden scalar hardness.
Available mechanisms
The kinds of admitted certificates that fit a specified budget. Unlike a selected proof label, this set records every available method in the declared certificate class.
Barrier depth
The least, over admissible paths, of the greatest drop below the starting objective value. It is \(+\infty\) when no path exists.
Behavioral system
A time domain, signal space, and set of admitted trajectories. Interconnection enforces shared constraints; hiding removes selected signals.
Claim-relative equivalence
Coherent translations in both directions preserve and reflect all propositions in the declared encounter-indexed claim languages, with round trips semantically equivalent to identity. Opposing directed transports alone do not establish it.
Comparison class
A declared collection of licensed encounter transformations across which a claim is intended to remain stable. Its independently justified relevance and breadth determine the authority of any resulting invariant.
Contraction margin
For \(e_{t+1}\le(q_0+\mu)e_t+d\), the gap \(1-q_0-\mu\) that supports the geometric error certificate.
Coordinate change
An invertible relabeling transporting every structure named in a claim. Exact conjugacy preserves runs and reachability.
Data-processing inequality
Post-processing available data cannot increase its mutual information with an unknown.
Decision-bearing support
On a nonempty finite benchmark with a fixed finite candidate type, the fraction of instances at which some two candidate outcomes differ. It bounds the fraction of instances on which two selectors' measured outcomes can differ, not arbitrary evaluation metrics.
Detectability
Decay of state ambiguity left unresolved by the admitted output history. Practical and robust versions allow specified residual tubes.
Directed claim transport
A licensed one-way claim translation preserving selected inferences or successful realizations without requiring reflection or an inverse. Identity transports and coherent composition make such witnesses composable across encounter chains.
Distortion
A declared reconstruction loss \(d(x,\widehat x)\), often averaged under a source distribution.
Encounter
A problem-object or instance family together with success, environment, information, action, program, and performance declarations. The raw tuple supplies strict formal identity; philosophically relevant sameness depends on a declared preservation relation.
Encounter alignment
A justification relating the tasks, success conditions, admissible worlds, interfaces, and resource conventions of two contexts. A performance-coordinate map is only one component and cannot establish semantic comparability by itself.
Endogenous law
A transition rule that is itself part of the state and can be changed by admitted actions, so that an action alters the semantics of later actions.
Error tube
A certified set containing estimation error under stated disturbance and mismatch bounds.
Extended cost gap
For extended nonnegative costs, the supremum of the two truncated differences. It is symmetric, separates unequal costs, and assigns zero rather than an undefined expression to infinity versus infinity.
Experiment class
The passive and active procedures quantified over when comparing states or models.
Extension
Addition of a sensor, actuator, predicate, state coordinate, oracle, axiom, primitive, or resource that changes the admitted realization structure. An embedding of the old encounter does not make the extension a presentation equivalence.
Fault tolerance
Accommodation retains a guarantee, detection separates fault from healthy uncertainty, and isolation distinguishes candidate faults.
Feedback capacity
A bound on task-relevant information entering through one feedback round under a specified policy and channel model.
Fibred order
Context-specific performance-region posets reindexed by preimage after encounter alignment has licensed the comparison. Ambient specifications transport through a performance map; realized classes additionally require preimage closure or encounter transport.
Forward simulation
A map sending each concrete step to a valid abstract step and therefore each concrete path to an abstract path.
Hardness ground
A fact about the problem-object, environment, interfaces, admitted realizations, resources, or their relations that helps explain an encounter's modal resistance. Grounds are not identical to the hardness they ground.
Information boundary
The observations, experiment class, prior state, and channel restrictions governing which distinctions enter a representation.
Invariant claim
An equality, order, bound, feasibility judgment, safety property, or other assertion proved stable over a declared comparison class. Stability may be exact or only directional.
Local step lifting
Realization of every offered abstract step from the concrete state actually reached. It lifts finite paths by induction.
Local variation
The supremum of the extended cost gap over a declared directed neighborhood of a target problem. It is relative to the chosen cost profile, proximity map, and radius.
Machine model
A specification of operations, input access, memory, randomness, and output timing used to state a complexity bound.
Mechanism stability
Persistence of a specified mechanism summary over a neighborhood. A summary may record a selected label or all budget-feasible kinds; these answer different questions. With the strict-ball definition, the supremum stable radius is itself stable. Availability can change while optimal cost stays constant.
Modal base
The realization class over which possibility, necessity, feasibility, or a lower bound is quantified. This book primarily uses encounter-relative admissibility; physical or metaphysical readings require additional justification.
Modal resistance
On the dispositional interpretation, the encounter-borne disposition constraining successful realization through impossibility, lower bounds, information deficiency, forced tradeoffs, sensitivity, or narrow robustness margins. Its force is relative to a declared modal base.
Model mismatch
Difference between the actual transition or observation law and the law used by an estimator.
Observability codistribution
The span of differentials of outputs and their admissible iterated Lie derivatives. Full rank gives the Hermann-Krener local sufficient condition.
Observational equivalence
Equality of all admitted finite observation laws from two states or models.
Operational difficulty
The comparison induced by attainable-performance structures under specified encounters. It is objective conditional on the declarations, generally partial rather than scalar, and distinct from the structural facts that may explain or certify it.
Operative representation
A causal state carrying selected consequences of past interaction into later inference or action.
Presentation equivalence
Isomorphism of two encounters' reducts to a fixed structural signature. Its witnesses have identity, inverse, and composition laws; they preserve only the structures retained by the signature.
Proof cost
The infimum extended-nonnegative cost of the admitted proofs of a target. An empty admitted-proof type has cost infinity. The definition begins after the proof language and cost model have been chosen.
Rate-distortion function
The infimum mutual information required by a reconstruction channel to attain a declared expected distortion.
Reachability
Membership in the closure generated by finite admissible steps from a starting state.
Robust action
One action acceptable for every state in an observation equivalence class; equivalently, a member of the intersection of their acceptable-action sets.
Signature reduct
The encounter structure \(E|_\Sigma\) obtained by retaining exactly the components, operations, relations, and laws named by the structural signature \(\Sigma\).
State capacity
The number of internal configurations available to a finite machine, converted to bits by a logarithm.
Strategic opacity
Indistinguishability, under the learning interaction, of environment strategies that require different behavior under a relevant deployment interaction.
Structural hardness ground
A hardness ground located principally in a property of the problem-object, such as topology, graph width, dependency structure, or proof-theoretic strength. It requires encounter conditions to yield a hardness fact.
Structural signature
An independently declared selection of encounter components, operations, relations, and laws. It determines the reduct whose isomorphisms witness presentation equivalence and therefore the relevant standard of structural sameness.
Structurally grounded disposition
A proposed explanation of modal constraints in terms of object structure and encounter conditions. A stable bound establishes its declared scope; locating the ground additionally requires an argument connecting the structure to that bound.
Successful profile
The performance image of the successful admissible realizations. Its emptiness records impossibility under the modal base; its frontier records the costs and tradeoffs conditional on success.
Task-relevant information
Information that reduces uncertainty under the loss or decision being studied.

/-- Deterministic experiment refinement is transitive. -/
theorem experimentRefines_trans {S : Type u} {A : Type v} {B : Type w}

/-- Refinement reverses inclusion of indistinguishability classes: equality of

theorem ExperimentRefines.indist {S : Type u} {F : Type v} {C : Type w}
    {fine : S → F} {coarse : S → C}

  exact (show FactorsThrough coarse fine from h).eq_of_eq hxy

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/InformationOrder.lean
/-- Actions acceptable at every state compatible with the current report.
The acceptable-action predicate is part of the task, rather than part of the
observation alone. -/
def RobustActions {S : Type u} {O : Type v} {A : Type w}
    (observe : S → O) (Acceptable : S → A → Prop) (x : S) : Set A :=
  {a | ∀ y, Indist observe x y → Acceptable y a}

/-- A finer experiment weakly enlarges the set of robust acceptable actions.
The result is task-relevant but deterministic: it compares compatibility
classes, not probabilities or average information. -/
theorem ExperimentRefines.robustActions_mono

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/InformationOrder.lean
/-- A strict finite decision loss. The identity experiment separates the two
Bool states, while the quotient to `Unit` merges them. An action is acceptable
exactly when it names the true state. At state `false`, the fine report permits
the robust action `false`; the coarse report permits no robust action. -/
theorem quotient_strict_decision_loss :
    let fine : Bool → Bool := id
    let coarse : Bool → Unit := fun _ ↦ ()
    let Acceptable : Bool → Bool → Prop := fun state action ↦ state = action
    ExperimentRefines fine coarse ∧
      ¬ Indist fine false true ∧ Indist coarse false true ∧
      RobustActions coarse Acceptable false = ∅ ∧
      RobustActions fine Acceptable false = {false} ∧
      RobustActions coarse Acceptable false ⊂
        RobustActions fine Acceptable false := by
  dsimp
  refine ⟨⟨fun _ ↦ (), rfl⟩, by simp [Indist], by simp [Indist], ?_⟩
  have hCoarse : RobustActions (fun _ : Bool ↦ ())
      (fun state action : Bool ↦ state = action) false = ∅ := by
    ext action
    constructor
    · intro ha
      have hFalse := ha false (by rfl)
      have hTrue := ha true (by rfl)
      simp only [Set.mem_empty_iff_false]
      cases action <;> simp_all
    · simp
  have hFine : RobustActions id
      (fun state action : Bool ↦ state = action) false = {false} := by
    ext action
    simp [RobustActions, Indist]
  refine ⟨hCoarse, hFine, ?_⟩
  rw [hCoarse, hFine]
  exact Set.empty_ssubset.mpr (Set.singleton_nonempty false)

  induction n with
  | zero => simpa using hinitial

          ring

/-- Algebraic posterior variance for a scalar Gaussian prior of variance `P₀`
after `n` independent measurements with noise variance `R`.

This definition packages the standard conjugate-Gaussian calculation. It does
not itself prove that a physical measurement process is Gaussian, independent,
or correctly modeled. -/
noncomputable def posteriorVariance (P₀ R : ℝ) (n : ℕ) : ℝ :=
  1 / (1 / P₀ + (n : ℝ) / R)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FeedbackBounds.lean
/-- Positive prior and measurement variances give positive posterior variance. -/
theorem posteriorVariance_pos {P₀ R : ℝ} (hP₀ : 0 < P₀) (hR : 0 < R)
    (n : ℕ) : 0 < posteriorVariance P₀ R n := by
  unfold posteriorVariance
  positivity

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FeedbackBounds.lean
/-- Reaching target variance `D` under the scalar posterior formula requires
at least `R * (1 / D - 1 / P₀)` measurements. The right side is real-valued;
an integer ceiling would be needed for a sharp natural-number statement.

This is an algebraic consequence of the displayed variance formula. It is not
a general Kalman-filter theorem and says nothing about model mismatch, process
noise, correlated measurements, or unobservable state directions. -/
theorem target_accuracy_requires_observations {P₀ R D : ℝ} {n : ℕ}
    (hP₀ : 0 < P₀) (hR : 0 < R) (hD : 0 < D)
    (hacc : posteriorVariance P₀ R n ≤ D) :
    R * (1 / D - 1 / P₀) ≤ (n : ℝ) := by
  have hden : 0 < 1 / P₀ + (n : ℝ) / R := by positivity
  have hcross : 1 ≤ D * (1 / P₀ + (n : ℝ) / R) := by
    exact (div_le_iff₀ hden).mp hacc
  have hprecision : 1 / D ≤ 1 / P₀ + (n : ℝ) / R := by
    apply (div_le_iff₀ hD).2
    nlinarith
  have hremaining : 1 / D - 1 / P₀ ≤ (n : ℝ) / R := by
    linarith
  simpa [mul_comm] using (le_div_iff₀ hR).mp hremaining

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FeedbackBounds.lean
/-- Iterating a scalar robust contraction gives a geometric finite-time bound.
The error need not be generated by any particular observer, but nonnegativity
of `q` is needed to propagate the upper inequality. -/
theorem error_le_geometric_sum (e : ℕ → ℝ) (q d : ℝ)
    (hq : 0 ≤ q) (hstep : ∀ n, e (n + 1) ≤ q * e n + d) (n : ℕ) :
    e n ≤ q ^ n * e 0 + d * ∑ k ∈ Finset.range n, q ^ k := by
  induction n with
  | zero => simp
  | succ n ih =>
      calc
        e (n + 1) ≤ q * e n + d := hstep n
        _ ≤ q * (q ^ n * e 0 + d * ∑ k ∈ Finset.range n, q ^ k) + d :=
          add_le_add (mul_le_mul_of_nonneg_left ih hq) le_rfl
        _ = q ^ (n + 1) * e 0 + d * ∑ k ∈ Finset.range (n + 1), q ^ k := by
          rw [geom_sum_succ]
          ring

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FeedbackBounds.lean
/-- With a strict contraction, the geometric sum can be written using the
disturbance tube `d / (1 - q)`. This is a finite-time statement, not an
asymptotic optimality claim. -/
theorem error_le_tube_transient (e : ℕ → ℝ) (q d : ℝ)
    (hq0 : 0 ≤ q) (hq1 : q < 1)
    (hstep : ∀ n, e (n + 1) ≤ q * e n + d) (n : ℕ) :
    e n ≤ q ^ n * (e 0 - d / (1 - q)) + d / (1 - q) := by
  have hqne : q ≠ 1 := ne_of_lt hq1
  have hbound := error_le_geometric_sum e q d hq0 hstep n
  rw [geom_sum_eq hqne n] at hbound
  calc
    e n ≤ q ^ n * e 0 + d * ((q ^ n - 1) / (q - 1)) := hbound
    _ = q ^ n * (e 0 - d / (1 - q)) + d / (1 - q) := by
      field_simp [sub_ne_zero.mpr hqne, sub_ne_zero.mpr hqne.symm]
      ring

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FeedbackBounds.lean
/-- The disturbance tube is forward invariant: if the initial error is below
`d / (1 - q)`, every later error remains below it. The assumption `0 ≤ d`
makes the displayed radius nonnegative; it is not needed merely for the upper
inequality but records its intended error-bound interpretation. -/
theorem error_le_tube (e : ℕ → ℝ) (q d : ℝ)
    (hq0 : 0 ≤ q) (hq1 : q < 1) (hd : 0 ≤ d)
    (hstep : ∀ n, e (n + 1) ≤ q * e n + d)
    (hinit : e 0 ≤ d / (1 - q)) (n : ℕ) :
    0 ≤ d / (1 - q) ∧ e n ≤ d / (1 - q) := by
  constructor
  · exact div_nonneg hd (sub_nonneg.mpr (le_of_lt hq1))
  · have hpow : 0 ≤ q ^ n := pow_nonneg hq0 n
    have htransient := error_le_tube_transient e q d hq0 hq1 hstep n
    have hdiff : e 0 - d / (1 - q) ≤ 0 := sub_nonpos.mpr hinit
    have hnonpos : q ^ n * (e 0 - d / (1 - q)) ≤ 0 :=
      mul_nonpos_of_nonneg_of_nonpos hpow hdiff
    linarith

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FeedbackBounds.lean
/-- Writing the effective contraction factor as `q₀ + μ` makes the robustness
margin explicit. A certified mismatch `μ` must remain below `1 - q₀`; under
that hypothesis the same finite-time disturbance-tube bound applies.

The result does not say that `μ` is a universal metric on model difference.
An observer analysis must first justify that the actual mismatch contributes
at most `μ` to this scalar one-step estimate. -/
theorem mismatch_le_margin_and_error_bound (e : ℕ → ℝ) (q₀ μ d : ℝ)
    (hq₀ : 0 ≤ q₀) (hμ : 0 ≤ μ) (hmargin : q₀ + μ < 1)
    (hstep : ∀ n, e (n + 1) ≤ (q₀ + μ) * e n + d) (n : ℕ) :
    μ < 1 - q₀ ∧
      e n ≤ (q₀ + μ) ^ n * (e 0 - d / (1 - q₀ - μ)) +
        d / (1 - q₀ - μ) := by
  constructor
  · linarith
  · have hq : 0 ≤ q₀ + μ := add_nonneg hq₀ hμ
    simpa [sub_sub] using
      error_le_tube_transient e (q₀ + μ) d hq hmargin hstep n

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FaultMargins.lean

the threshold plus the healthy-radius allowance, every admitted healthy
residual forces an alarm. The condition is sufficient, not necessary. -/

    Alarm η (healthy + fault) := by
  have hreverse : ‖fault‖ ≤ ‖healthy + fault‖ + ‖healthy‖ := by
    calc
      ‖fault‖ = ‖(healthy + fault) - healthy‖ := by
        congr 1
        abel
      _ ≤ ‖healthy + fault‖ + ‖healthy‖ := norm_sub_le _ _
  unfold Alarm
  linarith

/-- Two models are probe distinguishable from `s` when some policy of the

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Observational equivalence: no policy of the declared function type
distinguishes the two states at any finite horizon. -/
def ObsEquiv (M : POSystem S A O) (s s' : S) : Prop :=
  ∀ (π : List O → PMF A) (n : ℕ), obsProcess M π n s [] = obsProcess M π n s' []

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- Observational equivalence is an equivalence relation, as the notation
`≡` silently promises. -/
theorem obsEquiv_equivalence (M : POSystem S A O) : Equivalence (ObsEquiv M) where
  refl _ := fun _ _ => rfl
  symm h := fun π n => (h π n).symm
  trans h₁ h₂ := fun π n => (h₁ π n).trans (h₂ π n)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Observability.lean
/-- A necessary metric condition for asymptotic reconstruction: if the same
estimate converges to each of two state trajectories, their mutual distance
converges to zero. To turn this into a detectability theorem, a system-specific
argument must show that observationally indistinguishable trajectories really
feed the same estimate. -/
theorem common_estimate_forces_pairwise_convergence
    {X : Type*} [PseudoMetricSpace X] (x₁ x₂ xhat : ℕ → X)
    (h₁ : Tendsto (fun t => dist (x₁ t) (xhat t)) atTop (𝓝 0))
    (h₂ : Tendsto (fun t => dist (x₂ t) (xhat t)) atTop (𝓝 0)) :
    Tendsto (fun t => dist (x₁ t) (x₂ t)) atTop (𝓝 0) := by
  refine squeeze_zero (fun _ => dist_nonneg) (fun t => dist_triangle _ (xhat t) _) ?_

    h₂.congr' (Eventually.of_forall fun t => dist_comm (x₂ t) (xhat t))
  simpa only [zero_add] using h₁.add h₂'

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Finite-composition reachability: `y` is reachable from `x` by
applying effectors drawn from `G`. -/
inductive Reach (G : Set (X → X)) (x : X) : X → Prop
  | refl : Reach G x x
  | tail : ∀ {y z : X} {g : X → X}, Reach G x y → g ∈ G → z = g y → Reach G x z

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Any admissible trajectory stays inside the reachable set: at each
step some effector in `G` was applied, chosen by an arbitrary policy,
and no choice rule ever escapes the orbit. -/
theorem traj_mem_reach (G : Set (X → X)) (σ : ℕ → X)
    (hstep : ∀ n, ∃ g ∈ G, σ (n + 1) = g (σ n)) (n : ℕ) :
    Reach G (σ 0) (σ n) := by
  induction n with
  | zero => exact Reach.refl
  | succ n ih =>
      obtain ⟨g, hg, heq⟩ := hstep n
      exact Reach.tail ih hg heq

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Pointwise realizability over `G`: from every state, the image under `g` is
already reachable with `G`. The definition supplies neither a uniform generator
word nor a computable dispatcher for the witnessing paths. -/
def Programmed (G : Set (X → X)) (g : X → X) : Prop :=
  ∀ y : X, Reach G y (g y)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Inserting a pointwise realizable transformation as a primitive leaves every
reachability orbit unchanged. -/
theorem reach_insert_programmed (G : Set (X → X)) {g : X → X}
    (hg : Programmed G g) (x z : X) :
    Reach (insert g G) x z ↔ Reach G x z := by
  constructor
  · intro h
    induction h with
    | refl => exact Reach.refl
    | @tail y z f hy hmem heq ih =>
        rcases hmem with (rfl | hmem)
        · rw [heq]
          exact Reach.trans ih (hg y)
        · exact Reach.tail ih hmem heq
  · intro h
    induction h with
    | refl => exact Reach.refl
    | tail hy hmem heq ih =>
        exact Reach.tail ih (Set.mem_insert_of_mem g hmem) heq

theorem exists_new_effector_enlarges_reach :
    ∃ (G : Set (Bool → Bool)) (g : Bool → Bool) (x z : Bool),
      Reach (insert g G) x z ∧ ¬ Reach G x z := by
  refine ⟨∅, Bool.not, false, true, ?_, ?_⟩
  · exact Reach.tail Reach.refl (Set.mem_insert _ _) rfl
  · intro h

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- An admissible path in the configuration graph `Adj`, from `x` to `y`,
recorded as its list of visited configurations. -/
structure PathBetween (Adj : X → X → Prop) (x y : X) where
  points : List X
  head_eq : points.head? = some x
  last_eq : points.getLast? = some y
  admissible : points.IsChain Adj

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- Barrier height from `x` to `y`: the infimum, over admissible paths, of the
deepest dip below `J x` along the path (dips measured in `ℝ≥0∞`, so paths
that never dip contribute `0`). Empty infimum is `⊤`: no path, infinite
barrier. -/
noncomputable def barrier (Adj : X → X → Prop) (J : X → ℝ) (x y : X) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨅ γ : PathBetween Adj x y, ⨆ z ∈ γ.points, ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- A positive barrier means every admissible route to `y` contains a
configuration strictly worse than the start. No hypothesis here says that `y`
is better than the start or that the dip occurs before first reaching `y`. -/
theorem exists_dip_of_barrier_pos {Adj : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ} {x y : X}
    (h : 0 < barrier Adj J x y) (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) :
    ∃ z ∈ γ.points, J z < J x := by
  have hγ : 0 < ⨆ z ∈ γ.points, ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z) :=
    lt_of_lt_of_le h (iInf_le _ γ)
  obtain ⟨z, hz⟩ := lt_iSup_iff.mp hγ
  obtain ⟨hmem, hpos⟩ := lt_iSup_iff.mp hz
  refine ⟨z, hmem, ?_⟩
  have := ENNReal.ofReal_pos.mp hpos
  linarith

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- No admissible path at all makes the barrier infinite. -/
theorem barrier_eq_top_of_no_path {Adj : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ} {x y : X}
    (h : IsEmpty (PathBetween Adj x y)) :
    barrier Adj J x y = ⊤ :=
  iInf_of_empty _

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- If some admissible path never dips below the start value, the barrier
vanishes: the converse companion to `exists_dip_of_barrier_pos`. (Also
proved independently by Harmonic's Aristotle prover; see `aristotle/`.) -/
theorem barrier_eq_zero_of_monotone_path {Adj : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ}
    {x y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) (hγ : ∀ z ∈ γ.points, J x ≤ J z) :
    barrier Adj J x y = 0 := by
  refine le_antisymm ?_ zero_le
  refine le_trans (iInf_le _ γ) ?_
  refine iSup_le fun z => iSup_le fun hz => ?_
  simp [ENNReal.ofReal_eq_zero, hγ z hz]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- The barrier sits strictly below `d` exactly when some admissible path
keeps every dip strictly below `d`. The backward direction uses the
finiteness of a path's point list: finitely many quantities each below `d`
have supremum below `d`. -/
theorem barrier_lt_iff {Adj : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ} {x y : X}
    {d : ℝ≥0∞} :
    barrier Adj J x y < d ↔
      ∃ γ : PathBetween Adj x y, ∀ z ∈ γ.points,
        ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z) < d := by
  constructor
  · intro h
    obtain ⟨γ, hγ⟩ := iInf_lt_iff.1 h
    exact ⟨γ, fun z hz =>
      lt_of_le_of_lt (le_biSup (fun z => ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z)) hz) hγ⟩
  · rintro ⟨γ, hγ⟩
    have hx : x ∈ γ.points := List.mem_of_mem_head? γ.head_eq
    have hd : 0 < d := lt_of_le_of_lt zero_le (hγ x hx)
    exact lt_of_le_of_lt (iInf_le _ γ) (biSup_list_lt _ hd γ.points hγ)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- Reachability form: shortcuts backed by mere admissible paths do not
change what is reachable. -/
theorem pathBetween_shortcut_nonempty_iff {Adj S : X → X → Prop}
    (hback : ∀ u w, S u w → Nonempty (PathBetween Adj u w)) (x y : X) :
    Nonempty (PathBetween (fun a b => Adj a b ∨ S a b) x y) ↔
      Nonempty (PathBetween Adj x y) := by
  constructor
  · rintro ⟨γ⟩
    have hback' : ∀ u w, S u w → ∃ δ : PathBetween Adj u w,
        ∀ z ∈ δ.points, min ((fun _ : X => (0 : ℝ)) u) ((fun _ : X => (0 : ℝ)) w)
          ≤ (fun _ : X => (0 : ℝ)) z := by
      intro u w h
      obtain ⟨δ⟩ := hback u w h
      exact ⟨δ, fun _ _ => by simp⟩
    obtain ⟨γ', -⟩ := exists_splice hback' γ
    exact ⟨γ'⟩
  · rintro ⟨γ⟩
    exact ⟨γ.inl⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Ruggedness.lean
/-- Sufficient barrier certificate: shortcuts whose witnesses never dip below
the lower of their endpoints leave every barrier height unchanged. The theorem
does not claim that this certificate is necessary. -/
theorem barrier_shortcut_eq {Adj S : X → X → Prop} {J : X → ℝ}
    (hback : ∀ u w, S u w → ∃ γ : PathBetween Adj u w,
      ∀ z ∈ γ.points, min (J u) (J w) ≤ J z) (x y : X) :
    barrier (fun a b => Adj a b ∨ S a b) J x y = barrier Adj J x y := by
  refine le_antisymm (le_iInf fun γ => ?_) (le_iInf fun γ => ?_)
  · exact iInf_le_of_le γ.inl le_rfl
  · obtain ⟨γ', hγ'⟩ := exists_splice hback γ
    refine iInf_le_of_le γ' (iSup_le fun z => iSup_le fun hz => ?_)
    obtain ⟨p, hp, hple⟩ := hγ' z hz
    have key : ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z) ≤ ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J p) :=
      ENNReal.ofReal_le_ofReal (by linarith)
    exact key.trans (le_biSup (fun p => ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J p)) hp)

/-- A complete signal trajectory on time domain `T`. -/
abbrev Trajectory (T : Type u) (W : Type v) := T → W

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- A behavior is the set of trajectories admitted by a system model. -/
abbrev Behavior (T : Type u) (W : Type v) := Set (Trajectory T W)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Apply a signal map pointwise to a trajectory. -/
def mapTrajectory {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {V : Type w}
    (f : W → V) (x : Trajectory T W) : Trajectory T V :=
  f ∘ x

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Hide or relabel signal coordinates by taking the direct image of a
behavior under the pointwise signal map. -/
def hide {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {V : Type w}
    (f : W → V) (B : Behavior T W) : Behavior T V :=
  mapTrajectory f '' B

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- On one already aligned signal boundary, behavioral interconnection imposes
both component constraints. -/
def interconnect {T : Type u} {W : Type v}
    (B₁ B₂ : Behavior T W) : Behavior T W :=
  B₁ ∩ B₂

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Hiding after interconnection is contained in interconnecting after hiding.
The reverse inclusion can fail because the two projected witnesses need not be
the same hidden trajectory. -/
theorem hide_interconnect_subset {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {V : Type w}
    (f : W → V) (B₁ B₂ : Behavior T W) :
    hide f (interconnect B₁ B₂) ⊆
      interconnect (hide f B₁) (hide f B₂) := by
  rintro _ ⟨x, ⟨hx₁, hx₂⟩, rfl⟩
  exact ⟨⟨x, hx₁, rfl⟩, ⟨x, hx₂, rfl⟩⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Explicit strictness witness: each component admits a different hidden Bool
trajectory with the same visible projection. The components are individually
viable, their interconnection is empty, but their visible projections have a
nonempty interconnection. -/
theorem hide_interconnect_strict_counterexample :
    let f : Bool × Bool → Bool := Prod.fst
    let x₀ : Trajectory Unit (Bool × Bool) := fun _ ↦ (false, false)
    let x₁ : Trajectory Unit (Bool × Bool) := fun _ ↦ (false, true)
    let B₀ : Behavior Unit (Bool × Bool) := {x₀}
    let B₁ : Behavior Unit (Bool × Bool) := {x₁}
    B₀.Nonempty ∧ B₁.Nonempty ∧ interconnect B₀ B₁ = ∅ ∧
      hide f (interconnect B₀ B₁) ⊂
        interconnect (hide f B₀) (hide f B₁) := by
  dsimp
  refine ⟨Set.singleton_nonempty _, Set.singleton_nonempty _, ?_⟩
  have hEmpty :
      interconnect ({fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, false)} :
        Behavior Unit (Bool × Bool)) {fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, true)} = ∅ := by
    apply Set.eq_empty_iff_forall_notMem.mpr
    rintro z ⟨hz₀, hz₁⟩
    rw [Set.mem_singleton_iff] at hz₀ hz₁
    have hEq : (fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, false)) =
        fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, true) := hz₀.symm.trans hz₁
    have := congrFun hEq ()
    simp at this
  refine ⟨hEmpty, ?_⟩
  refine Set.ssubset_iff_subset_ne.mpr
    ⟨hide_interconnect_subset _ _ _, ?_⟩
  intro hEq
  have hVisible : (fun _ : Unit ↦ false) ∈
      interconnect
        (hide Prod.fst ({fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, false)} :
          Behavior Unit (Bool × Bool)))
        (hide Prod.fst ({fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, true)} :
          Behavior Unit (Bool × Bool))) := by
    exact ⟨⟨_, rfl, rfl⟩, ⟨_, rfl, rfl⟩⟩
  have hImpossible : (fun _ : Unit ↦ false) ∈
      hide Prod.fst
        (interconnect ({fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, false)} :
          Behavior Unit (Bool × Bool)) {fun _ : Unit ↦ (false, true)}) := by
    rw [hEq]
    exact hVisible
  rw [hEmpty] at hImpossible
  rcases hImpossible with ⟨_, hmem, _⟩
  exact hmem

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Exact relabeling by an equivalence loses no behavior. -/
theorem hide_equiv_symm {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {V : Type w}
    (e : W ≃ V) (B : Behavior T W) :
    hide e.symm (hide e B) = B := by
  ext x
  constructor
  · rintro ⟨_, ⟨z, hz, rfl⟩, hzx⟩
    have hzx' : z = x := by
      simpa [mapTrajectory, Function.comp_def] using hzx
    simpa [← hzx'] using hz
  · intro hx
    refine ⟨mapTrajectory e x, ⟨x, hx, rfl⟩, ?_⟩
    funext t
    simp [mapTrajectory, Function.comp_def]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- A temporal safety predicate holds at every time on every admitted
trajectory. -/
def IsSafe {T : Type u} {W : Type v}
    (B : Behavior T W) (Safe : W → Prop) : Prop :=
  ∀ x ∈ B, ∀ t, Safe (x t)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- A behavior is viable when it admits at least one trajectory. -/
def IsViable {T : Type u} {W : Type v} (B : Behavior T W) : Prop :=
  B.Nonempty

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Universal safety is vacuous on the empty behavior. -/
theorem empty_behavior_safe {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {Safe : W → Prop} :
    IsSafe (∅ : Behavior T W) Safe := by
  simp [IsSafe]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- The empty behavior is not viable. -/
theorem empty_behavior_not_viable {T : Type u} {W : Type v} :
    ¬ IsViable (∅ : Behavior T W) := by
  simp [IsViable]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Refining a behavior preserves every universal safety property. -/
theorem isSafe_mono {T : Type u} {W : Type v}
    {B B' : Behavior T W} {Safe : W → Prop}
    (hsub : B' ⊆ B) (h : IsSafe B Safe) : IsSafe B' Safe := by
  intro x hx t
  exact h x (hsub hx) t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Direct-image hiding has an exact safety semantics: a visible predicate
holds on every projected trajectory exactly when its pullback holds on every
original trajectory. This says nothing about predicates on discarded signal
coordinates. -/
theorem isSafe_hide_iff {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {V : Type w}
    (f : W → V) (B : Behavior T W) (SafeV : V → Prop) :
    IsSafe (hide f B) SafeV ↔ IsSafe B (SafeV ∘ f) := by
  constructor
  · intro h x hx t
    simpa [mapTrajectory, Function.comp_def] using
      h (mapTrajectory f x) ⟨x, hx, rfl⟩ t
  · rintro h _ ⟨x, hx, rfl⟩ t
    simpa [mapTrajectory, Function.comp_def] using h x hx t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Hiding preserves a safety property only when the signal map carries the
declared internal safe set into the external one. -/
theorem isSafe_hide {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {V : Type w}
    {B : Behavior T W} {SafeW : W → Prop} {SafeV : V → Prop}
    (f : W → V) (hB : IsSafe B SafeW)
    (hmap : ∀ w, SafeW w → SafeV (f w)) :
    IsSafe (hide f B) SafeV := by
  rintro _ ⟨x, hx, rfl⟩ t
  exact hmap (x t) (hB x hx t)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- A discrete trajectory follows `step` at every successor time. -/
def IsRun {S : Type u} (step : S → S) (x : Trajectory ℕ S) : Prop :=
  ∀ n, x (n + 1) = step (x n)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- All runs whose initial state lies in `Init`. -/
def generatedBehavior {S : Type u} (step : S → S) (Init : Set S) :
    Behavior ℕ S :=
  {x | x 0 ∈ Init ∧ IsRun step x}

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Forward invariance gives all-time safety for every generated run. -/
theorem generatedBehavior_safe_of_invariant {S : Type u}
    {step : S → S} {Init Safe : Set S}
    (hInit : Init ⊆ Safe) (hInv : Set.MapsTo step Safe Safe) :
    IsSafe (generatedBehavior step Init) Safe := by
  rintro x ⟨hx₀, hrun⟩ n
  induction n with
  | zero => exact hInit hx₀
  | succ n ih =>
      rw [hrun n]
      exact hInv ih

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- For the behavior generated from every state in `Safe`, temporal safety is
equivalent to one-step forward invariance. -/
theorem generatedBehavior_safe_iff_invariant {S : Type u}
    (step : S → S) (Safe : Set S) :
    IsSafe (generatedBehavior step Safe) Safe ↔
      Set.MapsTo step Safe Safe := by
  constructor
  · intro h s hs
    let x : Trajectory ℕ S := fun n ↦ Nat.rec s (fun _ current ↦ step current) n
    have hrun : IsRun step x := by
      intro n
      simp [x]
    have hx : x ∈ generatedBehavior step Safe := by
      exact ⟨by simpa [x] using hs, hrun⟩
    have hs₁ := h x hx 1
    change x 1 ∈ Safe at hs₁
    simpa [x] using hs₁
  · intro hInv
    exact generatedBehavior_safe_of_invariant (by exact fun _ h ↦ h) hInv

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Restrict a local trajectory from `I` to a smaller time domain `J`. -/
def restrict {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {I J : Set T}
    (hJI : J ⊆ I) (x : I → W) : J → W :=
  fun t ↦ x ⟨t.1, hJI t.2⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Two local trajectories agree wherever their time domains overlap. -/
def Compatible {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {I J : Set T}
    (x : I → W) (y : J → W) : Prop :=
  ∀ (t : T) (htI : t ∈ I) (htJ : t ∈ J),
    x ⟨t, htI⟩ = y ⟨t, htJ⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Compatible functions on two time domains have a unique global function on
their union with the prescribed restrictions. -/
theorem existsUnique_glue {T : Type u} {W : Type v} {I J : Set T}
    (x : I → W) (y : J → W) (hxy : Compatible x y) :
    ∃! z : ↥(I ∪ J) → W,
      restrict Set.subset_union_left z = x ∧
      restrict Set.subset_union_right z = y := by
  classical
  let z : ↥(I ∪ J) → W := fun t ↦
    if htI : (t : T) ∈ I then x ⟨t, htI⟩
    else y ⟨t, t.property.resolve_left htI⟩
  have hzI : restrict Set.subset_union_left z = x := by
    funext t
    simp [restrict, z]
  have hzJ : restrict Set.subset_union_right z = y := by
    funext t
    by_cases htI : (t : T) ∈ I
    · simpa [restrict, z, htI] using hxy t htI t.property
    · simp [restrict, z, htI]
  refine ⟨z, ⟨hzI, hzJ⟩, ?_⟩
  intro z' hz'
  funext t
  by_cases htI : (t : T) ∈ I
  · have h := congrFun hz'.1 (⟨t, htI⟩ : I)
    simpa [restrict, z, htI] using h
  · have htJ : (t : T) ∈ J := t.property.resolve_left htI
    have h := congrFun hz'.2 (⟨t, htJ⟩ : J)
    simpa [restrict, z, htI] using h

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Behavioral.lean
/-- Raw compatible functions glue, but a nonempty declared set of admissible
global trajectories need not contain that glue. Thus arbitrary local/global
admissibility data do not acquire the sheaf property for free. -/
theorem admissible_glue_counterexample :
    let I : Set Bool := {false}
    let J : Set Bool := {true}
    let x : I → Bool := fun _ ↦ false

        restrict Set.subset_union_right z = y := by

  refine ⟨?_, Set.singleton_nonempty _, ?_⟩
  · intro t htI htJ
    rw [Set.mem_singleton_iff] at htI htJ
    exact Bool.noConfusion (htI.symm.trans htJ)
  · rintro ⟨z, hz, ⟨_, hzJ⟩⟩
    subst z
    have h := congrFun hzJ (⟨true, by simp⟩ : (↑({true} : Set Bool)))
    simp [restrict] at h

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Drive deterministic dynamics with a finite input word, applying its
leftmost input first. -/
def run {U X : Type*} (step : U → X → X) : List U → X → X
  | [], x => x
  | u :: us, x => run step us (step u x)

/-- A conjugating equivalence commutes with every finite run. -/
theorem run_conj (e : X ≃ X') {step : U → X → X}
    {step' : U → X' → X'}
    (hstep : ∀ u x, e (step u x) = step' u (e x)) (us : List U) (x : X) :
    e (run step us x) = run step' us (e x) := by
  induction us generalizing x with
  | nil => rfl
  | cons u us ih =>
    simp only [run]
    rw [ih, hstep]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Coordinate change preserves finite-word indistinguishability in both
directions when it conjugates transitions and preserves outputs. -/
theorem indist_conj_iff (e : X ≃ X') {step : U → X → X}
    {step' : U → X' → X'} {out : X → Y} {out' : X' → Y}
    (hstep : ∀ u x, e (step u x) = step' u (e x))
    (hout : ∀ x, out x = out' (e x)) (x y : X) :
    Indist step out x y ↔ Indist step' out' (e x) (e y) := by
  constructor
  · intro h us
    rw [← run_conj e hstep, ← run_conj e hstep]
    rw [← hout, ← hout]
    exact h us
  · intro h us
    rw [hout, hout]
    rw [run_conj e hstep, run_conj e hstep]
    exact h us

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- A finite path including both endpoints. -/
structure PathBetween {X : Type u} (Adj : X → X → Prop) (x y : X) where
  points : List X
  head_eq : points.head? = some x
  last_eq : points.getLast? = some y
  admissible : points.IsChain Adj

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Relabel a graph along a state-space equivalence. -/
def Relabel {X : Type u} {Y : Type v} (e : X ≃ Y)
    (Adj : X → X → Prop) : Y → Y → Prop :=
  fun a b => Adj (e.symm a) (e.symm b)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Map a path through any edge-preserving function. -/
def PathBetween.map {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {Adj : X → X → Prop} {Adj' : Y → Y → Prop}
    (f : X → Y) (hstep : ∀ {a b}, Adj a b → Adj' (f a) (f b))
    {x y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) : PathBetween Adj' (f x) (f y) where
  points := γ.points.map f
  head_eq := by rw [List.head?_map, γ.head_eq]; rfl
  last_eq := by rw [List.getLast?_map, γ.last_eq]; rfl
  admissible := (List.isChain_map f).mpr
    (γ.admissible.imp fun _ _ h => hstep h)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Transport a path through a coordinate equivalence. -/
def PathBetween.relabel {X : Type u} {Y : Type v} {Adj : X → X → Prop}
    (e : X ≃ Y) {x y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) :
    PathBetween (Relabel e Adj) (e x) (e y) :=
  γ.map e (by intro a b h; simpa [Relabel] using h)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Return a transported path to the original coordinates. -/
def PathBetween.unrelabel {X : Type u} {Y : Type v} {Adj : X → X → Prop}
    (e : X ≃ Y) {x y : X}
    (γ : PathBetween (Relabel e Adj) (e x) (e y)) : PathBetween Adj x y := by
  simpa [Relabel] using
    γ.map e.symm (by intro a b h; simpa [Relabel] using h)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- The deepest dip below the score of the starting vertex along one path. -/
noncomputable def pathDepth {X : Type u} {Adj : X → X → Prop}
    (J : X → ℝ) (x : X) {y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ z ∈ γ.points, ENNReal.ofReal (J x - J z)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- The least path depth; the empty infimum is `⊤`. -/

    (J : X → ℝ) (x y : X) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨅ γ : PathBetween Adj x y, pathDepth J x γ

/-- Relabeling a path and objective preserves that path's depth exactly. -/

    {Adj : X → X → Prop} (e : X ≃ Y) (J : X → ℝ)
    {x y : X} (γ : PathBetween Adj x y) :
    pathDepth (fun z => J (e.symm z)) (e x) (γ.relabel e) =
      pathDepth J x γ := by

    Equiv.symm_apply_apply]
  apply le_antisymm
  · refine iSup_le fun z => iSup_le fun hz => ?_

      (le_iSup_of_le a

    refine le_iSup_of_le (e a) (le_iSup_of_le ?_ ?_)
    · exact List.mem_map.mpr ⟨a, ha, rfl⟩
    · simp

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Finite reflexive-transitive reachability generated by `Adj`. -/
inductive Reach {X : Type u} (Adj : X → X → Prop) (x : X) : X → Prop
  | refl : Reach Adj x x
  | tail {y z : X} : Reach Adj x y → Adj y z → Reach Adj x z

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- A map that preserves single steps preserves finite reachability. -/
theorem map {X : Type u} {Y : Type v} {Adj : X → X → Prop}
    {Adj' : Y → Y → Prop} {f : X → Y} {x y : X}
    (hstep : ∀ {a b}, Adj a b → Adj' (f a) (f b))
    (h : Reach Adj x y) : Reach Adj' (f x) (f y) := by
  induction h with
  | refl => exact Reach.refl
  | tail hxy hyz ih => exact Reach.tail ih (hstep hyz)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Some state in `G` is reachable from `x`. -/
def ReachTo {X : Type u} (Adj : X → X → Prop) (G : Set X) (x : X) : Prop :=
  ∃ y, Reach Adj x y ∧ y ∈ G

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Every concrete step has a corresponding abstract step. -/
def ForwardSimulation {X : Type u} {Y : Type v} (f : X → Y)
    (Adj : X → X → Prop) (Adj' : Y → Y → Prop) : Prop :=
  ∀ {x y}, Adj x y → Adj' (f x) (f y)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Every abstract step offered at `f x` can be implemented from that actual
concrete representative `x`. This is stronger than requiring some member of
the fibre to implement the step. -/
def StepLifting {X : Type u} {Y : Type v} (f : X → Y)
    (Adj : X → X → Prop) (Adj' : Y → Y → Prop) : Prop :=
  ∀ (x : X) (y' : Y), Adj' (f x) y' →
    ∃ y : X, Adj x y ∧ f y = y'

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Forward simulation sends a concrete solution to the image of its concrete
goal set. -/
theorem concrete_reach_maps {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {Adj : X → X → Prop} {Adj' : Y → Y → Prop}
    {f : X → Y} {G : Set X} {x₀ : X}
    (hmap : ForwardSimulation f Adj Adj') :
    ReachTo Adj G x₀ → ReachTo Adj' (f '' G) (f x₀) := by
  rintro ⟨y, hxy, hyG⟩
  exact ⟨f y, Reach.map hmap hxy, ⟨y, hyG, rfl⟩⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Local step lifting extends to a lift of every finite abstract path, with
the endpoint lying in the required fibre. -/
theorem lift_reach {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {Adj : X → X → Prop} {Adj' : Y → Y → Prop}
    {f : X → Y} (hlift : StepLifting f Adj Adj')
    {x : X} {y' : Y} (h : Reach Adj' (f x) y') :
    ∃ y : X, Reach Adj x y ∧ f y = y' := by
  induction h with
  | refl => exact ⟨x, Reach.refl, rfl⟩
  | @tail y' z' hxy hyz ih =>
      rcases ih with ⟨y, hxy', hy⟩
      rcases hlift y z' (hy ▸ hyz) with ⟨z, hyz', hz⟩
      exact ⟨z, Reach.tail hxy' hyz', hz⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- An abstract solution descends when paths lift and abstract goal membership
reflects concrete success. -/
theorem abstract_reach_lifts {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {Adj : X → X → Prop} {Adj' : Y → Y → Prop}
    {f : X → Y} {G : Set X} {G' : Set Y} {x₀ : X}
    (hlift : StepLifting f Adj Adj')
    (hreflect : ∀ x, f x ∈ G' → x ∈ G) :
    ReachTo Adj' G' (f x₀) → ReachTo Adj G x₀ := by
  rintro ⟨y', hxy, hyG⟩
  rcases lift_reach hlift hxy with ⟨y, hxy', hy⟩
  exact ⟨y, hxy', hreflect y (hy.symm ▸ hyG)⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- The hypotheses for exact reachability transfer. -/
structure ExactAbstraction {X : Type u} {Y : Type v} (f : X → Y)
    (Adj : X → X → Prop) (Adj' : Y → Y → Prop)
    (G : Set X) (G' : Set Y) : Prop where
  mapStep : ForwardSimulation f Adj Adj'
  liftStep : StepLifting f Adj Adj'
  goalExact : ∀ x, x ∈ G ↔ f x ∈ G'

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Representation.lean
/-- Under the exact hypotheses, concrete and abstract goal reachability agree. -/
theorem ExactAbstraction.reachTo_iff {X : Type u} {Y : Type v}
    {f : X → Y} {Adj : X → X → Prop} {Adj' : Y → Y → Prop}
    {G : Set X} {G' : Set Y} (h : ExactAbstraction f Adj Adj' G G') (x : X) :
    ReachTo Adj G x ↔ ReachTo Adj' G' (f x) := by
  constructor
  · rintro ⟨y, hxy, hyG⟩
    exact ⟨f y, Reach.map h.mapStep hxy, h.goalExact y |>.mp hyG⟩
  · exact abstract_reach_lifts h.liftStep (fun y => (h.goalExact y).mpr)

    {AdjX : X → X → Prop} {AdjY : Y → Y → Prop}
    {AdjZ : Z → Z → Prop} {GX : Set X} {GY : Set Y} {GZ : Set Z}
    (h₁ : ExactAbstraction f AdjX AdjY GX GY)
    (h₂ : ExactAbstraction g AdjY AdjZ GY GZ) :

  · intro x y hxy
    exact h₂.mapStep (h₁.mapStep hxy)
  · intro x z hz

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A deterministic machine with inputs `I`, outputs `O`, and state
space `S`: a step function and an output map. -/
structure Machine (I O S : Type*) where
  step : S → I → S
  out : S → O

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Run a machine from state `s` on an input word `w`. -/
def Machine.run (M : Machine I O S) (s : S) (w : List I) : S :=
  w.foldl M.step s

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A machine is output-separated when any two states that agree on the
outputs along every input word are equal. -/
def Machine.Separated (M : Machine I O S) : Prop :=
  ∀ s t : S, (∀ w : List I, M.out (M.run s w) = M.out (M.run t w)) → s = t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- `f` tracks machine `B` inside machine `A`: the encoding intertwines
the dynamics and reproduces the outputs. This is what it means for `A`
to maintain a faithful copy of `B`. -/
structure Tracks (A : Machine I O S) (B : Machine I O T) (f : T → S) : Prop where
  step_eq : ∀ t i, f (B.step t i) = A.step (f t) i
  out_eq : ∀ t, A.out (f t) = B.out t

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- A tracking map intertwines whole runs, not just single steps. -/
theorem Tracks.run_eq {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (t : T) (w : List I) :
    f (B.run t w) = A.run (f t) w := by
  induction w generalizing t with
  | nil => rfl
  | cons i w ih =>
    simp [Machine.run]
    rw [← h.step_eq]
    exact ih (B.step t i)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Tracking an output-separated machine is injective: the tracker must
hold distinct internal states for distinct tracked states. -/
theorem Tracks.injective {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (hB : B.Separated) : Function.Injective f := by
  intro t₁ t₂ hft
  apply hB t₁ t₂
  intro w
  have eq1 : f (B.run t₁ w) = A.run (f t₁) w := h.run_eq t₁ w
  have eq2 : f (B.run t₂ w) = A.run (f t₂) w := h.run_eq t₂ w
  rw [← h.out_eq (B.run t₁ w), ← h.out_eq (B.run t₂ w), eq1, eq2, hft]

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- The pigeonhole bound: a finite machine can exactly track an
output-separated machine only if it has at least as many states. This is a
capacity bound for the stated exact tracking relation. -/
theorem card_le_of_tracks [Fintype S] [Fintype T]
    {A : Machine I O S} {B : Machine I O T} {f : T → S}
    (h : Tracks A B f) (hB : B.Separated) :
    Fintype.card T ≤ Fintype.card S := by
  exact Fintype.card_le_of_injective _ (Tracks.injective h hB)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/BoundedMachines.lean
/-- Mutual exact tracking forces cardinality parity for finite
output-separated machines. It does not by itself give an isomorphism between
their transition systems. -/
theorem card_eq_of_mutual_tracks [Fintype S] [Fintype T]

    (hf : Tracks A B f) (hg : Tracks B A g)
    (hA : A.Separated) (hB : B.Separated) :
    Fintype.card S = Fintype.card T := by
  exact le_antisymm (card_le_of_tracks hg hA) (card_le_of_tracks hf hB)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Positional.lean
/-- The fixed-width input stream for multiplying `x` and `y`. -/
def multiplicationInput (base n x y : ℕ) [NeZero base] : List (Fin base) :=

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Positional.lean
/-- The machine computes `n`-digit multiplication when its final-state output

In particular, this quantifies over the actual transition-based execution; it

  ∀ x y, x < base ^ n → y < base ^ n →
    M.output (M.run (multiplicationInput base n x y)) = x * y

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/Positional.lean
/-- If a machine computes multiplication at width `n`, fixing the second
operand to one makes final configurations for distinct first operands distinct. -/
theorem finalState_injective {base range k n : ℕ} [NeZero base]
    (M : RegisterMachine base range k) (hbase : 2 ≤ base) (hn : 0 < n)
    (hM : M.ComputesMultiplicationAt n) :
    Function.Injective (fun x : Fin (base ^ n) =>
      M.run (multiplicationInput base n x 1)) := by
  intro x y hxy
  have h1 : (1 : ℕ) < base ^ n := one_lt_pow₀ (by omega) (by omega)
  have hx := hM x 1 x.is_lt h1
  have hy := hM y 1 y.is_lt h1
  simp only [mul_one] at hx hy
  simp only at hxy
  rw [hxy] at hx
  exact Fin.ext (hx.symm.trans hy)

    (hM : M.ComputesMultiplicationAt n) :
    base ^ n ≤ range ^ k := by

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/ResourceBounds.lean
/-- A real-valued objective attains a maximum on any nonempty finite resource
envelope. This is an existence theorem, not an optimization procedure. -/

  obtain ⟨p, hp, hmax⟩ :=

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- A strict contravariant family of partial orders over a category.

The pointwise identity and composition equations record a chosen splitting.
They are the only categorical coherence claimed in this file. -/
structure SplitIndexedOrder (C : Type u) [Category.{v} C] where
  /-- The carrier of the ordered fiber over a context. -/
  Fiber : C → Type w
  /-- The partial order in each fiber. -/
  fiberOrder : (c : C) → PartialOrder (Fiber c)
  /-- Contravariant transport along a context morphism. -/
  reindex : {c d : C} → (c ⟶ d) → Fiber d → Fiber c
  /-- Reindexing along an identity is the identity. -/
  reindex_id : ∀ (c : C) (x : Fiber c), reindex (𝟙 c) x = x
  /-- Reindexing reverses categorical composition. -/
  reindex_comp : ∀ {c d e : C} (f : c ⟶ d) (g : d ⟶ e)
    (x : Fiber e), reindex (f ≫ g) x = reindex f (reindex g x)
  /-- Every reindexing map is monotone in the fiber orders. -/
  reindex_mono : ∀ {c d : C} (f : c ⟶ d) {x y : Fiber d},
    (fiberOrder d).le x y → (fiberOrder c).le (reindex f x) (reindex f y)

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Reindexing as an order homomorphism. -/
def reindexHom (P : SplitIndexedOrder C) {c d : C} (f : c ⟶ d) :
    P.Fiber d →o P.Fiber c where
  toFun := P.reindex f
  monotone' := by
    intro x y hxy
    exact P.reindex_mono f hxy

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
@[simp]
theorem reindex_id_apply (P : SplitIndexedOrder C) (c : C) (x : P.Fiber c) :
    P.reindex (𝟙 c) x = x :=
  P.reindex_id c x

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
@[simp]
theorem reindex_comp_apply (P : SplitIndexedOrder C) {c d e : C}
    (f : c ⟶ d) (g : d ⟶ e) (x : P.Fiber e) :
    P.reindex (f ≫ g) x = P.reindex f (P.reindex g x) :=
  P.reindex_comp f g x

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- The context-indexed partial order of all candidate performance regions
induced by a functor of performance-coordinate types. Context morphisms act
on points by `Perf.map`; candidate regions move in the opposite direction by
preimage. Realizability by an encounter or program is not part of this data. -/
def performanceRegions {C : Type u} [Category.{v} C] (Perf : C ⥤ Type w) :
    SplitIndexedOrder C where
  Fiber c := Set (Perf.obj c)
  fiberOrder _ := inferInstance
  reindex f A := Perf.map f ⁻¹' A
  reindex_id c A := by
    ext x
    simp
  reindex_comp f g A := by
    ext x
    simp
  reindex_mono := by
    intro c d f A B hAB
    exact Set.preimage_mono hAB

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Encounter data sufficient to make realized performance regions stable
under contravariant context change.

No identity or composition equation is imposed on `transport` at the level of
raw encounters. Only its observable effect on regions is stored. That exact
compatibility is enough to induce split reindexing after duplicate encounter
witnesses have been forgotten. -/
structure CompatibleEncounterRegions {C : Type u} [Category.{v} C]
    (Perf : C ⥤ Type w) where
  /-- The type of admitted encounters in each context. -/
  Encounter : C → Type e
  /-- The candidate region actually realized by an encounter. -/
  region : (c : C) → Encounter c → Set (Perf.obj c)
  /-- Contravariant transport of encounters along a context map. -/
  transport : {c d : C} → (c ⟶ d) → Encounter d → Encounter c
  /-- Transport realizes exactly the preimage of the target encounter's
  region. -/
  region_transport : ∀ {c d : C} (f : c ⟶ d) (E : Encounter d),
    region c (transport f E) = Perf.map f ⁻¹' region d E

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- A region together with the proposition that some admitted encounter
realizes it. Different encounter witnesses with the same region determine the
same element of this subtype. -/
def RealizedRegion (D : CompatibleEncounterRegions Perf) (c : C) :=
  {A : Set (Perf.obj c) // ∃ E : D.Encounter c, D.region c E = A}

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Realized regions inherit the antisymmetric subset order from their
underlying sets; encounter witnesses do not participate in equality. -/
instance instPartialOrderRealizedRegion (D : CompatibleEncounterRegions Perf)
    (c : C) : PartialOrder (D.RealizedRegion c) :=
  PartialOrder.lift Subtype.val Subtype.val_injective

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Reindex a realized region by preimage. Compatibility supplies a transported
encounter witnessing that the resulting region is realized in the source
context. -/
def reindexRealized (D : CompatibleEncounterRegions Perf) {c d : C}
    (f : c ⟶ d) (A : D.RealizedRegion d) : D.RealizedRegion c :=

    rcases A.2 with ⟨E, hE⟩
    refine ⟨D.transport f E, ?_⟩
    rw [D.region_transport, hE]⟩

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Realized regions form a split context-indexed partial order. The split laws

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Compare encounters by inclusion of their attainable regions. This is the
pullback of the fiber order along an encounter-to-region map. -/
def EncounterLE {E : Type u} {P : Type w} (region : E → Set P)
    (x y : E) : Prop :=
  region x ⊆ region y

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Region comparison pulled back to encounters is reflexive. -/
theorem encounterLE_refl {E : Type u} {P : Type w} (region : E → Set P)
    (x : E) : EncounterLE region x x :=
  Set.Subset.rfl

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Region comparison pulled back to encounters is transitive. -/
theorem encounterLE_trans {E : Type u} {P : Type w} (region : E → Set P)
    {x y z : E} (hxy : EncounterLE region x y)
    (hyz : EncounterLE region y z) : EncounterLE region x z :=
  hxy.trans hyz

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- The pulled-back comparison is antisymmetric exactly when the
encounter-to-region map is injective. Without injectivity it is only a
preorder on encounters; quotienting encounters by equal regions restores a
partial order. -/
theorem encounterLE_antisymmetric_iff_injective {E : Type u} {P : Type w}
    (region : E → Set P) :
    (∀ ⦃x y : E⦄, EncounterLE region x y →
      EncounterLE region y x → x = y) ↔ Function.Injective region := by
  constructor
  · intro h x y hxy
    apply h
    · simp [EncounterLE, hxy]
    · simp [EncounterLE, hxy]
  · intro hinj x y hxy hyx
    exact hinj (Set.Subset.antisymm hxy hyx)

theorem constant_region_not_antisymmetric :

    EncounterLE region false true ∧ EncounterLE region true false ∧

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- Preimage along a surjective point map preserves and reflects inclusion.
Bijectivity is therefore sufficient but not necessary for exact comparison
transport at the level of arbitrary candidate regions. -/
theorem surjective_preimage_subset_iff {X : Type u} {Y : Type w}
    (f : X → Y) (hf : Function.Surjective f) (A B : Set Y) :
    f ⁻¹' A ⊆ f ⁻¹' B ↔ A ⊆ B := by

    exact h hy
  · exact Set.preimage_mono

contravariant reindexing. Hence an exact relabeling neither creates nor erases
comparisons between candidate performance regions. -/
theorem equiv_preimage_subset_iff {X : Type u} {Y : Type w}
    (e : X ≃ Y) (A B : Set Y) :
    e ⁻¹' A ⊆ e ⁻¹' B ↔ A ⊆ B :=
  surjective_preimage_subset_iff e e.surjective A B

-- LeanTest/HardProblems/FibredOrder.lean
/-- The preservation direction of exact coordinate invariance. -/
theorem equiv_preimage_preserves_inclusion {X : Type u} {Y : Type w}
    (e : X ≃ Y) {A B : Set Y} (h : A ⊆ B) :
    e ⁻¹' A ⊆ e ⁻¹' B :=
  (equiv_preimage_subset_iff e A B).2 h

  toFun := reachSet G
  monotone' := by
    intro S T hST z hz
    rcases hz with ⟨x, hxS, hxz⟩
    exact ⟨x, hST hxS, hxz⟩
  le_closure' := by
    intro S x hx
    exact ⟨x, hx, .refl⟩
  idempotent' := by
    intro S

after hiding. The displayed `Bool`/`Unit` example makes this inclusion strict,
so hiding is not a meet (lattice) homomorphism. -/
theorem hide_interconnect_subset_and_strict :
    (∀ {T W V : Type} (f : W → V) (B₁ B₂ : Behavior T W),
      hide f (interconnect B₁ B₂) ⊆ interconnect (hide f B₁) (hide f B₂)) ∧

     let B₂ : Behavior Unit Bool := {w | w () = true}

       interconnect (hide (fun _ : Bool => ()) B₁)
         (hide (fun _ : Bool => ()) B₂)) := by
  constructor
  · intro T W V f B₁ B₂ v hv
    rcases hv with ⟨w, ⟨hw₁, hw₂⟩, rfl⟩
    exact ⟨⟨w, hw₁, rfl⟩, ⟨w, hw₂, rfl⟩⟩

    constructor
    · intro v hv
      rcases hv with ⟨w, ⟨hw₁, hw₂⟩, rfl⟩
      exact ⟨⟨w, hw₁, rfl⟩, ⟨w, hw₂, rfl⟩⟩

      let v : Unit → Unit := fun _ => ()

      rw [hwf] at hwt
      contradiction

    twoCocycle h' h'' - twoCocycle (h + h') h''

/-!
# Decision-bearing support and proof-model stability

This module formalizes two diagnostics used by the book.

First, decision-bearing support is a finite benchmark statement: the instances
on which two selectors can produce different measured outcomes are contained
in the instances on which some pair of candidate outcomes differs.

Second, proof costs take values in `ℝ≥0∞`. The infimum over an empty proof
type is therefore `∞`, so unprovable problems are handled without a partial
definition. A symmetric gap built from truncated subtraction avoids the
undefined informal expression `∞ - ∞`. Local cost variation and mechanism
stability radii are then well-defined even at infinite proof cost.

The open-ball radius is itself stable. Available mechanisms are defined from
admitted proofs and budgets, so a change in a selected proof label can be
distinguished from the loss of an available method.
-/

variable {Instance Candidate Outcome : Type*}

/-- An instance is decision-bearing when at least two candidates have
different measured outcomes. -/
def IsDecisionBearing (outcome : Instance → Candidate → Outcome)
    (i : Instance) : Prop :=
  ∃ a b, outcome i a ≠ outcome i b

instance instDecidableIsDecisionBearing [Fintype Candidate] [DecidableEq Outcome]
    (outcome : Instance → Candidate → Outcome) (i : Instance) :
    Decidable (IsDecisionBearing outcome i) := by
  unfold IsDecisionBearing
  infer_instance

/-- If two selectors produce different outcomes on an instance, that instance
is decision-bearing. -/
theorem isDecisionBearing_of_selector_disagreement
    (outcome : Instance → Candidate → Outcome)
    (select₁ select₂ : Instance → Candidate) (i : Instance)
    (h : outcome i (select₁ i) ≠ outcome i (select₂ i)) :
    IsDecisionBearing outcome i := by
  exact ⟨select₁ i, select₂ i, h⟩

/-- On a non-decision-bearing instance every pair of selectors receives the
same measured outcome. -/
theorem selector_outcomes_eq_of_not_isDecisionBearing
    (outcome : Instance → Candidate → Outcome)
    (select₁ select₂ : Instance → Candidate) (i : Instance)
    (h : ¬ IsDecisionBearing outcome i) :
    outcome i (select₁ i) = outcome i (select₂ i) := by
  by_contra hne
  exact h ⟨select₁ i, select₂ i, hne⟩

/-- For every finite benchmark, the number of instances on which two
selectors obtain different measured outcomes is at most the number of
decision-bearing instances. This is the precise cardinality statement behind
the decision-bearing-support diagnostic; it does not bound an arbitrary
weighted or unbounded evaluation metric. -/
theorem selector_disagreement_card_le_decisionBearing_card
    [Fintype Candidate] [DecidableEq Outcome]
    (outcome : Instance → Candidate → Outcome)
    (select₁ select₂ : Instance → Candidate)
    (benchmark : Finset Instance) :
    (benchmark.filter fun i => outcome i (select₁ i) ≠ outcome i (select₂ i)).card ≤
      (benchmark.filter fun i => IsDecisionBearing outcome i).card := by
  classical
  apply Finset.card_le_card
  intro i hi
  simp only [Finset.mem_filter] at hi ⊢
  exact ⟨hi.1, isDecisionBearing_of_selector_disagreement outcome select₁ select₂ i hi.2⟩

variable {Problem Proof Mechanism : Type*}

/-- The least cost of an admitted proof. The codomain is extended
nonnegative real numbers, and the infimum of the empty proof subtype is `∞`. -/
noncomputable def proofCost (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    (problem : Problem) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨅ proof : {proof // Proves proof problem}, cost proof.1

/-- A problem with no admitted proof has infinite proof cost. -/
theorem proofCost_eq_top_of_no_proof
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    (problem : Problem) (h : ¬ ∃ proof, Proves proof problem) :
    proofCost Proves cost problem = ⊤ := by
  letI : IsEmpty {proof // Proves proof problem} :=
    ⟨fun proof => h ⟨proof.1, proof.2⟩⟩
  rw [proofCost]
  exact iInf_of_empty (fun proof : {proof // Proves proof problem} => cost proof.1)

/-- Every admitted proof gives an upper bound on the problem's proof cost. -/
theorem proofCost_le
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    {problem : Problem} {proof : Proof} (h : Proves proof problem) :
    proofCost Proves cost problem ≤ cost proof := by
  exact iInf_le_of_le ⟨proof, h⟩ le_rfl

/-- A uniform lower bound on all admitted proofs is a lower bound on proof
cost. The statement also holds vacuously for an unprovable problem. -/
theorem le_proofCost
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    {problem : Problem} {bound : ℝ≥0∞}
    (h : ∀ proof, Proves proof problem → bound ≤ cost proof) :
    bound ≤ proofCost Proves cost problem := by
  refine le_iInf fun proof => ?_
  exact h proof.1 proof.2

/-- A proof attaining the common lower bound realizes the proof cost exactly. -/
theorem proofCost_eq_of_isMin
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    {problem : Problem} {proof : Proof} (hproof : Proves proof problem)
    (hmin : ∀ other, Proves other problem → cost proof ≤ cost other) :
    proofCost Proves cost problem = cost proof := by
  apply le_antisymm (proofCost_le Proves cost hproof)
  exact le_proofCost Proves cost hmin

/-- Finite proof cost certifies existence of an admitted proof. -/
theorem exists_proof_of_proofCost_lt_top
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    {problem : Problem} (h : proofCost Proves cost problem < ⊤) :
    ∃ proof, Proves proof problem := by
  by_contra hnone
  rw [proofCost_eq_top_of_no_proof Proves cost problem hnone] at h
  exact (lt_irrefl ⊤ h)

/-- A symmetric gap on extended nonnegative costs. Truncated subtraction makes
the gap of `∞` with itself zero while retaining an infinite gap between a
finite cost and `∞`. -/
def costGap (a b : ℝ≥0∞) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  (a - b) ⊔ (b - a)

@[simp]
theorem costGap_self (a : ℝ≥0∞) : costGap a a = 0 := by
  simp [costGap]

theorem costGap_comm (a b : ℝ≥0∞) : costGap a b = costGap b a := by
  simp [costGap, sup_comm]

/-- The extended cost gap vanishes exactly when its arguments agree. -/
@[simp]
theorem costGap_eq_zero_iff (a b : ℝ≥0∞) : costGap a b = 0 ↔ a = b := by
  constructor
  · intro h
    have hab0 : a - b = 0 := by
      apply bot_unique
      exact le_trans le_sup_left (le_of_eq h)
    have hba0 : b - a = 0 := by
      apply bot_unique
      exact le_trans le_sup_right (le_of_eq h)
    exact le_antisymm (tsub_eq_zero_iff_le.mp hab0) (tsub_eq_zero_iff_le.mp hba0)
  · rintro rfl
    exact costGap_self a

@[simp]
theorem costGap_top_top : costGap (⊤ : ℝ≥0∞) ⊤ = 0 := by
  exact costGap_self ⊤

/-- A finite extended cost has infinite gap from an infinite cost. -/
@[simp]
theorem costGap_top_of_ne_top (a : ℝ≥0∞) (ha : a ≠ ⊤) :
    costGap a ⊤ = ⊤ := by
  simp [costGap, ha]

/-- Local variation of a cost profile in a declared directed neighborhood.
The codomain and `costGap` keep this total when costs are infinite. -/
noncomputable def localVariation (costProfile : Problem → ℝ≥0∞)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (ε : ℝ≥0∞)
    (problem : Problem) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ neighbor : Problem, ⨆ (_h : distance problem neighbor ≤ ε),
    costGap (costProfile neighbor) (costProfile problem)

/-- Every neighbor's cost gap is bounded by the local variation. -/
theorem costGap_le_localVariation
    (costProfile : Problem → ℝ≥0∞)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) {ε : ℝ≥0∞}
    {problem neighbor : Problem} (h : distance problem neighbor ≤ ε) :
    costGap (costProfile neighbor) (costProfile problem) ≤
      localVariation costProfile distance ε problem := by
  exact le_iSup_of_le neighbor (le_iSup_of_le h le_rfl)

/-- Enlarging the neighborhood cannot decrease local variation. -/
theorem localVariation_mono
    (costProfile : Problem → ℝ≥0∞)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) {problem : Problem}
    {ε₁ ε₂ : ℝ≥0∞} (hε : ε₁ ≤ ε₂) :
    localVariation costProfile distance ε₁ problem ≤
      localVariation costProfile distance ε₂ problem := by
  refine iSup_le fun neighbor => iSup_le fun hneighbor => ?_
  exact costGap_le_localVariation costProfile distance (hneighbor.trans hε)

/-- If the cost profile is constant throughout a neighborhood, its local
variation is zero. -/
theorem localVariation_eq_zero_of_constant
    (costProfile : Problem → ℝ≥0∞)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (ε : ℝ≥0∞)
    (problem : Problem)
    (h : ∀ neighbor, distance problem neighbor ≤ ε →
      costProfile neighbor = costProfile problem) :
    localVariation costProfile distance ε problem = 0 := by
  apply bot_unique
  refine iSup_le fun neighbor => iSup_le fun hneighbor => ?_
  simp [h neighbor hneighbor]

/-- Conversely, zero local variation forces equality of proof costs at every
declared neighbor. -/
theorem cost_eq_of_localVariation_eq_zero
    (costProfile : Problem → ℝ≥0∞)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) {ε : ℝ≥0∞}
    {problem neighbor : Problem} (hneighbor : distance problem neighbor ≤ ε)
    (hzero : localVariation costProfile distance ε problem = 0) :
    costProfile neighbor = costProfile problem := by
  apply (costGap_eq_zero_iff _ _).mp
  apply bot_unique
  exact (costGap_le_localVariation costProfile distance hneighbor).trans (le_of_eq hzero)

inside that directed ball has an equivalent mechanism. -/

    (sameMechanism : Mechanism → Mechanism → Prop)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem)
    (r : ℝ≥0∞) : Prop :=
  ∀ neighbor, distance problem neighbor < r →
    sameMechanism (mechanism neighbor) (mechanism problem)

/-- Radius zero is always stable because no extended nonnegative distance is

    StableAtRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem s := by
  intro neighbor hneighbor
  exact hr neighbor (hneighbor.trans_le hsr)

/-- The mechanism-stability radius is the supremum of all stable radii.
The strict ball condition makes this supremum stable, as proved below. -/
noncomputable def mechanismRadius (mechanism : Problem → Mechanism)
    (sameMechanism : Mechanism → Mechanism → Prop)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem) : ℝ≥0∞ :=
  ⨆ r : ℝ≥0∞, ⨆ (_h : StableAtRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem r), r

/-- Every stable radius is bounded by the mechanism-stability radius. -/
theorem le_mechanismRadius
    (mechanism : Problem → Mechanism)
    (sameMechanism : Mechanism → Mechanism → Prop)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem)
    {r : ℝ≥0∞} (hr : StableAtRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem r) :
    r ≤ mechanismRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem := by
  exact le_iSup_of_le r (le_iSup_of_le hr le_rfl)

/-- Every point strictly below the supremum lies inside a stable ball.
No metric axioms or laws on the mechanism relation are needed. -/
theorem stableAtRadius_mechanismRadius
    (mechanism : Problem → Mechanism)
    (sameMechanism : Mechanism → Mechanism → Prop)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem) :
    StableAtRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem
      (mechanismRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem) := by
  intro neighbor hneighbor
  unfold mechanismRadius at hneighbor
  obtain ⟨r, hr⟩ := lt_iSup_iff.mp hneighbor
  obtain ⟨hstable, hlt⟩ := lt_iSup_iff.mp hr
  exact hstable neighbor hlt

/-- Stable radii form the closed initial interval ending at the radius. -/
theorem stableAtRadius_iff_le_mechanismRadius
    (mechanism : Problem → Mechanism)
    (sameMechanism : Mechanism → Mechanism → Prop)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem)
    (r : ℝ≥0∞) :
    StableAtRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem r ↔
      r ≤ mechanismRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem := by
  constructor
  · exact le_mechanismRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem
  · intro hr
    exact StableAtRadius.mono mechanism sameMechanism distance problem
      (stableAtRadius_mechanismRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem) hr

/-- Any radius strictly above the supremum is not stable. -/
theorem not_stableAtRadius_of_mechanismRadius_lt
    (mechanism : Problem → Mechanism)
    (sameMechanism : Mechanism → Mechanism → Prop)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem)
    {r : ℝ≥0∞}
    (h : mechanismRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem < r) :
    ¬ StableAtRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem r := by
  intro hr
  exact (not_le_of_gt h) (le_mechanismRadius mechanism sameMechanism distance problem hr)

/-- Weakening the mechanism-equivalence requirement cannot decrease the
stability radius. -/
theorem mechanismRadius_mono_relation
    (mechanism : Problem → Mechanism)
    (same₁ same₂ : Mechanism → Mechanism → Prop)
    (distance : Problem → Problem → ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem)
    (hrel : ∀ a b, same₁ a b → same₂ a b) :
    mechanismRadius mechanism same₁ distance problem ≤
      mechanismRadius mechanism same₂ distance problem := by
  refine iSup_le fun r => iSup_le fun hr => ?_
  apply le_mechanismRadius mechanism same₂ distance problem
  intro neighbor hneighbor
  exact hrel _ _ (hr neighbor hneighbor)

/-! ## Mechanisms available from admitted proofs -/

/-- A mechanism is available when an admitted proof of that kind fits the
budget. This definition does not require selecting a proof for every problem. -/
def availableMechanisms (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop)
    (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞) (kind : Proof → Mechanism)
    (budget : ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem) : Set Mechanism :=
  {k | ∃ p, Proves p problem ∧ cost p ≤ budget ∧ kind p = k}

theorem availableMechanisms_mono_budget
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    (kind : Proof → Mechanism) {b B : ℝ≥0∞} (hb : b ≤ B)
    (problem : Problem) :
    availableMechanisms Proves cost kind b problem ⊆
      availableMechanisms Proves cost kind B problem := by
  rintro k ⟨p, hp, hc, hk⟩
  exact ⟨p, hp, hc.trans hb, hk⟩

theorem availableMechanisms_nonempty_iff
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    (kind : Proof → Mechanism) (budget : ℝ≥0∞) (problem : Problem) :
    (availableMechanisms Proves cost kind budget problem).Nonempty ↔
      ∃ p, Proves p problem ∧ cost p ≤ budget := by
  constructor
  · rintro ⟨_, p, hp, hc, _⟩
    exact ⟨p, hp, hc⟩
  · rintro ⟨p, hp, hc⟩
    exact ⟨kind p, p, hp, hc, rfl⟩

/-- A sound proof transformation with a cost allowance transports available
mechanisms. Merely changing the selected label supplies no such witness. -/
theorem availableMechanisms_transport
    (Proves : Proof → Problem → Prop) (cost : Proof → ℝ≥0∞)
    (kind : Proof → Mechanism) (transform : Proof → Proof)
    (translateKind : Mechanism → Mechanism)
    {source target : Problem} {b B : ℝ≥0∞}
    (hsound : ∀ p, Proves p source → cost p ≤ b → Proves (transform p) target)
    (hcost : ∀ p, Proves p source → cost p ≤ b → cost (transform p) ≤ B)
    (hkind : ∀ p, kind (transform p) = translateKind (kind p)) :
    translateKind '' availableMechanisms Proves cost kind b source ⊆
      availableMechanisms Proves cost kind B target := by
  rintro _ ⟨k, ⟨p, hp, hc, hk⟩, rfl⟩
  refine ⟨transform p, hsound p hp hc, hcost p hp hc, ?_⟩
  rw [hkind, hk]

/-- Different selected labels can coexist with identical availability sets. -/
theorem selected_labels_can_differ_without_availability_change :
    let proves : Bool → Bool → Prop := fun _ _ => True
    let cost : Bool → ℝ≥0∞ := fun _ => 1
    let kind : Bool → Bool := id
    (false : Bool) ≠ true ∧
      availableMechanisms proves cost kind 1 false =
        availableMechanisms proves cost kind 1 true := by
  exact ⟨Bool.false_ne_true, rfl⟩

end ModelStability

def frozenLoss (θ : ℝ) : ℝ := |θ - 1|
def feedbackLoss (θ : ℝ) : ℝ := |θ - (1 - θ)|
def worstLoss (θ : ℝ) : ℝ := max (frozenLoss θ) (feedbackLoss θ)

theorem fitted_model_fails_after_deployment :
    frozenLoss 1 = 0 ∧ feedbackLoss 1 = 1 := by
  norm_num [frozenLoss, feedbackLoss]

theorem feedback_fixed_point : feedbackLoss (1 / 2) = 0 := by
  norm_num [feedbackLoss]

theorem worstLoss_lower_bound (θ : ℝ) : (1 : ℝ) / 3 ≤ worstLoss θ := by
  have h₁ : -(θ - 1) ≤ worstLoss θ :=
    (neg_le_abs _).trans (le_max_left _ _)
  have h₂ : θ - (1 - θ) ≤ worstLoss θ :=
    (le_abs_self _).trans (le_max_right _ _)
  linarith

theorem worstLoss_at_two_thirds : worstLoss (2 / 3) = (1 : ℝ) / 3 := by
  norm_num [worstLoss, frozenLoss, feedbackLoss]

theorem robust_region (r ℓ : ℝ) :
    (∃ θ : ℝ, 0 ≤ θ ∧ θ ≤ 1 ∧ 1 ≤ r ∧ worstLoss θ ≤ ℓ) ↔
      1 ≤ r ∧ (1 : ℝ) / 3 ≤ ℓ := by
  constructor
  · rintro ⟨θ, _, _, hr, hℓ⟩
    exact ⟨hr, (worstLoss_lower_bound θ).trans hℓ⟩
  · rintro ⟨hr, hℓ⟩
    refine ⟨2 / 3, by norm_num, by norm_num, hr, ?_⟩
    rwa [worstLoss_at_two_thirds]

theorem frozen_region (r ℓ : ℝ) :
    (∃ θ : ℝ, 0 ≤ θ ∧ θ ≤ 1 ∧ 1 ≤ r ∧ frozenLoss θ ≤ ℓ) ↔
      1 ≤ r ∧ 0 ≤ ℓ := by
  constructor
  · rintro ⟨θ, _, _, hr, hℓ⟩
    exact ⟨hr, (abs_nonneg _).trans hℓ⟩
  · rintro ⟨hr, hℓ⟩
    refine ⟨1, by norm_num, le_rfl, hr, ?_⟩
    simpa [frozenLoss] using hℓ

namespace Calibration

abbrev Hardware := ℝ≥0 × ℝ≥0 × ℝ≥0 × ℝ≥0
abbrev Simulator := ℝ≥0 × ℝ≥0 × ℝ≥0

noncomputable def translate (p : Hardware) : Simulator :=
  (p.1 / 2, p.2.2.1 / 2, p.2.2.2 * 10)
def simulatorRegion : Set Simulator := {p | 6 ≤ p.1 ∧ 10 ≤ p.2.1}
def hardwareRegion : Set Hardware := {p | 12 ≤ p.1 ∧ 10 ≤ p.2.1 ∧ 20 ≤ p.2.2.1}

theorem preimage_formula (p : Hardware) :
    p ∈ translate ⁻¹' simulatorRegion ↔ 12 ≤ p.1 ∧ 20 ≤ p.2.2.1 := by
  simp only [Set.mem_preimage, simulatorRegion, Set.mem_ofPred_eq, translate]
  constructor <;> rintro ⟨h₁, h₂⟩ <;> constructor <;> linarith

theorem preimage_strictly_exceeds_hardware :
    hardwareRegion ⊂ translate ⁻¹' simulatorRegion := by
  refine Set.ssubset_iff_subset_ne.mpr ⟨?_, ?_⟩
  · intro p hp
    exact (preimage_formula p).mpr ⟨hp.1, hp.2.2⟩
  · intro heq
    have hmem : (12, 0, 20, 0) ∈ translate ⁻¹' simulatorRegion := by
      rw [preimage_formula]
      norm_num
    rw [← heq] at hmem
    norm_num [hardwareRegion] at hmem

end Calibration

namespace Pivot

inductive Recipe where
  | substitution
  | elimination
  deriving DecidableEq

def Proves : Recipe → ℝ → Prop
  | .substitution, _ => True
  | .elimination, t => t ≠ 0

def cost : Recipe → ℝ≥0∞
  | .substitution => 1
  | .elimination => 6

theorem substitution_sound (t : ℝ) :
    t * 1 + (1 - t) = 1 ∧ (1 : ℝ) = 1 := by
  constructor <;> ring

theorem elimination_sound (t : ℝ) (ht : t ≠ 0) :
    let m := 1 / t
    let u := -m
    let v := 1 - m
    let y := v / u
    let x := (1 - y) / t
    t * x + y = 1 ∧ x = 1 := by
  dsimp
  constructor <;> field_simp <;> ring

theorem proofCost_constant (t : ℝ) : proofCost Proves cost t = 1 := by
  apply proofCost_eq_of_isMin Proves cost (proof := Recipe.substitution) trivial
  intro other _
  cases other <;> norm_num [cost]

theorem substitution_available (t : ℝ) (B : ℝ≥0∞) :
    Recipe.substitution ∈ availableMechanisms Proves cost id B t ↔ 1 ≤ B := by
  simp [availableMechanisms, Proves, cost]

  simp [availableMechanisms, Proves, cost]

    localVariation (proofCost Proves cost) distance ε t = 0 := by
  apply localVariation_eq_zero_of_constant

  rw [proofCost_constant, proofCost_constant]
