Why Problems Are Hard

The full book · فارسی

A hard problem is a system you are trying to control. The formal companion, in which the load-bearing claims below are stated precisely and machine-checked, is kept with the source rather than here.

Hardness is not a property of the problem

Multiplying two four-digit numbers is hard for a person and trivial for a pocket calculator. Nothing about the problem differs between the two cases. The method is known and taught, every digit is visible, the procedure terminates. What differs is that long multiplication requires holding several partial products and their carries at once, human working memory holds roughly four items, and a calculator's registers hold exactly as many as the task needs.

That is not a quirk of arithmetic. It is the general shape of the thing. Difficulty is never a predicate on a system by itself; it is a predicate on a system together with whoever is attached to it. Change the attachment and the same system becomes easy or impossible.

Once you take that seriously, the vocabulary has to change. There are two systems in any hard problem, not one.

The first is the thing you are trying to move. It has some internal condition, which changes over time and in response to what you do. It emits signals you can read. There is some condition you want it to be in, and some conditions you need it to avoid on the way.

The second is you. You hold an assumed picture of how the first system works, a running estimate of what condition it is currently in, a rule for correcting that estimate when the signals disagree with your prediction, a rule for deciding what to do next, and hard limits on how much you can hold in mind and how fast you can think.

Hardness lives in the relationship between the two.

Observation is not looking

The most consequential thing to get right is what observation actually is, because the intuitive picture is wrong in a way that hides most of the difficulty.

Observation is not reading facts off the world. It is running a second system alongside the first: your own working copy, fed the same inputs, producing its own prediction of what you should be seeing, and continuously corrected by the gap between prediction and actual signal. What you have is never the condition of the world. It is an estimate maintained by feedback, and its quality is a question about whether that correction loop converges.

Engineers build exactly this and call it an observer. The construction makes several things immediately clear that are muddy in ordinary talk about evidence.

Your description is a dynamical thing with its own momentum, not a snapshot. It can be well tuned or badly tuned independent of whether it is right.

The correction signal is a symptom rather than a cause. Whether the gap between prediction and observation shrinks is decided by whether the underlying condition is recoverable from the signal at all, so a persistently large error is first a structural question and only second a question of trying harder.

And there is a failure mode with no analogue in the naive picture: you can run a confidently converging loop against a system that is not the system you think it is. The estimate is then internally consistent, self-correcting, and tracking nothing. It is not detectable from inside the loop, because the gap stays small precisely when your model is explaining its own predictions. It is detected only by feeding in something the model did not anticipate, which is why deliberately trying to break your own description is a diagnostic rather than a flourish.

Where hardness actually comes from

Five places, and they behave differently enough that treating them as one thing is most of why hard problems get mishandled.

1. The signals do not determine the condition. Two genuinely different situations produce identical readings. No cleverness in the decision rule repairs this, because the information is not present. It is worth being precise about what fails: this is not inaccuracy. A very precise instrument that conflates the two cases that matter is worse than a crude one that separates them. The corollary is unwelcome in practice: any quantity you compute from measurements you already have adds nothing, because it is a function of the same information. Only a genuinely new channel changes what can be told apart.

2. Your available actions do not reach the target. Even with a perfect picture of the situation, the set of conditions you can drive the system into may not include the one you want, or may not include it without passing through something you cannot afford. Perfect knowledge of an unreachable target is still failure, and this is a different failure from the first one, with a different remedy.

3. Your picture is not the system. Every method for building an estimate assumes a shape for the thing being estimated. If the shape is wrong, the estimate diverges, and it diverges in a way that looks like noise while being nothing of the kind. No amount of tuning repairs a missing piece. This one has enough structure to deserve its own treatment, and it gets one below, because not having an adequate picture is the condition that unifies the rest of this list.

4. You cannot run the loop. This is the multiplication case. Everything above is fine, the procedure exists, and it does not fit in your memory or your available time. No structural test detects this, which is why it is so often misdiagnosed as stupidity or as insufficient effort. It is diagnosed by changing the size of the problem and watching where performance falls off.

5. The system models you back. If what you are steering holds a picture of you and behaves differently because of it, then the two systems are not separate things that happen to be connected. You are estimating something that is estimating you. This is the one that does not reduce to the others, and it is treated below.

Delay, and why speed is not the fix

There is a sixth thing that is not a separate failure but a corruption of the loop: the signals report a past condition rather than a current one.

The naive response is to correct harder, and it is exactly wrong. Correction computed against a condition the system has already left pushes in a direction that was right a while ago. Push harder on stale information and the loop oscillates.

A delay you know about and that stays constant can be handled by running your model forward across the gap, so you correct against where the system probably is now rather than where it was. But that repair leans entirely on the model being good, so it is fragile in precisely the way the model is. Delay that is unknown, or that varies, or that depends on the situation, can only be partly compensated, and always at the cost of a slower and more cautious loop. That is the honest version of the advice not to steer faster than you can see.

There is no terrain, only a missing map

The most popular picture of a hard problem is rugged ground: valleys you have to cross, local peaks you are stuck on, a landscape that punishes you for improving. It is a vivid picture and it puts the difficulty in the wrong place.

Suppose you had a complete map. Every configuration you could be in, every move available to you, and the value of each. Then the statement that every route to something better passes through something worse becomes a line item. You read the route off the map, see the dip, price it, and decide whether to pay. That costs you something. It is not hard.

Now take the map away and leave everything else exactly as it was. A searcher that can only compare where it is against its immediate neighbours stops at the first point where nothing adjacent looks better, and stays there. A searcher holding the whole map does not stop there, because the better region is visible and the route to it can be read. Nothing about the ground differed between those two cases. What differed was what could be seen.

So the ruggedness was never the problem. Two things combine to produce the difficulty. Interaction between the parts of a decision is what makes bad routes possible at all, and that is a genuine feature of how the problem is put together. Not having a map is what makes those routes binding, and that is a fact about you rather than about the problem. Difficulty is the product of the two, which is exactly why talk of rugged terrain always behaved like neither a property of the world nor a property of the observer. It was the name of the product.

One honest limit on that argument. Holding the map does not make the search cheap if the space is astronomically large, so the precise claim is narrower than it first sounds: this kind of difficulty is a shortage of information rather than a shortage of computing.

Not having a map covers four situations, and they are worth separating because the remedies have nothing in common.

1. You know what varies and what moves you have, but not what the configurations are worth. The remedy is to measure.

2. You can see accurately where you are and nothing further. This is the situation that produces the experience of being stuck, and it is the one people describe as rugged terrain. The remedy is either an experiment that buys information about somewhere you are not, or a coarser map that lets you reason about whole regions instead of points.

3. You do not know what counts as one move. This is why declarations of impossibility are so often artifacts. Widen what counts as a single step and the barriers can only get lower, never higher, so an impossibility that depends on the step size was a statement about your bookkeeping.

4. You do not know what the configurations are made of. The space you are searching is the wrong space. No measurement inside it helps, because the distinction that matters cannot be expressed in it at all.

The first three are gaps in a map whose frame is correct. The fourth is a wrong frame, and it is the only one of the four where the answer is to draw a new map rather than to fill in the one you have.

This is the common factor under everything above. Each source of difficulty is a different way of lacking a usable map, which is why the responses divide so cleanly. A known map and room to run it means you simply traverse. A known map and no room to run it means you need a cheaper map. No map, and the ability to build one, means you build it. No map, and no ability to build one, is your ceiling rather than the problem's.

The next two sections are about the middle case, because it is the one that is most often mistaken for the others.

Re-describing the problem is free, and that is the point

There is a permanent temptation to believe that the right reframing dissolves a hard problem. It does something, and it is worth being exact about what.

Relabelling the parts of a situation, choosing better notation, finding the abstraction that makes the structure obvious, can make an intractable calculation tractable. It cannot make anything possible that was impossible. If two situations produced identical readings before, they still do after you rename everything, because renaming does not add a channel. If the target was out of reach, it stays out of reach, because renaming does not add an action. And if the terrain had many local traps, renaming preserves every one of them, since the trap structure of a landscape survives any smooth change of coordinates.

So re-description is the free move. It buys speed and clarity and never possibility. What actually changes what is possible is extending yourself: adding a channel you did not have, admitting an action you were not taking, or enlarging your picture to include something you had been treating as background. None of those is a re-description. Each one produces a genuinely different setup, which is why the claim that solving the new problem solves the old one needs an argument rather than an assurance.

Which raises an obvious objection. If re-description changes nothing, why does abstraction so plainly pay?

Why abstraction pays even though it changes nothing

The answer is the most useful borrowed idea in this whole framework, and it comes from route planning.

A satellite navigation system does not search the road network when you ask it for a route across a continent. Searching street by street would take far too long. Instead it has done expensive work in advance: it repeatedly removes intersections from the map, and every time removing one would destroy a route that passed through it, it inserts a shortcut standing for that route. The result is a stack of increasingly coarse maps. A query then climbs from your street to the coarse level, crosses at altitude, and descends to your destination, and it returns in milliseconds.

The thing that makes this legitimate rather than merely fast is easy to miss and is the whole point. Every shortcut is backed by a real path, and it reports that path's true cost. The coarse map is not an approximation you hope is close. It is exact about the question you asked and silent about everything else. So the hierarchy never invents a route. It makes findable a route that was already there.

That is precisely the distinction the previous section needed. Abstraction does not enlarge what is possible, and it was never supposed to. It converts a search that is too large to perform into one you can perform, which addresses the fourth source of hardness rather than the first two. A good abstraction is a cheaper representation of the same terrain.

Two consequences of the design carry over intact.

The cost is front-loaded. Building the hierarchy is enormously more expensive than querying it, and the two are separated in time. So a field's decades of apparently unmotivated theory-building are not a detour from solving its hard problems; they are the preprocessing that makes solving them possible in finite time. A breakthrough that seems sudden usually looks that way because only the query got counted.

And the descent matters as much as the ascent. Going up a level is easy and going up is not the achievement. You have to come back down to the question you were actually asked, and that return trip is the part that has to be earned.

Fermat, read as a query against a hierarchy

Fermat's Last Theorem is the cleanest illustration available, and it is worth being careful about what the story shows.

The implication that eventually settled it is a mathematical fact that did not depend on anyone noticing it. In that sense the route was always there. What was absent was any way of finding it from where mathematicians were standing.

At street level, meaning elementary number theory in the vocabulary of the original statement, three centuries of extremely capable people searched and failed. That failure is evidence about the search, not about the truth. Every partial result they found was a local success that led nowhere, because progress measured by resemblance to the original statement points away from the actual route.

Meanwhile, and mostly for unrelated reasons, levels were being built above: algebraic number theory, then the theory of elliptic curves, then modular forms, then the deep conjectured correspondence between the last two, then the machinery of Galois representations. None of that was constructed in order to settle Fermat. It was the preprocessing.

Once those levels existed, the connection became a short path at high altitude: attach a specific curve to a hypothetical counterexample, and the correspondence rules the curve out. The distance from the statement to a proof, measured in the coarse map, is small. Measured in the original vocabulary it was never small and is not small now.

So the honest reading is not that a higher abstraction made the theorem provable. It is that a higher abstraction made the proof findable, after a century of building the altitude to find it from. The suddenness was an artifact of counting only the last step, and the descent back to the elementary statement, showing that the high-level result really does settle the original question, is exactly the part that had to be earned.

The warning implicit in this is the same soundness condition as before, and it is not hypothetical. A coarse map whose shortcuts do not stand for real paths will happily hand you a route, and you will find that nothing you did at altitude transfers back down. In the navigation case that means driving into a field. In the intellectual case it means an elegant argument about a model that answers no question anyone asked. The condition that keeps the whole method honest is that every shortcut be witnessed by something real, and in the longer version of this work that condition is stated precisely enough to have been proved.

What kind of agent you are decides which walls you can climb

Because hardness is a property of the pairing, comparing problems means comparing agents. Agents differ along a scale of what they can change about themselves. At the bottom, a system with a fixed response and no internal picture at all, like a thermostat or a calculator. Above that, one that maintains an estimate against a fixed picture. Then one that tunes the picture's parameters. Then one that adds new variables to the picture. Then one that adds new channels of observation. Then one that adds new actions. At the top, one that revises what it is aiming at.

Two things about this scale matter more than the scale itself.

It is not a ranking of competence. A calculator sits at the bottom and beats a person at long multiplication, because that problem needs nothing from any higher rung and needs registers the person does not have. Where you sit on the scale determines which structural walls you can climb, not how capable you are. Most practical difficulty is a budget problem at a low rung, not a sophistication problem.

And only two rungs touch the walls that matter. A situation where the signals do not determine the condition yields to adding a channel and to nothing else. A target outside your reach yields to adding an action and to nothing else. Every rung below can revise pictures indefinitely without moving either wall. This is the precise sense in which thinking harder is not a substitute for measuring more, and it is the most practically useful thing in this whole framework.

One consolation, and it is real. Because what you can distinguish depends on what you do, an agent with a rich repertoire of actions can sometimes separate situations that a better passive instrument cannot. Poking a system is not a weaker form of watching it. It is often the only form that works, and this is the honest core of the advice to run small experiments rather than to plan more carefully.

An agent is a machine, and that is what a calculator is

There is a cheap way to make all of this precise, and it also settles the calculator.

Treat an agent as a machine with four parts: an input it does not control, an alphabet of things it can do, a budget of memory and time, and a program. Then hardness of the pairing is one question. Is there a program, inside that budget, that drives the coupled system to the target?

The first thing this buys is that most of the ladder collapses. Maintaining an estimate, tuning the estimate's parameters, adding a variable to the picture: these are not three capacities, they are three programs, and one machine runs any of them. A system that tunes its own model is just one fixed machine whose state happens to include the parameter. What it pays for the privilege is room, since the combined state is the product of the two.

What does not collapse is the rest, and it splits into exactly three levels. There is the program, meaning anything you can compute from what you already have. There is the interface, meaning your channels and your actions, which is your physical coupling to the thing. And there is the target, meaning what counts as done.

Stated that way, the earlier claim about walls stops being an observation and becomes arithmetic. No program over the same input distinguishes situations the input does not distinguish. No program enlarges the set of states its own actions can reach: whatever your policy, you stay inside the orbit of your effectors, and a macro assembled out of moves you already had adds nothing to it, while one genuinely new move can. Channels and actions are the interface. Everything under them is program. Software does not cross a hardware wall.

Now the calculator. Its problem has nothing hidden: the operands are the input, so seeing is free. Nothing about the problem adapts to it. Its output covers the answers. Every source of hardness is switched off except the budget. That is why an estimator would be useless to it and why the bottom of the ladder wins: a picture of the world is what a machine grows when its input stops being the whole story, and for a calculator the input is the whole story. The ladder was never a ranking of competence, because it is indexed by what is hidden, and nothing is hidden from a calculator.

The gap between the person and the calculator is then a matter of room, not of kind. Both run the same schoolbook procedure. The person has about four reliable registers, and the partial products do not fit in a fixed number of them; no machine with a fixed number of registers can check multiplication in general, however long you let it run. That is exactly the fourth source of hardness, the one you find by scaling the problem and watching performance fall off. Hand the person paper and the wall is gone, which is a pleasant thing to notice, because the mathematical model of a machine was invented in the first place as a description of a person with a pencil.

Two more of the earlier claims turn into counting. The stuck searcher and the searcher with the map are the same landscape presented to a machine that can see only its neighbours and to one that has been given the whole thing in advance, and there is a mature theory of exactly that gap. And the reflexive case has a floor: a machine faithfully tracking another machine needs at least as many states as the one it is tracking, so two of them tracking each other must be the same size. Something strictly smaller than you cannot hold a model of you. Steering it is ordinary work with a complicated object. Steering an equal is the other thing, and the difference is a count of states rather than a matter of attitude.

None of this hands you a method. It sharpens where the walls are and prices what each rung of the ladder costs, and it says nothing at all about what you should be aiming at.

When the thing you are steering is steering back

The hardest case is not a harder version of the others. It is the case where what you are trying to control holds a model of you.

Then identification stops being measurement of something fixed. Every action you take does three things rather than two: it moves the system, it informs you, and it informs the system about you. The third is not a side effect. It is the channel through which the other side updates its picture of what you are doing, which means probing teaches your counterpart what you are probing for.

The standard repair is to widen your own picture to include the other side's picture of you. That works, and it does not terminate. The widened description contains a representation of you, which you now have to estimate, and the other side is now modelling the widened you. Each round is perfectly well defined and each one adds something you cannot see.

What decides the difficulty is not how sophisticated either side is but the difference between them. Something below you on the scale cannot represent you, and steering it is ordinary work with a complicated object. Something at or above your level can, and then you are in the reflexive case whether or not you intended to be. This is why steering people is not a harder version of steering machinery but a different activity, and it is a claim about representational capacity rather than about anything else.

It also predicts a specific and common error. An agent that models its counterpart as simpler than it is will read the counterpart's model of it as noise, and will respond by correcting harder against a signal that is not noise. The estimate then moves further from the truth at exactly the moment it feels like more effort is being applied.

The test

A problem is tractable for you when four things hold at once, and it is hard when any one of them fails. 1. The condition from the signals you have, current enough and clean enough for your estimate to converge. 2. The target you can actually take, without passing through what you cannot afford. 3. Your picture to design against. 4. And the resulting procedure fits inside your memory and your time.

None of the four substitutes for another, and each one fails with its own signature. Failure to pin down part of the condition, with a sound model, means the information is not in the signals. Divergence with sound information means the picture is wrong or the delay is mis-specified. An estimate that converges while the target stays out of reach means the problem is your available actions. And degradation that grows with problem size while everything else checks out means you have run out of room, which is why you find it by varying the size rather than by staring at the model.

Where this framing stops is worth saying, because a frame that claims everything explains nothing. Some questions are not hidden but undetermined. When the answer is not fixed by the assumptions you are working from, the difficulty is not that you cannot see the condition; it is that there is no single condition to see. That is a different situation from a bad instrument, and this whole way of thinking presupposes there is something definite there to track.
