A short philosophical and mathematical edition

Difficulty is often treated as a property of a problem. A theorem is hard, a landscape is rugged, a system is complex. Yet the same multiplication is easy for a calculator and difficult for a person without paper. The same maze is transparent with a map and obscure without one. The same target is reachable through one set of actions and impossible through another.

The object has not changed in these comparisons. The relation to it has.

This does not make difficulty imaginary. A locked door remains locked under a better description, and a costly path remains costly after it is found. The world constrains which representations, actions, and algorithms succeed. Difficulty is relational because a complete account names both the task and the way an agent encounters it.

Difficulty belongs to an encounter

A task has a success condition and a space of admissible attempts. An agent meets it through a representation, an interface, and a resource envelope. The representation determines which distinctions can be stated. The interface determines what can be observed and changed. The resource envelope limits time, memory, queries, communication, precision, and physical effort.

These are objective features of a relation, not reports of how difficult something feels. Two states may produce exactly the same observations. A target may lie outside the closure of the available moves. A computation may require more memory than a machine has. Each claim can be mathematically true once its observation map, move set, or machine model has been fixed.

Four modes of difficulty

Four modes account for much of what the word hard conceals. They can coexist, but they are not interchangeable.

Epistemic difficulty appears when the evidence merges possibilities that the task requires the agent to distinguish. Two states can produce the same observation history while requiring incompatible answers. Further processing cannot recover a distinction absent from that history.

Practical difficulty appears when the target is outside the closure of available transformations, or every route violates a constraint. Knowledge of an unreachable target does not create an action. A macro made from existing moves may shorten a plan without changing its reachable endpoints.

Computational difficulty appears when an acceptable answer exists and can be represented, but computing or describing it exceeds the available resources. The algorithm may be known and the input fully visible. The obstruction is then neither ignorance nor a missing concept. It is a bound on computation.

Expressive difficulty appears when the current vocabulary cannot state a distinction, invariant, action, or target needed by a successor account. This is not an unknown value of a known variable. The variable or predicate is missing from the language itself. Adding the target predicate would solve this formally, but that circular construction says nothing about how useful concepts are discovered.

Observation and model error

Some observations are direct, such as a symbol on an input tape. Others are signals from which a latent state is inferred. In the second case an agent maintains an estimate, predicts the signal expected to arrive, and revises the estimate when prediction and signal differ.

This produces two distinct failures. The signal may not contain enough information to determine the relevant state. Or the model used to interpret the signal may be wrong. An estimate can be uncertain inside a sound model, or confidently concentrated inside a misspecified one. Confidence alone does not distinguish these cases.

Terrain, map, search, and traversal

The landscape metaphor combines the represented terrain, the cost of learning or storing its map, the cost of finding a route, and the cost of traversing that route.

A complete map can remove uncertainty and improve search. It does not remove a valley. If every admissible path from one state to a better state passes through a worse one, that barrier belongs to the objective and move relation. Knowing the route turns an unknown cost into a known cost. It does not pay it.

The converse distinction matters too. A local search rule can stop at a lesser peak even when a route to a better region exists. In the same graph, the target can be reachable, every route can contain a dip, and one search rule can fail to find any route. Reachability, barrier height, and search performance are different facts.

Three changes of representation

A coordinate change is invertible. It relabels states while transporting transitions, observations, goals, and costs. When that transport is exact, reachability and barriers do not change. Better coordinates can expose structure or shorten a calculation without adding possibility.

An abstraction is usually many-to-one. It forgets distinctions so that a specified class of questions can be answered more cheaply. It may preserve reachability while losing cost, or preserve external behavior while losing internal causation. Its adequacy is always relative to the question.

An extension adds something absent from the old setup, such as a predicate, state variable, sensor, action, or axiom. It can change what is expressible, observable, or reachable because it creates a richer language or interface. Success after extension concerns a new setup unless a transfer argument connects it back to the earlier task.

Exact solution transfer needs a return path

Let φ map concrete states to abstract states. Forward mapping sends every concrete step to a valid abstract step, so every concrete path has an abstract image. Thus the absence of an abstract path implies the absence of a corresponding concrete path.

The reverse direction needs local lifting. Every abstract step offered from φ(x) needs a concrete implementation starting from that particular x, not from some convenient member of the same abstract cell. Induction then lifts a finite abstract path one step at a time.

Goals need reflection as well. If φ(x) counts as an abstract success, then x counts as a concrete success. For reachability equivalence, concrete goals also map to abstract goals. These clauses prevent an abstract planner from inventing a solution that disappears on descent.

Costs and constraints require their own preservation clauses. A shortcut may stand for a real path while hiding a deep intermediate loss. Reachability soundness alone does not preserve barrier height or safety.

Complexity needs a model

A complexity statement concerns a family of instances, an encoding and size measure, a machine and access convention, and a resource measure. Time, working space, queries, communication, samples, and precision are different resources.

Without these declarations, a large search space is not a lower bound. An exponential policy set may have a flat or separable objective. Exponentially many finite states represent only linearly many bits. A one-pass streaming lower bound need not apply when input can be reread or external storage is available.

Representation costs can also move across time. An index, proof library, or abstraction hierarchy may be expensive to construct and cheap to query. Preprocessing cost and query cost answer different questions.

The family of fixed-width multiplication tasks illustrates the narrow point. A one-pass machine that retains an operand in a finite final state needs memory growing with operand width. This is an information-retention bound for that interface. It does not make multiplication hard in standard complexity classes, and it is not a theorem about human working memory.

Reflexivity changes the state, not the rules of logic

A system is reflexive in the relevant sense when a representation of the agent enters the causal process. An action can then change the system, reveal information to the agent, and reveal information about the agent to the system.

If a transition depends on another policy and that policy follows a specified update rule, the policy can be added to the state. The resulting product may again be Markovian. This represents one declared level of adaptation, but it does not solve it. The added state component may be hidden, the product space may be too large to search, and the update rule may be wrong.

Repeated explicit augmentation can produce a rapidly growing hierarchy of models of models. That growth follows from the chosen construction. It does not prove that every strategic problem lacks a compact equilibrium, sufficient statistic, symmetry reduction, or fixed point.

State counting has the same limit. Exact tracking of an output-separated finite machine requires an injective state encoding. This is a capacity bound, not a theory of understanding. Equal state counts do not guarantee tracking, and a smaller model may preserve the quotient needed for one decision.

The minimum mathematics

Epistemic difficulty uses the equivalence relation on states induced by all admissible finite experiments. Robust choice becomes infeasible exactly when the acceptable-answer sets over one class have empty total intersection. A disjoint pair is sufficient but not necessary. Practical difficulty uses reachability in a transition system, perhaps restricted to paths inside a safe set. A barrier is the infimum, over admissible paths, of their worst drop below the starting value; that infimum need not be attained.

Computational difficulty replaces one instance with a family I_n and asks how a declared resource R(n) grows. Expressive difficulty uses the partition induced by a vocabulary. A target is classifiable at that resolution exactly when it is a union of cells.

These are different mathematical realizations of one philosophical claim. Difficulty is attributed to a task-agent relation only after the relevant representation, interface, and resource regime have been named.

What Lean establishes

The Lean companion checks consequences of explicit definitions. It verifies, among other things, observational equivalence properties, robust-action obstructions, reachability closure under pointwise derived transformations, path-barrier facts, sound shortcut conditions, finite-state memory bounds, coordinate invariance, and product-state augmentation.

Lean does not prove the philosophical thesis as a universal law. It does not show that a formal observation map matches a real instrument, that a state space contains every relevant variable, or that an objective captures the question of interest. An existence proof need not provide an algorithm. A finite counterexample proves possibility, not prevalence. A cardinality bound proves only what follows under its encoding and separation hypotheses.

Formalization can expose a missing premise, a vacuous statement, or a false converse. Verification then checks the repaired derivation. Empirical adequacy, historical interpretation, and conceptual invention remain outside that kernel check.

The thesis

Problems are hard relative to a task, a representation, an interface, and a resource envelope. Some failures come from missing information, some from missing moves, some from insufficient computation, and some from an inadequate language. Maps, abstractions, and extensions alter different parts of that relation. Their effects become clear only when possibility, search, cost, and transfer are kept distinct.
