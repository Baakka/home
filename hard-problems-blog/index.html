Hard Problems: The Short Version

Why some problems stay hard, and what to do about them. This is the plain-language edition of a longer draft; the full text, with the mathematics and machine-checked proofs expandable in place, lives at 4st.space/hard-problems.

Hardness is a profile, not a score

Some problems resist solution for years while consuming enormous effort, and the people inside them usually explain this with a single word: complexity, politics, culture, scale. The claim of this book is that "hard" is not one thing. A problem's difficulty is a profile along seven diagnostic questions, and the count is presentation rather than commitment: three of the seven turn out to be one question asked of three different descriptions of the same problem, and another is a fourth question with a clock attached. What matters is that each can be diagnosed, each fails differently, and most call for different responses. The most wasted effort on hard problems comes from treating one kind of hardness as another: optimizing what needed governing, exhorting where authority was missing, waiting out what watching cannot resolve, or gunning across a valley nobody priced.

Formulation. Is the goal coherent, feasible, and legitimately chosen? Real goals are vectors: availability, cost, safety, fairness, learning. The useful proved fact here is about conflict. When two live options reverse their ranking as reasonable people vary the weights on those objectives, that reversal certifies genuine conflict, because we also proved that under any weighting that gives no objective a negative weight, an option that is worse on every count can never outscore one that beats it across the board. Reversals therefore cannot happen between options where one simply dominates; they mark real trade-offs. And the choice of weights itself is governance, made by people with standing, borne by people with exposure. No formula derives it, and a formalism that claimed to compute legitimacy would be laundering a political choice as a theorem.

Observability. Can what you see distinguish situations that require different actions? Think of every argument that has ever ended with "the dashboard says we're fine." We proved the uncomfortable version: if two states look identical under every probing you can do, and the actions acceptable in one are all unacceptable in the other, then no reliable choice exists at all, however sophisticated the decision rule. An immediate consequence, not itself one of the theorems: metrics computed from existing measurements refine nothing, because a computed error rate cannot separate two situations your raw logs already cannot separate; only genuinely new observation channels change what can be distinguished. And some ambiguity is worse than a shortage of data. We proved that when two rival theories are each consistent with every finite batch of observations you could take, there can be two complete world histories, one per rival, that agree with the entire observation stream. That is not a gap in diligence. It is a property of the language the theories are written in.

Agency. Is the desired state reachable by moves you are actually permitted to make? Plans routinely smuggle in steps that belong to someone else. The discipline is an authority map: levers you control directly, actions of others you can only induce, and drivers, the factors nobody at the table chooses at all. A driver misfiled as a lever is a step nobody can execute. Behind this sits an old cybernetic law with a modern proof: if distinct kinds of trouble really do produce distinct outcomes under any fixed response, then a regulator cannot absorb more kinds of trouble than the number of responses it actually deploys times the number of outcomes it is willing to tolerate. The book's worked case runs the arithmetic: a team with three deployable responses and four tolerable outcomes can absorb at most twelve classes of trouble, and the incident log listed fourteen. That team is not underperforming. It is under-equipped, and the remedy is authority, coalition, or wider tolerance, not exhortation.

Latency. When will you know whether an action worked? Every intervention has an observability lag: the time before its effects become distinguishable from not having acted. We proved that demanding stronger evidence never shortens the wait, and that before the lag there simply is no evidence at the resolution you demanded, so a decision cadence faster than the lag is revision on noise. What is not a theorem is the oscillation: early revision does not by itself guarantee overcorrection, which depends on how hard you steer and how you filter what you see. The response is unglamorous: forecast the lag before intervening, and set the review calendar to the forecast rather than to the anxiety.

Ruggedness. Do all routes to something better pass through something worse first? Some terrain has valleys, and crossing them is sometimes right. Two proved facts discipline the metaphor. First, a claimed valley with a painless route around it is not a valley: if some admissible path never dips, the barrier, meaning how far below your starting point the best available route forces you to go, is zero. Second, where the parts genuinely do not interact, an objective that is just a sum over independent decisions, changed one decision at a time, there is always a dip-free route to the best configuration. So a claimed valley in a system like that is a modeling error, and the interpretation, which belongs to the complexity theorists rather than to our theorems, is that ruggedness lives in the coupling between decisions. A third fact turned out to be about power rather than geometry: where decision rights are split, a configuration can be stable under every move each party can make alone while a coordinated move improves it. Who owns the moves decides where the dead ends are.

Reversibility. What does it cost to get back to an acceptable state after an error? This is deliberately the same reachability question as agency, asked backwards from the states a mistake would leave you in, which is why it carries no separate theorem of its own. It is the coordinate that separates bold from reckless, and its response is structural: stage the commitment, keep an undo path priced and rehearsed, and treat any step that forecloses return as requiring a different order of justification.

Reflexivity. How does the system change because you are steering it? People adapt to control. Metrics get gamed. The formal move is to fold the other agents' strategies into the description of the situation itself, and we proved the enlarged description still behaves lawfully, in the precise sense that it reproduces the original system's behavior when you ignore the new coordinates. That part is a theorem; the sting is prose but earned: the new coordinates are exactly the things you cannot observe, and the enlarged description is a diagnostic, not something you can compute your way through. The sharpest fragment is the same blindness theorem from the observability question, now pointed at a summary an adaptive agent can manipulate: any decision rule acting only through a statistic inherits that statistic's blind spots, and if the statistic conflates two situations demanding incompatible responses, every rule built on it acts unacceptably somewhere. This is a mechanism, not a law; some measures survive being targeted. But where the mechanism operates, agents under measurement do not wait for conflations to occur naturally. They manufacture them. The refutable form of "our metric is intervention-stable" is an exhibit: one scenario where you intervene, the metric holds steady, and the goal still falls, ends the discussion. The response is institutional rather than clever: multiple measures, protected reporting, monitors accountable to the people being measured.

The escape that is really a re-description

Sometimes no route exists, and the correct response is not to search harder. It is to abandon the representation.

The most consequential proved fact in the book is about this move. The same start, the same target, the same standard of evaluation can carry an infinite barrier in one description of the world and no barrier in another. Unreachability is a property of the representation, not of the problem. Mathematics supplies the canonical examples, read as reconstructions rather than as claims about how the history actually unfolded: Fermat's Last Theorem did not fall to harder work in the old vocabulary, it fell when the problem was re-described into the geometry of elliptic curves, where a previously invisible property became visible and a previously nonexistent path became walkable.

But escape hatches have a soundness condition, and it is the clause practitioners omit: a re-description counts only if success in the new picture implies success in the old. A move to a friendlier world where you win proves nothing about the world you actually live in. Static program analysis has carried this obligation explicitly for fifty years; most other fields inherit the maneuver without the clause. The same condition polices abstraction generally. Compressed maps of the kind route planners build are trustworthy precisely because every shortcut is backed by a real path that is no worse than it claims; we proved that backed shortcuts change nothing about what is reachable or how deep the valleys are, and also that dropping the fine print lets a shortcut hide a valley entirely.

Changing the representation is the highest-leverage act available, and the most dangerous, because every re-description forgets something and what it forgets is unrecoverable downstream. So it deserves a contract before you attempt it: a mechanism for why the new picture should help, a depth, meaning how much loss you will tolerate on the way, a duration before reassessment, a signal that would show it working, a guardrail that terminates it, and a named party who bears the loss if the new picture is wrong.

There is no final map

Here the book makes its most philosophical claim, and part of it is literally a theorem.

Enriching your vocabulary provably pays: one new distinction can make a question answerable that no rule in the old vocabulary could answer, and one re-description can erase an infinite barrier. And enrichment provably never finishes. For any stock of decision rules you could list one by one, even an infinite list, there is a target built by flipping each rule on its own case that escapes every rule in the stock, and the construction applies verbatim to every stage of every hierarchy of such stocks. Each new map carries a blind spot of its own. The floor of this tower is concrete and famous, and also proved in the companion: whether a program halts is not decidable by any program. The classical extension, which we cite from Turing and Post rather than prove: grant machines an oracle, a hypothetical helper that answers the halting question instantly, and the halting problem for machines with that helper reappears one level up, forever.

Within the formal model, then, the diagonal facts make "no final map" a theorem rather than a resignation, and the oracle tower above them is inherited mathematics. What carries into practice is not the metaphysics; it is a discipline built for exactly this shape of non-closure. If no representation is ever final, then no analysis terminates on its own, and stopping has to be engineered.

Acting to learn

When the model of the world is the uncertain thing, a large intervention is a bad first measurement. The alternative is a probe: a small, bounded action selected because its possible outcomes would change what you do next.

That criterion is a theorem, not a taste. With a finite menu of actions and outcomes, each outcome carrying some probability, observing before acting never lowers what you can achieve, and the value of an observation is exactly zero when one action is best no matter how the observation comes out. The practical rule flips that around: fund an experiment only if its outcomes select different roadmaps. There is also a proved boundary between what analysis can settle and what only intervention can: there exist pairs of rival models that no amount of passive watching will ever separate, while a single well-chosen action separates them immediately. That boundary is a property of the situation, not of the analyst's diligence, and it is the honest core of the advice to meet complexity with probes rather than plans.

Probes carry obligations: a bounded and priced risk, consent from the people inside the experiment, a named signal, a named reviewer, and a branch for every outcome including harm. Safe-to-fail is a design property, not an adjective, and a pilot that transfers unconsented risk to a less powerful group is not safe merely because it is small.

Stopping without being finished

Wicked problems, in the classic formulation, have no stopping rule; the problem itself will never tell you that you are done. The book's answer is not to overturn that finding but to answer it: engineer the stopping rule, since nature supplies none.

The output of an analysis is a navigation contract: the goal and its unresolved trade-offs, the current best model and its known blind spots, the admissible actions and their owners, the forecast lags, the guardrails, the anticipated counter-moves, the next decision-relevant probe, and an explicit stopping condition. Analysis then ends in one of four verdicts: execute, probe, escalate to whoever actually holds the authority, or declare the target unreachable as presently posed. The order in which those verdicts are tested is itself a governance choice; a legitimacy-first ordering refuses to execute what one lacks standing to choose, even feasibly. And the fourth verdict is guarded by a proof: under any declared order, infeasibility can only be reached after the three positive findings have failed, so "nothing can be done" can never be declared by impatience while a live option still sits unchecked.

The fourth verdict is also narrower than it sounds. Infeasibility is declared relative to the present description of the situation, and the escape-hatch results name the move that remains after it: exit from that description, not from the problem.

What the mathematics is for

Every claim above marked as proved corresponds to a theorem checked by a computer down to the axioms, and in the full edition each one sits behind its paragraph, expandable in place. The point of that discipline is not grandeur. The theorems are small and exact, and their value is exactly their smallness: they pin the load-bearing steps of an argument that would otherwise run on metaphor, and metaphor is how bad interventions get authorized. "Crossing the valley" has excused unbounded harm; a barrier with stated conventions cannot. "The dashboard says we're fine" has outlived many systems; a blindness theorem says precisely when that sentence is empty.

The mathematics also knows its place. It cannot choose the weights, confer legitimacy, or turn people into controllable dials inside a model, and a formalism that pretended otherwise would be the very thing this framework warns against. There is no final theorem that resolves human difficulty. There is a discipline: do not promise a solution until you have specified the model, what it preserves, what it cannot see, and the harms your method permits. Write the contract, run the probe, respect the lag, and when the analysis stops, let it stop the episode, not the problem, because the problem, like the tower of maps, does not end.
