Why Problems Are Hard

A hard problem is a system you are trying to control. This is the short version of a longer draft; the full text, with the mathematics and machine-checked proofs expandable in place, lives at 4st.space/hard-problems.

Hardness is not a property of the problem

Multiplying two four-digit numbers is hard for a person and trivial for a pocket calculator. Nothing about the problem differs between the two cases. The method is known and taught, every digit is visible, the procedure terminates. What differs is that long multiplication requires holding several partial products and their carries at once, human working memory holds roughly four items, and a calculator's registers hold exactly as many as the task needs.

That is not a quirk of arithmetic. It is the general shape of the thing. Difficulty is never a predicate on a system by itself; it is a predicate on a system together with whoever is attached to it. Change the attachment and the same system becomes easy or impossible.

Once you take that seriously, the vocabulary has to change. There are two systems in any hard problem, not one.

The first is the thing you are trying to move. It has some internal condition, which changes over time and in response to what you do. It emits signals you can read. There is some condition you want it to be in, and some conditions you need it to avoid on the way.

The second is you. You hold an assumed picture of how the first system works, a running estimate of what condition it is currently in, a rule for correcting that estimate when the signals disagree with your prediction, a rule for deciding what to do next, and hard limits on how much you can hold in mind and how fast you can think.

Hardness lives in the relationship between the two.

Observation is not looking

The most consequential thing to get right is what observation actually is, because the intuitive picture is wrong in a way that hides most of the difficulty.

Observation is not reading facts off the world. It is running a second system alongside the first: your own working copy, fed the same inputs, producing its own prediction of what you should be seeing, and continuously corrected by the gap between prediction and actual signal. What you have is never the condition of the world. It is an estimate maintained by feedback, and its quality is a question about whether that correction loop converges.

Engineers build exactly this and call it an observer. The construction makes several things immediately clear that are muddy in ordinary talk about evidence.

Your description is a dynamical thing with its own momentum, not a snapshot. It can be well tuned or badly tuned independent of whether it is right.

The correction signal is a symptom rather than a cause. Whether the gap between prediction and observation shrinks is decided by whether the underlying condition is recoverable from the signal at all, so a persistently large error is first a structural question and only second a question of trying harder.

And there is a failure mode with no analogue in the naive picture: you can run a confidently converging loop against a system that is not the system you think it is. The estimate is then internally consistent, self-correcting, and tracking nothing. It is not detectable from inside the loop, because the gap stays small precisely when your model is explaining its own predictions. It is detected only by feeding in something the model did not anticipate, which is why deliberately trying to break your own description is a diagnostic rather than a flourish.

Where hardness actually comes from

Five places, and they behave differently enough that treating them as one thing is most of why hard problems get mishandled.

The signals do not determine the condition. Two genuinely different situations produce identical readings. No cleverness in the decision rule repairs this, because the information is not present. It is worth being precise about what fails: this is not inaccuracy. A very precise instrument that conflates the two cases that matter is worse than a crude one that separates them. The corollary is unwelcome in practice: any quantity you compute from measurements you already have adds nothing, because it is a function of the same information. Only a genuinely new channel changes what can be told apart.

Your available actions do not reach the target. Even with a perfect picture of the situation, the set of conditions you can drive the system into may not include the one you want, or may not include it without passing through something you cannot afford. Perfect knowledge of an unreachable target is still failure, and this is a different failure from the first one, with a different remedy.

Your picture is not the system. Every method for building an estimate assumes a shape for the thing being estimated. If the shape is wrong, the estimate diverges, and it diverges in a way that looks like noise while being nothing of the kind. No amount of tuning repairs a missing piece.

You cannot run the loop. This is the multiplication case. Everything above is fine, the procedure exists, and it does not fit in your memory or your available time. No structural test detects this, which is why it is so often misdiagnosed as stupidity or as insufficient effort. It is diagnosed by changing the size of the problem and watching where performance falls off.

The system models you back. If what you are steering holds a picture of you and behaves differently because of it, then the two systems are not separate things that happen to be connected. You are estimating something that is estimating you. This is the one that does not reduce to the others, and it is treated below.

Delay, and why speed is not the fix

There is a sixth thing that is not a separate failure but a corruption of the loop: the signals report a past condition rather than a current one.

The naive response is to correct harder, and it is exactly wrong. Correction computed against a condition the system has already left pushes in a direction that was right a while ago. Push harder on stale information and the loop oscillates.

A delay you know about and that stays constant can be handled by running your model forward across the gap, so you correct against where the system probably is now rather than where it was. But that repair leans entirely on the model being good, so it is fragile in precisely the way the model is. Delay that is unknown, or that varies, or that depends on the situation, can only be partly compensated, and always at the cost of a slower and more cautious loop. That is the honest version of the advice not to steer faster than you can see.

Re-describing the problem is free, and that is the point

There is a permanent temptation to believe that the right reframing dissolves a hard problem. It does something, and it is worth being exact about what.

Relabelling the parts of a situation, choosing better notation, finding the abstraction that makes the structure obvious, can make an intractable calculation tractable. It cannot make anything possible that was impossible. If two situations produced identical readings before, they still do after you rename everything, because renaming does not add a channel. If the target was out of reach, it stays out of reach, because renaming does not add an action. And if the terrain had many local traps, renaming preserves every one of them, since the trap structure of a landscape survives any smooth change of coordinates.

So re-description is the free move. It buys speed and clarity and never possibility. What actually changes what is possible is extending yourself: adding a channel you did not have, admitting an action you were not taking, or enlarging your picture to include something you had been treating as background. None of those is a re-description. Each one produces a genuinely different setup, which is why the claim that solving the new problem solves the old one needs an argument rather than an assurance.

This is also the honest account of what a mathematician does with a hard problem, and of why the successful route so often looks like a digression. Fermat's Last Theorem did not fall to harder work on the original statement. It fell when the problem was re-described into the geometry of elliptic curves, where a previously invisible structure became visible. Read carefully, that story is about a route that ran through territory no measurement aimed at the original statement would score as progress, which is why every direct assault stalled.

What kind of agent you are decides which walls you can climb

Because hardness is a property of the pairing, comparing problems means comparing agents. Agents differ along a scale of what they can change about themselves. At the bottom, a system with a fixed response and no internal picture at all, like a thermostat or a calculator. Above that, one that maintains an estimate against a fixed picture. Then one that tunes the picture's parameters. Then one that adds new variables to the picture. Then one that adds new channels of observation. Then one that adds new actions. At the top, one that revises what it is aiming at.

Two things about this scale matter more than the scale itself.

It is not a ranking of competence. A calculator sits at the bottom and beats a person at long multiplication, because that problem needs nothing from any higher rung and needs registers the person does not have. Where you sit on the scale determines which structural walls you can climb, not how capable you are. Most practical difficulty is a budget problem at a low rung, not a sophistication problem.

And only two rungs touch the walls that matter. A situation where the signals do not determine the condition yields to adding a channel and to nothing else. A target outside your reach yields to adding an action and to nothing else. Every rung below can revise pictures indefinitely without moving either wall. This is the precise sense in which thinking harder is not a substitute for measuring more, and it is the most practically useful thing in this whole framework.

One consolation, and it is real. Because what you can distinguish depends on what you do, an agent with a rich repertoire of actions can sometimes separate situations that a better passive instrument cannot. Poking a system is not a weaker form of watching it. It is often the only form that works, and this is the honest core of the advice to run small experiments rather than to plan more carefully.

When the thing you are steering is steering back

The hardest case is not a harder version of the others. It is the case where what you are trying to control holds a model of you.

Then identification stops being measurement of something fixed. Every action you take does three things rather than two: it moves the system, it informs you, and it informs the system about you. The third is not a side effect. It is the channel through which the other side updates its picture of what you are doing, which means probing teaches your counterpart what you are probing for.

The standard repair is to widen your own picture to include the other side's picture of you. That works, and it does not terminate. The widened description contains a representation of you, which you now have to estimate, and the other side is now modelling the widened you. Each round is perfectly well defined and each one adds something you cannot see.

What decides the difficulty is not how sophisticated either side is but the difference between them. Something below you on the scale cannot represent you, and steering it is ordinary work with a complicated object. Something at or above your level can, and then you are in the reflexive case whether or not you intended to be. This is why steering people is not a harder version of steering machinery but a different activity, and it is a claim about representational capacity rather than about anything else.

It also predicts a specific and common error. An agent that models its counterpart as simpler than it is will read the counterpart's model of it as noise, and will respond by correcting harder against a signal that is not noise. The estimate then moves further from the truth at exactly the moment it feels like more effort is being applied.

The test

A problem is tractable for you when four things hold at once, and it is hard when any one of them fails. The condition of the system is recoverable from the signals you have, current enough and clean enough for your estimate to converge. The target is reachable by actions you can actually take, without passing through what you cannot afford. Your picture of the system is good enough to design against. And the resulting procedure fits inside your memory and your time.

None of the four substitutes for another, and each one fails with its own signature. Failure to pin down part of the condition, with a sound model, means the information is not in the signals. Divergence with sound information means the picture is wrong or the delay is mis-specified. An estimate that converges while the target stays out of reach means the problem is your available actions. And degradation that grows with problem size while everything else checks out means you have run out of room, which is why you find it by varying the size rather than by staring at the model.

Where this framing stops is worth saying, because a frame that claims everything explains nothing. Some questions are not hidden but undetermined. When the answer is not fixed by the assumptions you are working from, the difficulty is not that you cannot see the condition; it is that there is no single condition to see. That is a different situation from a bad instrument, and this whole way of thinking presupposes there is something definite there to track.
